# From Mao to Deng: Richard Werner's Credit-Based Account of China's Rise

China's transformation after 1978 is one of the most studied events in modern economic history. Most accounts credit market reforms: decollectivized farming, special economic zones, foreign investment, and the gradual freeing of prices. The economist Richard A. Werner tells the story differently. In his view, the decisive change was not simply that China embraced markets, but that Deng Xiaoping adopted a particular system of **directing bank credit** that he had seen at work in Japan.

Werner has developed this argument most recently in a Substack series titled *Chinese Lessons*. This article lays out the framework behind it, the historical narrative he builds on that framework, and the main points on which other economists and historians would push back.

## Who Is Richard Werner?

Werner is a German-born economist with a doctorate from Oxford who spent much of the 1990s in Japan, including time as a researcher connected to the Bank of Japan. He has since held academic posts in the United Kingdom, most prominently at the University of Southampton.

He is known for three things:

1. **Coining "quantitative easing"** in the mid-1990s, when he proposed it as a remedy for Japan's post-bubble slump. His original idea centered on expanding credit to the productive economy, which differs from the bond-buying programs that later carried the name.
2. ***Princes of the Yen* (2003)**, which argues that Japan's postwar boom, its late-1980s asset bubble, and the long stagnation that followed were all driven by the Bank of Japan's quiet control over how much banks lent and to whom.
3. **Empirical work on money creation**, including a 2014 study in which he observed a bank making a loan and concluded that banks create new money when they lend rather than simply passing on existing deposits. This view of banking is now broadly accepted, and the Bank of England published a similar account the same year.

## The Core Theory: Credit Creation and Where It Goes

Werner's analysis of China rests on what he calls the **Quantity Theory of Credit**. Its central claims are:

- **Banks create most of the money supply** whenever they extend loans.
- **What matters is where that new credit flows.**
  - Credit for **productive investment** (factories, equipment, technology) raises output and generates growth without much inflation.
  - Credit for **consumption** raises demand faster than supply and tends to produce consumer price inflation.
  - Credit for **asset purchases** (property, stocks, land speculation) inflates asset prices and creates bubbles that later burst into banking crises.
- **Therefore, steering credit is the master lever of development.** A state that guides banks toward productive lending can grow quickly; one that lets credit flood into speculation courts disaster.

In Werner's telling, Japan's mechanism for doing this was **"window guidance"** (*madoguchi shidō*): the central bank informally told each commercial bank how much it could lend and roughly in which sectors. He traces this system to Japan's wartime planning of the late 1930s and 1940s, which survived into the postwar era and powered the high-growth decades from the 1950s to the 1970s.

## The Mao Years: "The Darkest Part of China's Night"

The first installment of *Chinese Lessons* focuses on the three decades before 1978. Werner argues that the rise that followed cannot be understood without first reckoning with what came before it. The post is subtitled with a provocative question about whether a government would kill 55 million of its own people, and it raises the subject of eugenics as well.

The broad historical record behind this framing includes:

- **The Great Leap Forward (1958-1962)**, during which forced collectivization, unrealistic production targets, and grain requisitioning produced a catastrophic famine. Death toll estimates vary widely depending on method and source, from roughly 15 million to over 45 million, with some estimates going higher. Werner's 55 million figure sits at the upper end of this range.
- **The Cultural Revolution (1966-1976)**, a decade of political purges and social upheaval that disrupted education, administration, and economic management. Deng himself was purged twice during this period.

From Werner's credit-centered perspective, the Mao economy was one in which resources were commanded by political campaigns rather than allocated to productive use. There was no working mechanism for channeling funds into sustainable investment, and the results ranged from stagnation to mass death.

## Deng's Rise to Power

The second installment, *The Elixir of Growth & Prosperity*, is subtitled "When China got a break: Deng Xiaoping imported the Japanese high growth formula."

Werner describes how Mao's chosen heir, **Hua Guofeng**, struggled to build enthusiasm after Mao's death in 1976. That opened space for Deng, a party veteran who had joined in the 1920s and marched on the Long March in 1934. Deng spent 1977 and much of 1978 preparing, restoring a measure of economic order after the chaos of the previous decade.

The turning point came at the **Third Plenary Session of the 11th Central Committee in December 1978**, where Deng set out the direction China would take and urged his colleagues to "emancipate the mind." Hua kept his formal titles into the early 1980s, but real authority had shifted.

Werner emphasizes a feature of Deng's leadership that sets him apart from Mao: Deng **never became Chairman, President, or Premier** after Mao's death. He exercised power from second-tier formal posts. Werner presents this modesty as a deliberate trade-off Deng was willing to make in exchange for the ability to deliver on growth.

## Importing the Japanese Formula

The question that frames Part II is how Deng was going to deliver growth. Werner's answer is that Deng looked east.

The historical backdrop supports at least the outline of this claim. In **October 1978**, two months before the Third Plenum, Deng made a high-profile visit to Japan to exchange ratification of the China-Japan Treaty of Peace and Friendship. He toured major industrial sites, including steel, automobile, and electronics plants, and famously rode the Shinkansen bullet train. Japan had gone from wartime devastation to the world's second-largest economy in roughly thirty years, and it did so with a strong state guiding private enterprise rather than a purely free market.

Werner's argument is that what China borrowed was not just technology or management techniques, but the **financial architecture**:

- A banking system dominated by **state-directed lending**.
- Central guidance over **how much credit banks extend and to which sectors**, prioritizing industry, infrastructure, and exports.
- Restraint on credit flowing into **speculation**, at least in the early decades.

Under this system, China grew at close to 10 percent a year for roughly three decades. The People's Bank of China used annual credit plans and quotas for much of the reform era, and after formally abolishing hard quotas in 1998, it continued to influence lending through a practice it explicitly called **window guidance**, the same term used in Japan.

## Why the Story Matters to Werner

For Werner, China is a confirmation of his broader thesis, not an isolated case. The same mechanism, he argues, explains:

- Japan's boom and its 1990s collapse, when credit was steered into real estate.
- South Korea's and Taiwan's rapid industrialization under similar systems.
- The recurring banking crises in economies where credit flows mainly into property.

The lesson he draws for policymakers is that **development depends less on ideology or free-market reforms than on keeping bank credit flowing into productive investment.** He also tends to favor many small, local banks focused on lending to businesses, a model he associates with Germany's regional banks.

## Points of Debate

Werner's account is distinctive, and several of its claims are contested.

**On the role of credit.** Most economists accept that banks create money when they lend, but many do not share Werner's view that the allocation of credit is *the* decisive driver of growth. Mainstream explanations of China's boom give heavy weight to other factors: agricultural reform that freed rural labor, the move of hundreds of millions of workers from farms to factories, high savings, foreign direct investment, export markets, technology transfer, and a demographic dividend.

**On the Japanese influence.** Deng's admiration for Japan's achievements is well documented, and Japanese loans and expertise did play a role in early reform. But China's reforms drew on many models and much domestic experimentation, including the household responsibility system that started in villages before being endorsed from Beijing. Historians differ on how directly Chinese financial policy was modeled on the Bank of Japan's practices.

**On the Mao era.** Some scholars argue that the Mao period, despite its catastrophes, laid groundwork that Deng's reforms built on, such as basic heavy industry, rising literacy, and improved life expectancy. This does not excuse the famine, but it complicates a clean "darkness then dawn" narrative.

**On the death toll figures.** Estimates of Great Leap Forward deaths remain disputed among demographers and historians, and figures as high as 55 million represent the upper bound rather than a consensus number.

**On the costs of the model.** Credit-directed growth has also left China with very high debt levels and a large property sector, which by Werner's own logic signals credit flowing into assets rather than production. Critics note that the system that drove the boom may also have contributed to the vulnerabilities China faces today.

## Conclusion

Richard Werner's account of China's rise is a story about money, not just markets. In his telling, Mao's China was a cautionary tale of resources commanded by politics, and Deng's genius was to recognize what had made Japan rich and to import its machinery for directing bank credit into productive investment. Whether one accepts that credit allocation was *the* decisive factor or sees it as one important element among many, the argument offers a useful lens: it asks not only *whether* a country reforms, but *where its newly created money goes*.
