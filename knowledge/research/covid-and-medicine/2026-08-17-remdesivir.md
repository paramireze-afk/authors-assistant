# The Case Against Remdesivir: How Critics Say a Failed Ebola Drug Became COVID's Standard of Care

*An examination of the arguments made by Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and other physicians, researchers, and whistleblowers who have challenged remdesivir's clinical trials, safety record, and path to becoming the first FDA-approved COVID-19 treatment.*

---

## A note on what this article is

This piece lays out the strongest version of the case against remdesivir, as argued by its most prominent critics — chief among them Robert F. Kennedy Jr., whose 2021 book *The Real Anthony Fauci* devotes substantial attention to the drug. It is not a conventional "both sides" news article. Where a claim rests on a published study, government document, or verifiable fact, that is noted. Where it rests on a critic's interpretation, inference, or allegation, that is flagged as such. The goal is to let readers understand *why* a significant and vocal segment of doctors, researchers, and commentators came to distrust remdesivir — not to adjudicate who was ultimately right.

---

## 1. A drug that had already failed once

Remdesivir did not begin its life as a COVID-19 drug. It was developed by Gilead Sciences as a broad-spectrum antiviral, originally tested against hepatitis C and respiratory syncytial virus, and then repurposed as a candidate treatment for Ebola virus disease.

In 2018–2019, remdesivir was one of four experimental therapies tested head-to-head in the PALM trial, a randomized study run in the Democratic Republic of Congo and sponsored jointly by the NIH's National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) — led by Anthony Fauci — and Congolese health authorities. The trial compared remdesivir, ZMapp, mAb114, and REGN-EB3 against each other in Ebola patients.

The results, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in November 2019, were unambiguous: remdesivir had the **worst outcomes of the four drugs tested**. Overall mortality in the remdesivir arm was 53.1% (93 of 175 patients), statistically indistinguishable from the 50–51% mortality of ZMapp, the control arm — while mAb114 and REGN-EB3 brought mortality down to roughly 34–35%. An independent data and safety monitoring board reviewed the interim results and recommended that patients stop being randomized to remdesivir and ZMapp altogether, because the other two drugs were clearly saving more lives. Remdesivir was withdrawn from Ebola treatment in the DRC.

This is the fact critics lead with, and it is well documented: a drug that had just been dropped from an active outbreak response for being the *least* effective option among its competitors — with a mortality rate matching an experimental antibody cocktail already considered obsolete — reemerged four months later as a leading candidate for a brand-new disease. Kennedy and others argue this timeline alone should have prompted more public skepticism than it did, especially given that the same institution (NIAID, under Fauci) that had just watched remdesivir underperform in Ebola was now central to promoting it for COVID-19.

## 2. The pivotal trial: ACTT-1 and the moved goalposts

The trial that put remdesivir on the map for COVID-19 was ACTT-1 (Adaptive COVID-19 Treatment Trial), a NIAID-sponsored, placebo-controlled study of hospitalized patients, with preliminary results announced by Fauci at a White House press event on April 29, 2020, and published shortly after in the *New England Journal of Medicine*.

Here is where critics — and not only critics; several mainstream clinical-trial methodologists raised the same concern in real time — focus their sharpest scrutiny. ACTT-1's **original primary endpoint** was a clinical status score at day 15, an ordinal scale that, among other things, captured whether patients lived or died. On March 22, 2020, after 72 patients had already been enrolled, the study's primary endpoint was changed to "time to recovery" — essentially, how many days it took a patient to no longer require medical care. Mortality was demoted to a secondary outcome.

On the new endpoint, remdesivir looked good: median time to recovery was reduced from 15 days to 11 days (later revised to 10 days), a statistically significant result. On the original endpoint — survival — the picture was murkier. Mortality at 14 days was 7.1% in the remdesivir group versus 11.9% in the placebo group, a difference that did *not* reach statistical significance (hazard ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.47–1.04, meaning the confidence interval crossed 1.0, the line where there could be no effect at all).

Critics — including physicians writing in real time, such as the emergency-medicine blog *PulmCrit* and the clinical review site *First10EM* — asked the obvious question: why switch the primary endpoint mid-trial, and why to a new, never-before-used composite measure rather than one of the three time-to-recovery definitions the trial's own pre-registered protocol already listed as secondary outcomes? The trial's principal investigators explained the change as a response to evolving understanding that COVID-19 was a "slower disease process" than initially modeled, and NIAID has defended the decision as scientifically justified — a rationale later echoed in NIH-funded successor trials, which argued mortality-only endpoints require impractically large sample sizes during a fast-moving pandemic. Critics accept that this rationale exists, but characterize it as after-the-fact framing that conveniently arrived once interim mortality data pointed toward a null result, and argue that switching primary endpoints after a trial begins is exactly the kind of practice that invites the appearance of result-shopping, regardless of intent.

The FDA's Emergency Use Authorization for remdesivir, granted May 1, 2020 — just two days after Fauci's press conference and before ACTT-1's full peer-reviewed results were even published — rested primarily on this trial. Critics argue the timing reflects a regulatory system moving to accommodate a predetermined outcome rather than neutrally weighing evidence, particularly because EUA law requires that "no adequate, approved, and available alternative" exist — a threshold that, as discussed below, was arguably only satisfied because hydroxychloroquine's own EUA had been revoked weeks earlier.

## 3. What the larger, later trials found

If ACTT-1 was ambiguous on survival, the trials that followed were more direct — and less favorable to remdesivir, according to critics who point to them as the more statistically powerful verdict.

The **WHO Solidarity trial**, the largest randomized trial of COVID-19 therapeutics ever conducted (over 11,000 patients across 30+ countries), reported its interim results in October 2020 and final results in 2022. The topline mortality finding: 14.5% of remdesivir patients died versus 15.6% of controls — a difference that was not statistically significant (rate ratio 0.91, 95% CI 0.82–1.02, p=0.12). Critically, Solidarity found **no mortality benefit whatsoever in ventilated patients** (rate ratio 1.13 — numerically worse), and only a modest, borderline-significant benefit in non-ventilated patients on oxygen. When the WHO Solidarity Trial Consortium combined its results with every other randomized remdesivir trial in a formal meta-analysis, the pooled mortality rate ratio was 0.91 (95% CI 0.79–1.05) — statistically indistinguishable from no effect at all.

WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus's own summary at the time stated plainly that remdesivir, along with hydroxychloroquine, lopinavir, and interferon, "appeared to have little or no effect on hospitalized COVID-19, as indicated by overall mortality, initiation of ventilation, and duration of hospital stay."

Norway's independent **NOR-Solidarity** sub-study reached a similar conclusion and, notably, its national data safety monitoring committee decided to stop enrolling patients in the remdesivir arm entirely in October 2020 — citing low overall mortality in Norwegian hospitals, concern about potential adverse effects in ventilated patients, and the drug's apparently limited benefit. France's **DisCoVeRy** trial likewise found no substantial improvement in clinical outcomes.

Critics' summary argument: the trial that established remdesivir's authorization (ACTT-1) used a time-based endpoint that showed benefit but was statistically inconclusive on survival; the far larger trials that followed, designed explicitly to test mortality, found none. Yet the drug's authorized status, and its place in treatment guidelines, was never revisited to reflect the newer, larger, less favorable evidence — which critics argue exposes an asymmetry: extraordinary regulatory speed to authorize the drug in April–May 2020, followed by regulatory inertia when stronger disconfirming evidence arrived just months later.

## 4. Kidney and liver toxicity concerns

A recurring theme in the critical literature — echoed by Kennedy and by physicians who treated COVID patients — is that remdesivir's harms were underrepresented in the trials that led to its authorization, because those trials excluded the sickest and most vulnerable patients from enrollment in the first place.

Both ACTT-1 and the earlier Gilead-sponsored trials excluded patients with significant pre-existing kidney impairment (an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 30 or 50 mL/min, depending on the trial) and patients with markedly elevated liver enzymes (AST or ALT more than five times the upper limit of normal). Critics note this is a familiar pattern in pharmaceutical trial design — excluding the patients most likely to be harmed by a drug tends to make its safety profile look better than it will in the general hospitalized population, where kidney and liver dysfunction are common, especially in severe COVID-19.

Once remdesivir moved into real-world use — where such exclusions don't apply — pharmacovigilance researchers began flagging a signal. Several independent analyses of the FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and the WHO's global pharmacovigilance database (VigiBase) found that acute kidney injury was reported disproportionately often in patients on remdesivir compared with other COVID-19 treatments:

- A 2022 study in *Frontiers in Pharmacology* analyzing nearly 4,000 FAERS reports found a reporting odds ratio (ROR) for acute kidney injury of 2.81 (rising to 3.85 after propensity-score matching) — meaning AKI was reported roughly three to four times more often with remdesivir than with comparator drugs. The same study found a 36.45% fatality rate among the AKI cases reported.
- A follow-up disproportionality analysis published in the *International Journal of Clinical Pharmacy* in 2023, covering a longer period, found a smaller but still statistically significant signal (ROR 2.00 for AKI, 2.35 for broader renal disorders).
- A Brazilian analysis published in *Cadernos de Saúde Pública* found renal and urinary adverse events made up nearly 17% of all remdesivir reports in FAERS, and that remdesivir carried more than double the reporting odds of renal adverse events compared with hydroxychloroquine, lopinavir/ritonavir, or tocilizumab.
- An earlier WHO VigiBase analysis (Gérard et al., 2021) found an even more striking reporting odds ratio of 20.30 for AKI associated with remdesivir compared to other COVID-19 drugs.

Researchers conducting these studies are careful to note the limitations of adverse-event-reporting databases: they cannot establish causation, are subject to reporting bias, and COVID-19 itself independently causes kidney injury in a large share of severe cases (some estimates put it at 20–40% of ventilated patients), making it hard to separate the drug's contribution from the disease's. The FDA itself responded to these signals by advising caution in patients with reduced kidney function and stating remdesivir isn't recommended in patients with an eGFR under 30 mL/min.

Critics, however, argue that the caution came only after the drug was already in widespread hospital use, and that the signal — consistently reproduced across multiple independent pharmacovigilance databases using different methodologies — is stronger and more consistent than regulators' relatively muted response would suggest. Kennedy, in *The Real Anthony Fauci*, frames this pattern as part of a broader argument: that adverse signals for repurposed, patentable drugs favored by NIAID and its industry partners received comparatively gentle regulatory treatment, while off-patent alternatives like hydroxychloroquine faced aggressive warnings over comparatively rarer safety concerns (see Section 6).

## 5. The financial architecture around remdesivir

This is where Kennedy's book, and critics generally, spend considerable energy — not on the chemistry of the drug, but on the financial incentives surrounding its use.

**Development cost and pricing.** Public Citizen and other watchdog groups noted that U.S. taxpayers, through NIH and Department of Defense funding, had underwritten a substantial share of remdesivir's development — public estimates at the time put direct government contributions at roughly $70 million, not counting the government-run ACTT-1 trial itself, which NIAID designed, funded, and ran. Gilead nonetheless set a U.S. commercial price of $3,120 for a typical five-day treatment course for privately insured patients ($2,340 for government purchasers), even as the nonprofit Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER) — after the discovery that the cheap, generic steroid dexamethasone reduced COVID mortality (a benefit remdesivir never demonstrated) — recalculated a "cost-effective" benchmark price of just $2,520–$2,800. Gilead's CEO defended the pricing as reasonable given projected hospital savings from shorter stays; critics counter that those projected savings rested on the time-to-recovery data whose mortality implications remained contested.

**Hospital reimbursement incentives.** Under the CARES Act, hospitals treating COVID-19 patients received enhanced Medicare reimbursement, and separately, Gilead applied for a Medicare "new technology add-on payment" specifically to offset hospitals' cost of administering remdesivir. Critics argue this created a structural incentive: hospitals had a direct financial reason to use, and to be seen using, an authorized, guideline-recommended drug, independent of how strong the mortality evidence for that drug actually was. This argument does not require asserting that individual physicians were personally corrupted — the claim is structural, about how billing and reimbursement rules shape institutional behavior at scale.

**Regulatory/industry proximity.** Kennedy's broader argument in *The Real Anthony Fauci* is that NIAID, under Fauci's decades-long leadership, functioned less as a neutral referee of competing treatments and more as a partner to the pharmaceutical industry — funding the pivotal trials of patentable drugs it had a hand in developing, then working alongside the FDA (which Kennedy portrays as substantially funded by industry user fees) to fast-track authorization, while showing comparatively little institutional enthusiasm for investigating cheap, generic, non-patentable alternatives like hydroxychloroquine or ivermectin. Independent fact-checking organizations, including FactCheck.org, have reviewed this thesis and describe it as resting on "selective evidence and insinuation rather than verified misconduct" — a characterization critics of Kennedy's book raise, and which readers should weigh alongside the documentary record above.

## 6. Regulatory sequencing: how EUA law shaped the field

One of the more technical but consequential arguments critics make involves the legal mechanics of Emergency Use Authorization itself. Under Section 564 of the Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act, the FDA can only issue an EUA for a product if there is **no adequate, approved, and available alternative**.

Hydroxychloroquine had received its own EUA for COVID-19 in late March 2020. On June 15, 2020 — six weeks after remdesivir's EUA was granted — the FDA revoked the hydroxychloroquine authorization, citing trial data (including the UK's RECOVERY trial) showing no benefit and citing cardiac safety concerns. Critics, Kennedy among them, argue the sequencing mattered: with hydroxychloroquine's authorization gone, one of the few enumerated barriers to remdesivir's EUA — the existence of an approved, available alternative — was removed as a live legal question, clearing a cleaner regulatory path for remdesivir's continued and expanded authorization over the following months (the EUA was reissued and broadened in June, July, and August 2020 before remdesivir received full FDA approval — the first COVID-19 drug to do so — on October 22, 2020).

Mainstream infectious-disease researchers point to a large and separately-replicated body of randomized trial evidence (including RECOVERY, the largest hydroxychloroquine trial) showing the drug did not reduce COVID-19 mortality and carried real cardiac risk, as the actual basis for its regulatory reversal, independent of remdesivir's status. Critics don't generally dispute that trials found hydroxychloroquine ineffective; their argument is narrower — that the *manner and speed* of hydroxychloroquine's public discrediting (including its entanglement with the retracted May 2020 Surgisphere/*Lancet* study, later withdrawn for unverifiable data) served to clear the field for a costlier, patentable competitor whose own trial evidence was, by the critics' reading, no more compelling.

## 7. NIH treatment guidelines and hospital protocols

By mid-2020, the NIH's COVID-19 Treatment Guidelines Panel — which included Fauci in a senior institutional capacity — recommended remdesivir for hospitalized patients requiring supplemental oxygen, and many hospital systems built internal protocols and pharmacy formularies around that guidance, in some cases initially restricting prescribing to infectious-disease specialists before loosening it as case volumes rose.

Physicians and nurses who have spoken out publicly (several featured in independent documentaries and interviews cited in Kennedy's book and by allied outlets like Children's Health Defense) describe hospital protocols that made remdesivir close to a default order for oxygen-requiring COVID patients, sometimes bundled with other interventions, arguing this created institutional momentum that was hard for individual clinicians to depart from — whatever their personal read of the mortality data — without running afoul of hospital committees, liability concerns, or, in critics' more pointed framing, the reimbursement incentives described above. It's worth noting these are largely firsthand accounts and anecdotal reports rather than systematic surveys, and hospitals differed considerably in how rigidly they enforced remdesivir protocols; national studies of real-world prescribing patterns during this period are limited.

## 8. Why critics say the drug stayed in use despite the doubts

Pulling the strands together, the critics' case for why remdesivir remained a guideline-recommended, widely administered drug even as Solidarity, NOR-Solidarity, and DisCoVeRy accumulated null mortality results rests on several claims, some documented, some inferential:

1. **First-mover advantage.** Remdesivir was the first — and for months, the only — FDA-authorized COVID-19 treatment, giving it an institutional and reputational head start that later, larger trials struggled to dislodge.
2. **Sunk institutional credibility.** NIAID had publicly championed the drug at a White House event; walking that back in light of Solidarity's results would have required a public reassessment that, critics argue, the agency and its allied guideline panels were reluctant to make.
3. **Financial infrastructure.** Hospital reimbursement mechanisms (the Medicare add-on payment, CARES Act provisions) were built around remdesivir's use, creating friction against de-prescribing it even as evidence weakened.
4. **Absence of competitors for a period.** Until dexamethasone's mortality benefit was confirmed (June 2020) and monoclonal antibodies and oral antivirals like Paxlovid arrived later, remdesivir had little competition as an authorized pharmaceutical option, which critics argue lowered the bar for continued use regardless of its own evidentiary strength.
5. **Guideline lag.** Clinical practice guidelines are typically revised on a periodic cycle and tend toward caution about removing an authorized therapy absent an active safety signal serious enough to force the issue — a structural tendency critics say worked in remdesivir's favor.

None of these five points, individually, requires bad faith on anyone's part — each describes an ordinary institutional or economic mechanism. Kennedy's framework goes further, arguing the pattern reflects deliberate regulatory capture by pharmaceutical interests operating through Fauci and NIAID specifically. That stronger claim is the most contested part of his book, and it is where independent fact-checkers, virologists, and public-health historians most sharply part ways with him, characterizing it as an inference the underlying facts don't clearly support rather than a documented conclusion.

## 9. What isn't in dispute

Stepping back, a few things are matters of published record rather than interpretation, and are worth stating plainly because they form the foundation critics build on:

- Remdesivir underperformed its three competitor drugs in a randomized Ebola trial and was withdrawn from that use.
- ACTT-1's primary endpoint was changed after the trial had begun enrolling patients, and its mortality result did not reach statistical significance.
- The WHO Solidarity trial, the largest randomized COVID-19 treatment trial ever run, found no statistically significant mortality benefit overall, and no benefit at all in ventilated patients.
- Multiple independent pharmacovigilance analyses, using different databases and methods, have found a consistent statistical signal for elevated acute kidney injury reporting associated with remdesivir.
- Remdesivir's U.S. list price was several times higher than independent cost-effectiveness benchmarks suggested even before its mortality benefit came into question, and hospitals received supplemental Medicare payments tied to its administration.
- The drug nonetheless became, and largely remained, the first-line authorized pharmaceutical treatment for hospitalized COVID-19 patients in U.S. guidelines through much of the pandemic.

What remains genuinely contested — between Kennedy's camp and mainstream infectious-disease researchers — is not really these underlying facts, most of which are drawn from peer-reviewed literature and government documents, but the *interpretation* laid over them: whether the pattern reflects ordinary scientific uncertainty and institutional inertia working out in real time under pandemic pressure, or a more deliberate architecture of regulatory and financial incentives that critics argue kept a marginally-effective, financially lucrative drug in wide use longer than the accumulating evidence justified.
