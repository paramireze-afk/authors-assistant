# Reading the 2020s Through Mises, Hayek, and Rothbard
### An Applied Report

---

## A Note on What This Document Is

Everything in this conversation's earlier documents — praxeology, economic calculation, time preference, sound money, fractional-reserve banking, and Austrian Business Cycle Theory — was assembled as a faithful record of how Mises, Hayek, and Rothbard understood their own concepts. This document is different in kind: it takes that inherited toolkit and points it at the actual economy of 2020–2026, using current data. That means it is **interpretive** in a way the other documents were not. Austrian economists themselves would insist (see the praxeology document, §4.2) that no set of historical statistics can definitively "prove" a theory — data can illustrate a theory's applicability, or fail to fit it cleanly, but it cannot serve as a controlled experiment. This report follows Rothbard's own practice in *America's Great Depression*: using the Austrian framework as a lens to organize and interpret the observable facts, while being honest about where the fit is clean and where it is more contested. Where mainstream or rival readings of the same data exist, I've tried to flag them rather than pretend the Austrian reading is the only one on offer.

All data below reflects the situation as of late July 2026.

---

## 1. The Starting Conditions: What an Austrian Would Look At First

Before applying any specific theory, an Austrian analyst's instinct is to ask: **what has happened to the money supply, to credit, and to interest rates over the relevant period** — because in this framework, those are the variables that set everything else in motion (see the companion ABCT and time-preference documents).

The broad arc since 2020:

- **2020–2021: the largest, fastest monetary expansion in modern US history.** In response to the COVID-19 shutdowns, the Federal Reserve cut its policy rate to the zero lower bound and undertook quantitative easing on a scale that pushed M2 money supply growth to roughly 25–27% year-over-year in 2020 — a peacetime record, exceeding even the monetary expansion of the 1970s. This was combined with historic fiscal deficits, much of which was effectively monetized.
- **2022–2023: the correction.** As price inflation, having been dismissed for much of 2021 as "transitory," proved persistent, the Fed undertook one of the fastest rate-hiking cycles in its history, moving the federal funds rate from near zero to over 5% in roughly eighteen months.
- **March 2023: a cluster of bank failures** (Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic) — the second-, third-, and fourth-largest bank failures in US history by assets, occurring within weeks of each other.
- **2024–2026: an uneasy plateau.** By July 2026, the Fed funds rate sits at 3.50–3.75%, held there for five consecutive meetings amid a genuinely divided FOMC (a 9–3 vote, with three regional presidents dissenting in favor of a hike). <cite index="6-1">Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy.</cite> Headline CPI has swung between roughly 2.4% and 4.2% over the past year, landing at 3.5% for the twelve months ending June 2026, with core inflation at 2.6%. <cite index="18-1">Meanwhile, total US gross national debt stands at $39.39 trillion</cite>, with a debt-to-GDP ratio above 120% and annual interest expense exceeding $1 trillion — now larger than the defense budget.

That is the terrain. What follows is how each layer of the Austrian framework reads it.

## 2. ABCT Applied: Was 2020–2023 a Textbook Cycle?

### 2.1 The boom phase, in Rothbardian terms

Run the 2020–2021 episode through the mechanics laid out in the companion ABCT document, and the fit is unusually clean by the standards of any real historical case (Rothbard himself, in *America's Great Depression*, had to work much harder to make his case than this episode would require):

- **Credit/money expansion, not backed by voluntary savings.** The 2020–21 M2 surge was not the product of a nationwide surge in genuine thrift — it was created directly, through Fed asset purchases and, more unusually than in Rothbard's classic cases, direct fiscal transfers (stimulus checks, enhanced unemployment benefits, PPP loans) that were themselves debt-financed and substantially monetized. In Rothbard's vocabulary, this is about as pure an instance of "circulation credit" as one could construct — the loanable-funds-and-spending equivalent showed up in the economy without any corresponding reduction in anyone's consumption.
- **The interest rate pushed below its natural level.** Zero-rate policy, sustained through 2021 even as goods-sector demand was visibly overheating, is a nearly literal instantiation of the mechanism in §5 of the companion time-preference document: a market rate deliberately held far below whatever the economy's underlying time preference actually was.
- **Malinvestment concentrated in interest-sensitive, long-horizon sectors.** The clearest candidates in this cycle: a historic run-up in housing and asset prices, a boom in speculative technology and "growth" equities, and — as discussed in §4 below — the beginnings of the AI infrastructure buildout, all sectors whose profitability is unusually sensitive to the cost of capital.
- **The "Ricardo effect" reasserting itself.** As 2021 wore on, resource and labor costs (the "everything shortage," rising wages in tight labor markets) began squeezing margins in exactly the way §5 of the ABCT document describes — the boom's own success bidding up the inputs it depended on.

### 2.2 The bust — and the crucial way it *didn't* follow the Rothbardian script

Here is where a fair-minded application of the theory has to note a real departure. Rothbard's policy prescription (see the ABCT document, §6) is that the bust should be allowed to liquidate malinvestment without interference — that "doing nothing" is the fastest route back to a sound structure of production. The actual 2023 episode did not follow this path:

- When Silicon Valley Bank and its peers failed in March 2023 — largely because they had bought long-duration Treasury and mortgage-backed securities during the zero-rate era and were caught holding underwater assets once rates rose sharply — the Fed responded within days with the **Bank Term Funding Program**, offering to lend against those same underwater securities at par value, and regulators extended deposit-insurance-like guarantees to depositors well beyond the statutory $250,000 cap.
- In Rothbardian terms, this is a nearly perfect illustration of the distinction between a **primary depression** (the necessary liquidation of a specific set of malinvestments — in this case, banks that had mismatched duration risk during an era of free money) and the **secondary depression** Mises and Rothbard worried about avoiding through indiscriminate re-liquification (see the companion ABCT document, §6.1). The 2023 intervention arguably succeeded, on its own terms, in preventing a secondary, contagious banking panic — but a stricter Rothbardian reading would say it did so precisely by blunting the corrective signal the bust was supposed to send, papering over the specific error (duration mismatch encouraged by years of suppressed rates) rather than letting it be liquidated and repriced.
- This is also a live illustration of the **fractional-reserve fragility** argument from the companion FRB document: SVB's failure was, at root, a bank run — a demand-deposit base that expected full, instant availability, sitting atop a longer-duration, less-liquid asset base. It is not the classic 19th-century fractional-reserve panic Rothbard chronicles in *A History of Money and Banking in the United States*, but the underlying structural vulnerability (short-term, callable liabilities funding longer-term, revaluation-sensitive assets) is a direct descendant of it.

### 2.3 An honest complication: was this actually a "cluster of errors," or a series of independent shocks?

A fair presentation has to note where the clean ABCT narrative gets messier. Some of the current inflationary pressure — <cite index="6-1">"supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy"</cite>, tied specifically to the 2026 US-Iran conflict — is not obviously a credit-expansion story at all; it looks more like a classic real-supply shock of the kind Rothbard himself distinguished from monetary-cycle phenomena (see the companion ABCT document, §9, on distinguishing ordinary sectoral disruptions from the cluster-of-error pattern ABCT specifically explains). An Austrian would likely respond that the *underlying* persistence and stickiness of inflation — the fact that it has stayed well above the Fed's 2% target for over five years, on top of an already-inflated price level from 2021–2022 — is the monetary story, while the *specific timing* of the 2026 acceleration is a real, geopolitical shock layered on top of that monetary base. Both things can be true simultaneously, and separating them cleanly is exactly the kind of task Mises himself said statistics alone cannot definitively settle (companion praxeology document, §4.2).

## 3. Fiscal Dominance: Rothbard's "Central Bank as Revenue Mechanism," Live

This is arguably where the current data maps onto the Austrian framework most precisely of all, and it centers on a concept — **fiscal dominance** — that has moved from an academic curiosity into mainstream financial commentary over the past two years, describing almost exactly the dynamic the companion sound-money document lays out in §6.

- <cite index="19-1">As of July 2026, total US gross national debt stands at $39.39 trillion, and at the current average interest rate of 3.41% on outstanding marketable debt, annual interest expense already exceeds $1 trillion per year — roughly $2.9 billion every day.</cite> <cite index="21-1">That monthly interest bill — roughly $88 billion — is now comparable to combined federal spending on defense and education.</cite>
- This is precisely the structural bind Rothbard described as the underlying, usually-unstated reason states tolerate and even encourage central banking: a government whose debt-service costs are this large has a direct fiscal interest in **suppressed real interest rates**, regardless of what price stability might otherwise require. <cite index="19-1">A central bank that cannot raise rates aggressively without straining the Treasury's own borrowing costs is not a fully free agent</cite> — this is fiscal dominance in the technical sense, and it is close to a textbook description of what Rothbard, writing decades earlier about the Fed's founding-era relationship with Treasury financing, argued was the *real*, structural function of a central bank standing behind an increasingly indebted state (see the companion sound-money document, §6, and the FRB document, §3).
- The three FOMC dissenters in July 2026 (Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan) wanting to hike rates against a majority holding steady, amid inflation stuck above target for half a decade, is the kind of internal split an Austrian would read as the observable symptom of this tension playing out inside the institution itself — a central bank whose stated mandate (price stability) is increasingly in tension with the government's fiscal position (debt sustainability), with no institutional mechanism to force a resolution one way or the other short of political choice.

### 3.1 The market's own verdict: gold, and the retreat from Treasuries

If Rothbard's sound-money framework is right that markets eventually "see through" a currency that a fiscally-dominant state has strong incentives to debase, the gold market in 2026 is worth reading closely:

- <cite index="19-1">Gold has risen from roughly $1,560 in January 2020 to around $4,040 by July 2026 — a gain of roughly 160% over six years</cite> — a period that, not coincidentally on the Austrian reading, spans essentially the entire post-2020 monetary and fiscal expansion described above.
- <cite index="21-1">Central banks purchased more than 1,000 tonnes of gold in 2025, the second-highest annual total in history, driven substantially by de-dollarization and reserve diversification</cite>, with <cite index="20-1">official purchases in 2026 projected to average roughly 60 metric tons per month.</cite> <cite index="19-1">Reserve managers of roughly 70 countries have been quietly reducing their US Treasury exposure and building gold reserves instead.</cite>
- This is a genuinely interesting test case for the sound-money framework specifically: central banks — the very institutions Rothbard regarded as the primary *engine* of unsound money domestically — are, in their own reserve-management behavior, revealing a preference for the market-originated commodity money Rothbard argued the state's own money-creation power ultimately depends on parasitically (companion sound-money document, §2.1). A Rothbardian reading would say this is the international monetary system's own participants voting, with their actual reserve allocations, for the Mengerian/regression-theorem logic over confidence in any single fiat currency's continued soundness — even though most of those same central banks continue to run their own domestic fiat systems without hesitation, an inconsistency Rothbard would likely have relished pointing out.
- It's worth flagging the more conventional, non-Austrian explanations analysts also offer for the same gold rally — real-rate dynamics, geopolitical hedging by sanctioned or sanction-wary states (Russia, China), and portfolio diversification unrelated to any judgment about the dollar's long-term soundness. The Austrian reading doesn't require rejecting these; it treats them as compatible layers on top of the more structural debasement story.

## 4. The AI Capital-Expenditure Boom: A Live Test of the Capital-Structure Framework

This is the most speculative — and, for a Rothbard-influenced reader, probably the most interesting — application, because it's genuinely contested in real time rather than settled with hindsight the way 2020–2023 already is.

### 4.1 The scale of the buildout

<cite index="26-1">The five largest hyperscalers (Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle) are projected to spend $725 billion on AI capital expenditure in 2026 alone, with total global AI spending forecast at $2.53 trillion.</cite> <cite index="29-1">JPMorgan now estimates total global AI-related capital expenditure will reach $5.5 trillion through 2030</cite>, with a growing share financed by debt rather than operating cash flow. <cite index="27-1">This is being framed, even by mainstream and central-bank-adjacent institutions, against explicit historical parallels: the Bank for International Settlements' 2026 annual report compared the buildout to "canal and British railway mania in the 1800s, electrification exuberance of the 1920s, and the dotcom boom of the 1990s," noting all these episodes "shared one common trait: a genuine technological breakthrough that attracted capital in excess of what commercial returns could ultimately justify."</cite>

### 4.2 Reading this through the capital-structure lens

The companion ABCT and time-preference documents give a specific vocabulary for exactly this kind of question, and it's worth being precise about what the framework does and doesn't claim:

- **The structure-of-production question.** Data centers, chip fabrication capacity, and the surrounding energy infrastructure are about as "higher-order," capital-intensive, and remote-from-final-consumption as a production stage gets — this is Böhm-Bawerkian roundaboutness at an extreme scale. Whether this lengthening of the capital structure is *sustainable* — genuinely warranted by underlying time preference and real resource availability — versus an *artifact of a still-unusually-accommodative credit environment*, is precisely the diagnostic question ABCT is built to ask.
- **The financing structure is the tell an Austrian would look at first**, more than the technology itself. <cite index="28-1">Depreciation schedules set in 2023 are already outdated, understating real asset depletion by an estimated $176 billion between 2026 and 2028, and over $662 billion in lease commitments sit off balance sheets — at Amazon specifically, capital expenditure has grown from $12 billion in 2017 to $151 billion in the last twelve months, now exceeding operating cash flow entirely and pushing free cash flow negative for the first time.</cite> <cite index="29-1">JPMorgan estimates debt financing tied to the AI build-out at $4.1 trillion, with high-grade corporate debt accounting for more than $2.1 trillion of the total.</cite> A Rothbardian would flag this shift — from self-funded, cash-flow-financed expansion toward debt-financed expansion — as the specific marker that distinguishes ordinary entrepreneurial risk-taking (which the framework has no quarrel with) from the credit-expansion-fueled overinvestment ABCT specifically diagnoses, *if and to the extent* that debt is itself being priced below what a genuinely tight, savings-constrained credit market would charge.
- **The "cluster of errors" question, live.** ABCT's answer to "why would sophisticated technology executives at five different companies all overinvest simultaneously" (see the companion ABCT document, §5, on this exact objection) is that they aren't independently guessing — they're all responding to the same environment: multi-year near-zero real rates during the buildout's inception, a shared competitive fear of being left behind, and, per the Fed's own assessment, a systemic interdependency that makes individually rational behavior look collectively precarious. <cite index="26-1">Notably, the Federal Reserve formally listed AI as a top systemic risk to financial stability in 2026, ranking just behind geopolitical threats</cite> — an unusually direct, real-time acknowledgment from the very institution ABCT holds most responsible for generating this kind of cluster in the first place.
- **The uncomfortable complication for a clean Austrian reading**: unlike the 2020–21 episode, the AI buildout isn't obviously being financed by *fresh* central-bank-created credit in the way the pandemic-era boom was — much of it is running through corporate bond markets and hyperscaler balance sheets during a period of comparatively *tight*, not loose, policy (a 3.5–3.75% funds rate, held there specifically because inflation remains stubborn). A strict Misesian would note that some of the loanable funds financing this buildout may in fact represent **genuine savings** — insurance companies, pension funds, and other real capital pools bidding for AI-linked corporate debt because they judge the returns credible — which is exactly the "sustainable lengthening of the capital structure" case the theory says is *not* a problem (companion time-preference document, §4.2). <cite index="29-1">JPMorgan's own house view is explicitly that the surge is "not only durable, but increasingly profitable," with operating cash flow projected to surpass $900 billion by 2027</cite> — a genuinely contested empirical claim, not a settled one either way.
- Where an Austrian and a mainstream analyst would likely converge, interestingly, is on the diagnosis that **the real constraint to watch is monetary, not technological**: <cite index="32-1">economists such as Ruchir Sharma warn the buildout could unwind specifically "if US interest rates rise and cheap capital becomes scarce,"</cite> which is a distinctly Misesian framing even from a non-Austrian commentator — the boom's sustainability is treated as contingent on the *cost of capital*, not on the underlying technology's merit, which is exactly ABCT's structural claim about what determines whether a lengthened capital structure holds together or unwinds.

### 4.3 What ABCT does *not* claim here, stated plainly

It's worth being explicit, in the spirit of fair presentation: ABCT does not claim AI itself is fraudulent, worthless, or doomed — Rothbard's theory has never been a claim that new technologies or long-horizon investment are inherently suspect. Its claim is narrower and more specific: that the *sustainability* of a given investment boom is a function of whether it is financed by genuine, voluntary savings reflecting real time preference, or by credit conjured independently of savings and priced below its natural rate. Sorting the current AI buildout into one category or the other in real time is exactly the kind of question Mises's own methodology says can't be resolved by statistics alone (companion praxeology document, §4.2) — it will, on the Austrian view, only be fully legible in hindsight, the way 2020's imbalances only became fully legible by 2023.

## 5. Economic Calculation in an AI-Saturated Economy: A Genuine Rabbit Hole

This is worth a short detour because it connects the calculation-debate document to a question Mises could not have anticipated but whose logic bears directly on.

- A recurring 2020s-era question — could sufficiently powerful AI/computation eventually solve central planning's calculation problem, succeeding where Lange's 1930s trial-and-error mechanism failed? — is addressed almost exactly by the objection already catalogued in the companion economic-calculation document, §9.1: writing down or computing the *solution* to an allocation problem is not the same as generating the underlying **data** the solution requires as inputs. No degree of computational power resolves the deeper issue that the relevant valuations — what specific goods are worth to specific people at a specific moment, information that on the Misesian account exists nowhere prior to and independent of actual market exchange — are not the kind of thing available to be fed into any model, however sophisticated the model. Ironically, the same AI buildout discussed in §4 is, on this reading, itself entirely a product of **decentralized, market-priced entrepreneurial calculation** (hyperscalers bidding for capital, GPUs, and power against each other and against alternative uses of the same resources) rather than a step toward centrally *replacing* that calculation — the infrastructure is being built through the price system, not as a substitute for it.
- A live, non-hypothetical version of the fractional-reserve question has also emerged in the stablecoin sector, where privately issued dollar-pegged tokens raise, in modern form, exactly the "is this a bailment or a loan, and is it fully backed" question the companion FRB document treats at length regarding 17th-century goldsmith banking (§3) and the Foley v. Hill legal reclassification (§5.3) — current stablecoin-reserve disclosure requirements are, in effect, a live 21st-century re-run of the same underlying dispute between the Rothbardian 100%-reserve position and the free-banking view that transparent, disclosed fractional backing is legitimate.

## 6. Where the Framework Offers the Sharpest Diagnosis — and Where It's Most Contestable

In the interest of fair presentation, it's worth separating these explicitly rather than letting the Austrian reading stand unqualified throughout.

**Where the fit is strongest:**
- The 2020–2023 sequence (unprecedented monetary expansion → asset and goods-price inflation → aggressive tightening → concentrated bank failures in duration-mismatched, rate-sensitive institutions) tracks the ABCT boom-bust mechanics about as cleanly as any real historical episode Rothbard himself examined.
- The fiscal-dominance dynamic — a heavily indebted state with a direct interest in suppressed real rates, and a central bank increasingly unable to act independently of that fact — is close to a textbook confirmation of Rothbard's "central bank as revenue mechanism" thesis (companion sound-money document, §6), and is now openly discussed in exactly those terms by mainstream financial commentary, not just Austrian sources.
- Gold's structural bull market and central banks' own reserve-diversification behavior offer a genuinely striking, real-time illustration of the sound-money framework's claim that markets eventually price in debasement risk, even if the specific triggers (de-dollarization, sanctions-driven diversification) are more geopolitically contingent than a pure Rothbardian account would emphasize.

**Where it's most contestable:**
- The current bout of elevated inflation is significantly entangled with a real, geopolitical energy shock (the Iran conflict) that isn't obviously a monetary-expansion story, complicating any claim that today's above-target inflation is a clean, continuing echo of 2020–21 credit expansion rather than a fresh, independent shock.
- The AI capital-expenditure boom is occurring during a period of comparatively tight monetary policy, which cuts against the simplest version of the ABCT narrative (credit expansion → malinvestment) even as the debt-financing structure of the buildout raises other, related Austrian concerns about sustainability.
- Whether the 2023 bank-rescue interventions were, on net, a costly deferral of necessary liquidation (the Rothbardian reading) or a successful, low-cost prevention of an unnecessary and destructive secondary panic (closer to the mainstream and even the Misesian "avoid the secondary depression" reading — see companion ABCT document, §6.1) is a real, open dispute even *within* the Austrian tradition's own internal vocabulary, not just between Austrians and their critics.

## 7. Closing Synthesis

Read together, the 2020s look, from this framework's vantage point, like an unusually rich natural experiment in almost every concept this knowledge base has assembled: a textbook credit-expansion boom and bust (2020–2023); a live demonstration of fractional-reserve fragility under duration risk (SVB); a fiscal-dominance dynamic Rothbard predicted in structural terms decades before the phrase existed; a sound-money stress test being run in real time by the gold market and by central banks' own reserve behavior; and an enormous, genuinely uncertain capital-structure bet — the AI buildout — whose ultimate verdict, on Mises's own methodological terms, cannot yet be read off the data with certainty, only interpreted, provisionally, the way Rothbard interpreted the 1920s while still living through the 1970s. That last point is worth sitting with: the Austrian framework's own epistemology counsels real humility about pattern-matching a still-unfolding episode too neatly onto history's clearest prior cases, even when — especially when — the fit looks unusually clean.
