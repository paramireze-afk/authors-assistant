# When Words Had to Become Action

*A look back at Radiology leadership in the summer of 2020*

It's hard to remember just how much was happening at once in the spring of 2020.

The Department of Radiology was already operating in a world transformed by COVID-19. Many of us were working from home, normal routines had been disrupted, and the department was adapting to a public health emergency whose length and consequences nobody yet understood.

Then came the killing of George Floyd and the nationwide reckoning over racism that followed.

In Radiology, the conversation moved remarkably quickly from the national stage to a practical question: what should our department do?

## June 2: “We need to take action”

On June 2, Department Chair Dr. Thomas Grist was drafting a message to the department about racial discrimination and bias. What stands out in the email thread “FW: Call to action - Radiology effort” is that he didn't want to issue only a statement. He wanted to announce three specific actions:

1. Appoint Dr. Daniella Martin as Director of Diversity and Inclusion.
2. Ask the department's finance committee to consider philanthropic opportunities.
3. Establish bystander-awareness training for the department.

Dr. Grist explicitly embraced the idea that words alone would not produce change. The department needed actions behind them.

Reading the email six years later, you can see leadership thinking in real time. It wasn't yet a polished institutional announcement; people were still working out what the department could actually commit to.

## June 3: Turning an idea into a plan

The next day, the conversation became more concrete.

Jessica Robbins sent a summary that she and Dr. Martin had put together, covering work already underway and work planned for the future. It also described a broader professional development curriculum on fostering an inclusive and diverse environment, though not every piece of it had been decided. Mark Connolly was looking into how the department might use the Harvard Implicit Association Test.

Meanwhile, Christopher Temme, Radiology's senior communications specialist, was helping frame the initiatives for a department-wide message.

What began as “What should we say?” was becoming “What are we going to do?”

## June 4: The message goes to the department

On June 4, Dr. Grist's “Call to action - UW Radiology Effort” went out to everyone in Radiology.

One line captures its central idea: “it is not our words, but our actions, that will be our legacy.”

The message tied the department's diversity and inclusion commitments to concrete steps and again made the case that words by themselves would not bring change.

It is striking how little time passed between the first internal discussion and the department-wide message: the drafting request came on June 2, and the message went out on June 4.

## June 5: Taking the message beyond email

The next morning, people were already talking about sharing the message more widely.

Maggi Schrader wrote that it had come up in a leadership meeting and asked whether it could be adapted for the department's Twitter account. She also told Dr. Grist that she and others felt supported by him and by department leadership. He approved sharing it.

This small exchange adds something the formal announcement doesn't: it shows the message circulating through leadership conversations rather than being issued once and forgotten.

## June 9: From an email to the faculty meeting

Five days after the department-wide message, the initiatives reached the faculty meeting.

Dr. Grist asked Christopher Temme to prepare slides describing the diversity action plan, specifically covering the new Director of Diversity and Inclusion position, philanthropy guidelines, and the planned year-long training effort.

The progression is worth noticing:

| Date   | Step |
|--------|------|
| June 2 | Define concrete actions |
| June 3 | Develop the initiatives and the communication |
| June 4 | Announce the plan to the department |
| June 5 | Discuss sharing the message publicly |
| June 9 | Bring the action plan to the faculty meeting |

That is a much more interesting story than “Radiology released a statement.”

## June 15–19: Part of the department's record

By mid-June, the topic was also headed for the department's summer newsletter.

Christopher Temme's June 15 draft of the Summer 2020 Newsletter listed Diversity and Inclusion as one of its new articles, alongside a piece on the department's COVID-19 experience. The newsletter went to department employees on June 19, and its introduction opened with the department's experience of the pandemic.

In other words, COVID-19 and the racial justice moment weren't separate episodes in the department's history. They shared the same newsletter and the same month.

## July–November: From response to education

The email record shows that the educational work continued well past June.

A later request from Mark Connolly documents a 2020–2021 Faculty Development Series on unconscious bias. The first presentation took place on July 28, a second on November 11, and a third was planned for February 2021. He asked that the recordings and materials be added to the Diversity, Equity & Inclusion resources on the department intranet.

On November 12, Andrew Craven, Radiology's IT manager, forwarded a Bias in Healthcare presentation to his team. He explained that the roughly 90-minute session had been mandatory for faculty, residents and fellows, the SMPH administrative leadership team, and UW Health managers, and he recommended that his own staff watch it as well.

That gives the story an important second act. The June emails weren't the end of the trail. Months later, bias and inclusion were part of professional development programming, the department intranet, and everyday staff conversations.

## Looking back

What I find most interesting about these old emails isn't any single announcement. It's the sequence.

The first messages show leadership wrestling with how to respond to an extraordinary national moment. Within days, the conversation shifted from statements to specific departmental commitments. Those ideas then moved into a department-wide message, leadership discussions, a faculty meeting, the newsletter, professional development, and the intranet.

And all of it happened while the department was also dealing with COVID-19.

For those of us who were here, that may be the detail most easily lost with time. Today it's easy to file “COVID” and “racial justice protests” as separate chapters in our memories of 2020. We didn't experience them that way. We were working remotely, coping with a pandemic, keeping Radiology's systems and operations running, watching events unfold in Madison and across the country, and reading messages asking what our institution and our department should do differently.

Looking back through the department's old correspondence, June 2020 feels less like a single leadership announcement and more like a moment when a conversation quickly became an action plan.

Perhaps that's the most valuable thing an old Outlook inbox can preserve: not just what eventually became official, but the messy in-between steps that show how people got there.
