# Beyond Cannabinoids: Contaminants in Cannabis

> **In short:** The chemicals of concern in cannabis aren't just the plant's own cannabinoids and terpenes. Microbial toxins, heavy metals, pesticide residues, and poorly characterized synthetic byproducts are documented risks, especially for people who inhale cannabis or have weakened immune systems.

**How to read this article:** It centers Kevin McKernan's research and arguments. Where his views differ from regulators, industry groups, or other researchers, a **"Where views differ"** note summarizes the other side. Some of his positions are widely accepted, and the article says so where that's the case.

## About the Source

Kevin McKernan is a genomics scientist and the founder and chief scientific officer of **Medicinal Genomics**. He led R&D work during the Human Genome Project and was involved in early sequencing of the cannabis genome.

Through papers, conference talks (such as CannMed), company testing data, and his Substack, he has focused on two sides of cannabis chemistry:

- the plant's natural compounds, such as cannabinoids and terpenes
- the unwanted contaminants that can end up in the final product

> **Context for readers:**
> - **Commercial interest.** Medicinal Genomics sells DNA-based (qPCR) pathogen tests for cannabis. Critics point out that his arguments against culture-based testing align with his company's products. Supporters respond that his data is published and that some of his recommendations, such as species-specific *Aspergillus* testing, have since been adopted by regulators.
> - **Dissident reputation.** McKernan is widely known outside cannabis for claiming that mRNA COVID-19 vaccines contain excess residual plasmid DNA, including SV40 promoter sequences. Health agencies such as the FDA and Health Canada have said residual DNA levels are within limits and pose no safety concern. That dispute is separate from his cannabis work, but it shapes how some readers receive him.

## The Main Contaminant Categories

A 2017 book chapter McKernan co-authored, *Contaminants of Concern in Cannabis: Microbes, Heavy Metals and Pesticides*, groups the risks into three categories. Later work added a fourth.

| Category | Examples | Main concerns | How contested? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microbes and their byproducts | *Aspergillus* fungi, bacteria, mycotoxins, endotoxins | Infection in immunocompromised users, allergies, asthma, organ toxicity | Partly. Testing methods and consumer endotoxin risk are debated. |
| Heavy metals | Lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury | Neurological and kidney damage from chronic exposure | Largely mainstream |
| Pesticides and growth regulators | Malathion, plant growth regulators | Residues that may concentrate in extracts or change when burned | Largely mainstream |
| Synthetic byproducts | Converted cannabinoids, residual catalysts | Poorly studied compounds with unknown safety profiles | Largely mainstream among regulators; disputed by parts of the hemp industry |

### 1. Microbes and Their Byproducts

**Live organisms.** Bacteria and fungi can cause infections, particularly in people with compromised immune systems. Even dead organisms can trigger allergic reactions or asthma.

**Mycotoxins.** Fungal overgrowth can produce toxins such as aflatoxins and ochratoxins, which may damage the liver or kidneys or carry cancer risk.

**Testing gaps.** McKernan's 2015 sequencing study of dispensary flower, published in *F1000Research*, argued that culture-based testing rules borrowed from the food industry have serious limits for cannabis. His work suggests that "total yeast and mold" culture tests can:

- miss important pathogens, such as certain *Aspergillus* species
- produce false positives on some growth media
- allow other bacteria, including toxin-producing ones, to grow unnoticed

He advocates DNA-based methods (qPCR and sequencing) for greater accuracy and specificity.

> **Where views differ:** Culture-based total yeast and mold counts remain a standard requirement in many state programs. Defenders argue that culture methods are established, inexpensive, and measure *living* organisms, which matter most for infection risk, whereas DNA tests can also detect dead organisms. That said, McKernan's core concern has gained ground: several states now require testing for specific *Aspergillus* species, and qPCR methods are accepted in a number of them.

**Endotoxins.** More recently, McKernan has focused on endotoxins, which are inflammatory molecules (lipopolysaccharides) from the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria. Key points from his work:

- His testing of dispensary flower and hemp samples found endotoxin widely present, and levels did not track with standard colony counts.
- Remediation methods such as ozone, irradiation, or e-beam kill live microbes but don't remove endotoxins. He has argued that some growers rely on these methods to pass microbial tests rather than growing cleanly in the first place.
- No state currently requires endotoxin testing on cannabis.

> **Where views differ:** Mainstream research treats endotoxin mainly as an **occupational** hazard. NIOSH studies have measured endotoxin exposure among cannabis workers, with the highest levels during processing tasks such as grinding. Whether endotoxin on flower meaningfully harms **consumers** is not established, in part because little is known about how much survives smoking or vaping. Notably, Medicinal Genomics' own write-up says consumer risk may be limited to people with conditions such as asthma, and calls for research on endotoxin in cannabis smoke and vapor.

Airborne cannabis hazards received wider attention after the 2022 death of a worker at a Massachusetts cultivation facility, which was attributed to occupational asthma from cannabis dust. The investigation measured endotoxin in the air but did not pin the death on a single cause.

### 2. Heavy Metals

Cannabis is very good at pulling metals out of soil and water. Hemp has even been used to help clean up contaminated land for this reason. The downside is that metals like lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury can end up in the product, and long-term exposure by inhalation or ingestion can harm the nervous system, kidneys, and other organs.

> **Consensus:** This concern is widely shared, and most state cannabis programs require heavy metal testing.

### 3. Pesticides and Growth Regulators

Because cannabis is still federally illegal in the U.S., the EPA hasn't approved any pesticides or set residue limits for it. That leaves a regulatory gap that states fill unevenly.

McKernan's analysis of "THCA hemp" flower sold online found:

- pesticides in some samples, including **Malathion**, which can convert to the more toxic **malaoxon** when burned
- plant growth regulators above allowed limits in some samples

Extraction can make this worse: residues may become more concentrated in a concentrate than they were in the original flower.

> **Consensus:** Concern about pesticides in cannabis is mainstream. The open questions are mostly about which compounds to test for and what limits to set, not whether testing is needed.

### 4. Synthetic Conversions and Mislabeling

Some hemp-derived products are made by chemically converting CBD into delta-8 THC or other analogs, often using acid catalysts. These processes can leave behind:

- poorly studied byproduct compounds
- residual catalysts and solvents

Mislabeling and adulteration are also more common in loosely regulated hemp markets than in state-licensed cannabis markets.

> **Where views differ:** Regulators and public health agencies largely share these concerns. Parts of the hemp industry argue that well-made converted products are safe when properly tested, and that the problems come from bad actors rather than the chemistry itself.

## The Online THCA Hemp Problem

In a set of about 30 THCA hemp flower samples purchased online that McKernan discussed, many showed:

- contamination with *Aspergillus fumigatus*
- total potential THC well above the 0.3% legal limit for hemp, since THCA converts to THC when heated
- pesticide and other quality failures at rates well above those typical of regulated cannabis

Hemp-derived products can sidestep the stricter testing required in state cannabis programs, which lets contaminated or mislabeled product reach consumers. Practices such as "lab shopping" (choosing labs known for lenient results) add to the problem.

> **Where views differ:** Hemp industry groups have argued that THCA flower was legal under the 2018 Farm Bill, which measured only delta-9 THC, and that a small sample of online purchases doesn't represent the whole market. Federal law is moving in McKernan's direction on potency, though: a law signed in November 2025 redefines hemp using total THC, including THCA, which would make most THCA flower federally illegal. It is scheduled to take effect in late 2026, but Congress has considered delays, so check its current status.

## Who Is Most at Risk?

- **People who smoke or vape**, because toxins and spores go straight to the lungs
- **Medical patients**, especially those with weakened immune systems or asthma
- **Concentrate users**, since extraction can concentrate contaminants
- **Cannabis workers**, who face the best-documented endotoxin and allergen exposures
- **Anyone relying on "remediated" product**, since killing microbes doesn't remove their toxins or DNA, which can create a false sense of safety

> **Where views differ:** Many states permit remediation methods such as irradiation, which is also widely used on foods and spices. Regulators generally view it as an effective way to reduce microbial risk. McKernan's critique is less that remediation is dangerous and more that it can hide poor growing conditions and leave toxins behind.

## Historical Context: Fungi as Drug-Crop Control

McKernan has pointed to a lesser-known chapter of drug policy history: government research into using plant-killing fungi against illicit crops.

| Period | Development |
|---|---|
| 1970s | U.S.-funded research explored *Fusarium oxysporum* strains as a biological herbicide against cannabis, studying host specificity and how long the fungus persists in soil. |
| 1990s | Related work targeted coca and opium poppy as part of international drug eradication efforts. |
| Late 1990s | A proposal to test a *Fusarium* strain against cannabis in Florida drew opposition from state environmental officials. |
| ~2000 | U.S. pressure for Colombia to use *Fusarium* against coca met strong resistance and was not adopted. |

**Why critics objected.** Opponents, some of whom called these agents "Agent Green," raised concerns about:

- uncontrolled spread to other plants, including food crops
- long-term persistence in soil
- human exposure to the fungi or their toxins, since some *Fusarium* species produce trichothecenes and other mycotoxins

These programs were never deployed at scale. McKernan has argued that they showed little regard for what would happen to people who later consumed affected plant material.

> **Where views differ:** Supporters of the research presented host-specific fungi as a more targeted alternative to broad chemical herbicide spraying. McKernan's point about disregard for consumers is his interpretation of the record, not a finding from the programs themselves.

**Why it still matters.** *Fusarium* species are also natural cannabis pathogens. They cause wilt, and their mycotoxins have been detected in some seized samples. The history illustrates a dual-use problem: the same organism can be viewed as a crop-control tool in one context and a consumer contamination risk in another.

## A Note on "Weaponization"

Today's contaminant problems are not evidence of deliberate attacks on consumers. In modern hemp and cannabis markets, the realistic threats are **negligence and opportunistic adulteration**, such as:

- spraying products with synthetic cannabinoids
- bundling with other substances, such as kratom alkaloids
- weak age verification
- selling product that fails pathogen or pesticide tests

## What Would Help

McKernan's work points toward several practical improvements:

1. **Species-specific microbial testing** using DNA methods (qPCR and sequencing) rather than broad yeast-and-mold counts alone
2. **Routine screening** for endotoxins, pesticides, and heavy metals
3. **Genomic tools** for verifying strains and breeding pathogen-resistant plants
4. **Transparent data sharing** across labs and regulators
5. **Closing regulatory gaps** created by the mismatch between federal and state law and by the fast growth of novel hemp-derived products

Items 3 through 5 are broadly uncontroversial. Items 1 and 2 are where McKernan pushes further than most current regulations.

## Conclusion

The safety of a cannabis product depends on more than its cannabinoid content. Pesticides, heavy metals, microbial toxins, and synthetic byproducts are real, measurable risks that McKernan has helped document. On heavy metals, pesticides, and synthetics, his concerns sit close to the mainstream. On testing methods and endotoxins, he is ahead of current regulation, and the evidence for consumer harm is still developing. Rigorous testing, clearer regulation, and independent research remain the best defenses.
