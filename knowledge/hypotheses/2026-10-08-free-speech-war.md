# Who Decided the Cornell 7 Was the Story?

I was watching the DarkHorse Podcast when Bret and Heather started talking about the "Cornell 7," and I did what I usually do, which is start googling while they talk. The more I read, the less the story itself bothered me and the more one question did: why is this national news?

I don't mean that the allegations aren't serious. They are. I mean that thousands of campus sexual assault cases happen every year and almost none of them become a coast-to-coast story with its own nickname. Somebody, or something, decides which ones do. This article is about that decision, and the Cornell 7 is just the case that made me notice it.

## What Happened

Here's the short version. In October 2024, a 20-year-old Cornell student, known in court papers as Jane Doe, had about ten drinks over a few hours and walked to the Chi Phi fraternity house to see a friend. She says two brothers pressured her into snorting ketamine, and she agreed to a threesome with her friend and another member. Then one of them posted about her in the fraternity group chat, more men came into the room, and by her account it went on for hours. She says two of the newcomers also had sex with her without her consent, and others kissed, groped or watched.

She reported it to campus police a few weeks later. The district attorney declined to file charges, saying her signed statement described everything as voluntary, even though a transcript later surfaced where she told police she was raped. His office never actually investigated. Cornell ran its own Title IX process, expelled two of the men, and handed out lesser punishments to the rest.

And then, for almost two years, nothing. Outside of Ithaca, nobody cared.

So let's assume every word of her account is true. That still doesn't answer why this case, and why now.

## The Timing

When something suddenly shows up everywhere at once, with the same catchy tagline on page after page of results, I get suspicious. So I looked at what else was happening at Cornell that month.

- **September 15:** A student coalition called Students Not Bombs announces that Hasan Piker will speak on campus. Tickets sell out within hours.
- **September 16:** Jane Doe files a 101-page civil lawsuit.
- **September 24:** Piker speaks to a packed Sage Chapel, livestreamed to tens of thousands of viewers.
- **September 28:** The DA reopens the case, citing "community outrage stemming from the incomplete news and social media coverage."

A lawsuit that size was obviously in the works for months, so I'm not claiming it was filed because of Piker. What I'm asking is why it detonated when it did. And notice what the DA himself said: he didn't reopen the case because of new evidence he went out and found. He reopened it because of outrage, and he called the coverage driving that outrage incomplete.

## What It Buried

Students Not Bombs isn't a vague anti-war club. It's a coalition of Students for Justice in Palestine, Jewish Voice for Peace and Young Democratic Socialists of America, and it has a specific target: Cornell's partnership with the Technion, Israel's top engineering school and a major pipeline into the Israeli defense industry.

That partnership isn't a line item in an endowment portfolio. Cornell Tech, the university's flagship campus on Roosevelt Island, was built as a joint project with the Technion, and the two run an institute together right in the middle of it. Cornell's Student Assembly voted back in March to end the partnership. Zohran Mamdani floated reassessing it during his mayoral campaign. And "sever ties with the Technion" was printed right on the Piker flyer.

That's a movement with real momentum aimed at something a lot of powerful people want to protect. Cornell's administration already defends the partnership, so I don't think anyone needed to change the university's official position. But if you wanted to stop a movement, you wouldn't argue with it. You'd change the subject. Within days of Piker filling Sage Chapel, nobody was talking about the Technion. Everybody was talking about the Cornell 7.

The Technion wasn't the only thing that slid off the front page that week, either. Around September 25, the Wall Street Journal reported that Trump had rejected an Iranian ceasefire proposal and told aides he expects bombing to resume after the midterms. That's a story about whether this country stays at war. It barely registered.

## So Who Actually Controls the Media?

When people ask "who controls the media," it usually turns into either a conspiracy theory or an eye-roll. I don't think it has to be either. I think the honest answer is that control doesn't look like one guy in a room. It looks like a handful of layers, each of which can decide what you see, and almost none of which you ever voted for.

**The owners.** Larry Ellison, one of the richest men in the world, has given millions to Friends of the IDF. He financed his son David's takeover of Paramount, which owns CBS, and David installed Bari Weiss as editor-in-chief of CBS News. In January, an Oracle-led consortium took over TikTok's American operations, and the new joint venture controls content moderation for U.S. users. That's a network news division and the platform where most young Americans get their news, both now answerable to people with open, public commitments on exactly this issue.

**The people who own the venues.** Macklemore said "Free Palestine" on stage at MetLife Stadium and was dropped from Ed Sheeran's tour ten days later. According to Macklemore, it wasn't the fans or even Sheeran who forced it. It was stadium owners, led by Robert Kraft, telling the tour he'd be banned from their venues. You don't need to own a newspaper to control speech. Owning the stage works too.

**The donors.** When Penn's president gave her infamous answer to Congress in 2023, it was the threat of a major donor pulling a $100 million gift that helped push her out within days. Harvard's president followed, and Columbia's in 2024. Every university president in America watched that happen. Cornell's president is watching it now.

**The pressure groups.** StopAntisemitism named Ms. Rachel, a preschool YouTuber who spoke up for children in Gaza, a finalist for "Antisemite of the Year," on the same list as Tucker Carlson. The Israeli-American Council launched the petition that kicked off the Macklemore removal. These groups don't run newsrooms, but they're very good at manufacturing the outrage that newsrooms then cover.

**The government.** Cornell already paid $60 million to get its federal funding restored, and agreed to report admissions data to Washington through 2028. Florida wrote a definition of antisemitism into state law that counts calling Israel's founding "a racist endeavor" or holding Israel to "double standards" as antisemitism. When the government holds your money and defines your speech, you don't need anyone to tell you what not to say.

**The politicians.** Kathy Hochul and Letitia James are both on the ballot next month. A governor appointing a special prosecutor in a high-profile campus rape case weeks before an election has its own obvious logic, whatever else is going on. That's not a cabal. That's just incentives, and incentives are most of how this works.

And to be clear, because this question has an ugly history: I'm not talking about Jews controlling anything. Plenty of the loudest critics of Israel's wars are Jewish, including Jewish Voice for Peace, one of the groups that brought Piker to Cornell. I'm talking about specific owners, donors, organizations and officials, most of whom are perfectly happy to tell you exactly where they stand.

## The Deeper Problem

Here's what bothers me most. None of these layers has to coordinate for the result to look coordinated. An owner sets the tone. A pressure group supplies the outrage. A platform's algorithm amplifies it. A prosecutor and a governor respond to it. Every newsroom picks up the same nickname because every other newsroom already has. And the story that was quietly gaining ground, a student movement asking an Ivy League school to cut ties with a foreign defense pipeline, just stops existing.

That's not censorship in the old sense. Nobody banned anything. It's something more effective: a system where the people with the most money and the most leverage don't have to silence a conversation, they just have to make sure it isn't the one everybody's having.

Maybe the Cornell 7 timing is a coincidence. I genuinely can't prove otherwise. But I'd still like to know who decided this was the story, and I think we should all be asking that question a lot more often.
