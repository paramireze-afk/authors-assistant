# The 2006 Problem: Why the Scariest Year for Housing Wasn't 2008 — and What It Means for AI

If your retirement savings sit in an index fund, here's an uncomfortable fact: nearly half of that money is riding on a promise. Specifically, on roughly $2.1 trillion in future payments that Microsoft, Oracle, Google, and Amazon are counting on receiving from AI companies — most notably OpenAI — that have never once turned a profit.

That's the setup investment commentator Jay Martin uses to open his case that the AI boom is quietly repeating a mistake the U.S. economy already made once. Not in 2008, when Lehman Brothers collapsed and the financial system visibly buckled. Martin argues the real damage was done two years earlier, in 2006 — a year that, on paper, looked completely fine.

## Everybody Remembers 2008. Almost Nobody Remembers 2006.

By the time Lehman fell in 2008, the outcome had already been decided. The year that actually mattered, in Martin's telling, was 2006 — the year the underlying structure quietly died while every headline said things were fine, and home prices sat at record highs.

The mechanism was a mortgage product called the 2/28: two years at a low, easy rate, followed by 28 years at a much higher one that most borrowers could never actually afford. But almost nobody planned to pay that higher rate. The understood arrangement — for borrower and lender alike — was that the home's value would keep rising, allowing the owner to refinance before the rate jumped, pay off the old loan with a new one, and reset the clock on another two years of cheap payments. It worked, too: by the end of 2006, nearly four out of five of these loans written in 2003 had already been refinanced this way.

These loans were never actually designed to be repaid. They were designed to be replaced.

Here's the part Martin says most people remember backwards: house prices did *not* crash in 2006. They were near record highs. What changed was the *speed* of the climb — annual gains that had been running in the mid-teens shrank into single digits. Still positive. Still climbing. Just climbing slower.

And that was enough to break the machine. A borrower whose home value rose 8% instead of 15% couldn't pull enough new equity out of it to cover the old loan plus fees. Banks would only lend against the gain, and when the gain shrank, the replacement loan came up short. The refinancing ladder snapped — and defaults started climbing in record numbers *while prices were still near their all-time peak*. The visible crash came a year later. The panic came two years after that. The crash was the echo, not the origin.

Demand for housing itself was never the problem — people needed homes before, during, and after 2008. The problem was a financial structure stacked on top of housing that only worked if prices rose faster every single year. That's something no real economy can deliver forever.

## The Machine, Rebuilt — This Time Out of Valuations

Martin's argument is that this same structure has been rebuilt today, with OpenAI at its center and valuations standing in for home prices.

OpenAI's price tag over the last two and a half years: roughly $86 billion in early 2024, $157 billion by October 2024, $300 billion by March 2025, $500 billion by October 2025, and then — this past March — a record $122 billion funding round, the largest private raise ever recorded, at an $852 billion valuation.

On the other side of the ledger: OpenAI brought in around $20 billion in revenue in 2025, triple the year before. Real money. But the company spends far more than that every year on computing power, staff, and research, and loses tens of billions of dollars annually. It has never earned a profit.

So how does a company losing that much money every year pay its bills? By raising new money — and it can only raise new money because the valuation keeps climbing, and climbing fast. Each funding round from 2024 through 2025 priced the company at roughly 1.7 to 1.9 times the round before it. In Martin's framing, the rising valuation isn't the scoreboard. It's the income. Yesterday's bills get paid with today's higher price tag — the same way a 2/28 borrower paid off an old mortgage using a fresh, higher appraisal.

## Take-or-Pay: Where the $2 Trillion Comes From

The bills are enormous because of how AI computing gets purchased. OpenAI and similar labs have signed "take-or-pay" contracts — promising to pay for computing capacity years into the future whether they end up using it or not.

The companies on the receiving end of those payments — Microsoft, Oracle, Google, Amazon — record them as "backlog," treated as guaranteed, locked-in future revenue. Oracle's backlog now stands at roughly $638 billion, up 363% in a single year. Microsoft's sits near $625 billion. Across the four major platforms combined, the total contracted backlog is estimated around $2.1 trillion.

The concentration is the striking part: analysts tracing these contracts estimate that about half of that $2.1 trillion is owed by just two companies — OpenAI and Anthropic — neither of which is profitable. More than half of Oracle's entire backlog reportedly traces back to OpenAI alone.

And just as in the mortgage era, the lenders aren't simply waiting around to collect. Microsoft, Oracle, and their peers are borrowing real money *against* that guaranteed future revenue to fund data-center construction right now. Construction spending across these companies has gone from about $150 billion in 2023, to $226 billion in 2024, to $410 billion in 2025, to a planned roughly $725 billion this year. For the first time, that construction bill now exceeds all the cash these companies collect from their entire existing businesses combined — meaning the buildout is increasingly funded by debt, borrowed against promises made by companies with no income.

Trace the full chain: OpenAI promises future payments it can only meet by raising new money → it can only raise new money if its valuation keeps climbing → the tech giants treat those promises as guaranteed revenue and borrow real cash against them. Wall Street calls this "locked-in future demand." Traced to its source, Martin argues, it's a $2 trillion loan extended to borrowers with no income.

## Thirty Years of Dismissed Dominoes

The question, then, is what could slow the climb — because if AI valuations stop accelerating, the ability to raise fresh money against them breaks down, and the guaranteed future revenue everyone is counting on starts to look far less guaranteed.

Martin points to a pattern he says is three decades old. In the 1990s, China took over furniture, textiles, and toys — dismissed as cheap labor. In the 2000s, steel and shipbuilding — dismissed as a one-off. Then solar panels; China now makes roughly 8 out of 10 solar panels worldwide. Then batteries. Then electric vehicles: Tesla once dominated China's EV market and now holds only a single-digit share there, while BYD — once Tesla's student — now sells more EVs than any company on Earth. Each time, the reaction was the same: dismiss it as an isolated event, right up until the industry was gone.

Two weeks before this analysis, a Chinese lab called Moonshot released a model named Kimi K3. Within a day it topped a widely watched coding leaderboard, beating the best models from both Anthropic and OpenAI in blind testing at roughly 40% lower cost. Eleven days later, Moonshot released it for free — anyone, anywhere, can now download and run it. The White House's AI czar, David Sacks, reportedly called the development concerning.

The usage data backs that up. On OpenRouter, a marketplace where businesses shop for AI models, American models handled about 70% of traffic a year ago. Today it's around 30%, and the single most-used provider on the platform is now Chinese.

The Chinese models don't need to be better than the American ones, Martin argues — just close enough and cheap enough. That pulls some customers away entirely and forces the American labs to cut prices for the customers who stay. Either way, it slows the one number the entire funding ladder depends on: the speed of American AI revenue growth. And speed is the collateral. Every OpenAI round from 2024 to 2025 priced in at 1.7 to 1.9 times the last one; the next widely anticipated step — a public offering north of $1 trillion — would represent a jump of barely 1.2 times, the smallest yet, arriving at the exact moment free Chinese alternatives are eating into the growth that justifies the whole structure. House prices in 2006 didn't need to crash to break the mortgage machine. They only needed to rise 8% instead of 15%.

## A Ban That Runs the Playbook Backwards

Washington's response has been to debate an emergency ban on Chinese AI models — and Martin argues this inverts a strategy China has run for 25 years. Beijing has long invited strong foreign competitors — Google, Facebook, Uber, Tesla — into its market on purpose, letting the competitive pressure force domestic firms to improve quickly, before eventually tightening regulation once the lesson had been learned. That's the origin story behind companies like WeChat, Didi, and BYD.

A U.S. ban on Chinese models runs that logic in reverse: American businesses would lose access to a nearly free tool their competitors abroad keep using, while the protected domestic labs lose the competitive pressure that forces improvement. Leave the models in, and the growth curve underneath American AI risks flattening — the same trillion-dollar structure starts to wobble either way.

## The Third Borrower

Martin extends the parallel one level further, to what he calls the biggest borrower in the story: the U.S. government itself. Over the past 12 months the federal deficit ran about $1.6 trillion, covered the same way any borrower without profits covers a gap — by issuing new debt. Roughly $12 trillion in existing Treasury debt is due to be refinanced, not repaid, before the end of next year.

The government doesn't pay off old debt; it replaces it with new debt, and that only works as long as new lenders keep showing up. People lend not because they expect a lump-sum payback, but because they believe another lender will always be behind them — that American growth will keep the whole system credible.

Three borrowers, Martin argues, all running the identical structure: the 2/28 borrower rode rising house prices, OpenAI rides rising valuations, and the U.S. Treasury rides the world's continued belief in American growth. And these aren't parallel, independent risks — they're stacked. Belief in American AI helps hold up the S&P 500. The S&P 500 helps hold up the world's savings parked in American markets. Those savings, in turn, help fund each new Treasury auction. All of this sits against a national debt of $39.8 trillion, where interest payments alone now exceed $1 trillion a year — more than the entire U.S. military budget.

There's a further wrinkle. In 2008, panicked money fled into U.S. Treasuries because the fear was aimed at banks, not at the government — a flood of eager lenders let Washington borrow cheaply even in the middle of a crisis. A crisis rooted in AI would be different: the fear would be aimed at the belief that America owns the future of technology — the very belief propping up demand for U.S. debt in the first place. If that flight-to-safety reflex doesn't trigger the same way, a government already borrowing $1.6 trillion a year would have to offer higher rates to keep attracting lenders in a thinner market.

## What Martin Is and Isn't Claiming

To his credit, Martin draws a clear line here. He isn't arguing AI is worthless or that demand for it is fake — people needed houses before, during, and after 2008, and people will use AI before, during, and after whatever comes next. That was never the real question in 2006, and it isn't the real question now. The 2006 question wasn't "are house prices high?" It was "are house prices still rising faster than last year?" The honest answer turning to no is what set the arithmetic in motion — quietly for a year, then loudly for two.

He's equally clear that you cannot time this. In 2006, nearly two years passed between growth slowing and the structure fully breaking, and the people who flagged the problem looked publicly and painfully wrong the entire time, while prices kept setting records. The mechanism is knowable. The calendar isn't.

## What to Watch Instead of the Headlines

Since record highs themselves are meaningless as a warning sign, Martin says the useful move is watching the *speed*, not the level:

- Does OpenAI's — or similar firms' — next funding round price meaningfully higher than the last one, and by how much?
- Are the big cloud companies' contracted backlogs still *growing*, or merely staying large? Those aren't the same thing.
- Most tellingly: watch for the first time a major tech firm announces it's *cutting* data-center construction spending — and its stock rises on the news. In Martin's view, the day markets start rewarding a company for exiting the race is the day the race is effectively over.

## Martin's Advice to Investors

- **Understand concentration risk.** Roughly 40% of the S&P 500 sits in just ten companies, so a broad index fund carries heavy exposure to the same AI-driven belief system described above — not the diversification it appears to offer.
- **Examine counterparty risk.** For each holding, ask whether its value ultimately depends on someone else's future promise continuing to be believed. Some assets carry that risk; some don't.
- **Manage the psychology.** The hardest part is the waiting, while records keep printing and the crowd insists "this time is different." Investors who came through 2008 intact generally weren't the ones who guessed Lehman's exact date — they understood the underlying mechanics and positioned accordingly ahead of time.

His closing point: the number that has ever truly mattered isn't how high valuations climb, but how fast they're still climbing. Personally, he says he leans toward hard assets and commodities — raw materials that carry little or no counterparty risk.
