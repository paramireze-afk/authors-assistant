# The Case Against the Miracle Drug: Inside the GLP-1 Dissent

Few drugs have been sold to the public as hard as the GLP-1 class. Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound have been called the end of the obesity epidemic, a cure for addiction, a heart drug, a kidney drug, and possibly a treatment for Alzheimer's. Celebrities use them, employers cover them, and by some counts more than one in ten American adults has taken one.

A loose coalition of doctors, journalists, activists, lawyers and politicians thinks the enthusiasm has gotten far ahead of the evidence. Some of them make narrow, technical arguments that mainstream researchers take seriously. Others make sweeping claims about corruption and control. This article lays out what they say, explains the biology behind the side effects they point to, and then looks at what holds up.

---

## Part 1: How the drugs work (and why that matters for side effects)

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) is a hormone your gut releases after you eat. In its natural form it lasts only a couple of minutes in the bloodstream. It does several things at once:

- **Tells the pancreas to release insulin** when blood sugar is high, and suppresses glucagon, the hormone that raises blood sugar.
- **Slows down the stomach**, so food leaves it more gradually.
- **Signals the brain that you're full**, acting on appetite centers in the hypothalamus and brainstem.
- **Dampens food "reward"** by acting on dopamine circuits involved in craving.

The drugs are engineered versions of this hormone that last about a week instead of a few minutes. Tirzepatide also mimics a second gut hormone, GIP. The key point critics make is that the drugs don't target one system. They switch on a natural signal far harder and far longer than the body ever does on its own, in tissues all over the body. Nearly every side effect below traces back to that fact.

## Part 2: The dissenting voices

### Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — "Fix the food, not the patient"

Before becoming Secretary of Health and Human Services, Kennedy was one of the loudest skeptics of the drugs. His core argument is economic and philosophical. The money the country would spend putting tens of millions of people on a lifelong injection, he argued, could instead go toward making real food affordable. In interviews he pointed to the European Medicines Agency's review of possible links to suicidal thoughts and to how quickly Ozempic's list of approved uses was growing.

In office, his position has been more complicated. His HHS has emphasized diet and ultra-processed food as root causes, while the administration has also pursued deals to lower GLP-1 prices and widen access. Critics on both sides have noticed the tension.

### Calley Means — "Obesity is not an Ozempic deficiency"

Means, a former consultant to food and drug companies who co-founded TrueMed, became the face of the anti-Ozempic argument in 2024, especially after a widely shared interview with Tucker Carlson. His case has three parts:

1. **The drug treats the symptom, not the cause.** Obesity, in his view, is a downstream result of ultra-processed food, inactivity and metabolic dysfunction. A drug that suppresses appetite leaves all of that in place.
2. **Lifelong dependence is the business model.** Because weight typically returns when people stop, he argues the industry has found the ideal product: one that a large share of the population could take forever.
3. **The institutions that should be skeptical have been captured.** He points to drug-industry money flowing to medical societies, patient advocacy groups and influencers who shape treatment guidelines and public opinion. He has singled out pediatric guidelines that opened the door to prescribing these drugs to adolescents.

Means has said he does not want the drugs banned and that adults should be free to take them. Readers should know that his company's business is built around the food-and-exercise alternative he promotes.

### Casey Means — the metabolic-health argument

Casey Means, a Stanford-trained physician, co-founder of the glucose-monitoring company Levels, and author of *Good Energy*, frames the issue around "metabolic dysfunction." Her view is that obesity, type 2 diabetes, heart disease and many chronic illnesses share a root cause in how cells handle energy, driven largely by diet, sleep, stress and toxins. GLP-1 drugs lower weight and blood sugar, she argues, but it is not yet clear they restore the underlying metabolic health, and the focus on them pulls attention away from lifestyle changes that do.

### Dr. Robert Lustig — the endocrinologist's caution

Lustig, a pediatric endocrinologist at UCSF and longtime critic of sugar, is more measured. He has explained the drugs' mechanisms in detail and raised three main worries: loss of muscle along with fat, the uncertain long-term effects of starting children on them, and the cost. He has also suggested the drugs might work best as a temporary "jumpstart" that helps people change their diet, rather than as a permanent therapy.

### Tucker Carlson — the platform

Carlson is not a medical critic himself, but his show gave the anti-Ozempic argument its biggest audience. His framing was that legacy media, which he sees as heavily funded by pharmaceutical advertising, had been uncritical of the drugs, and that viewers deserved to hear the other side. That framing, more than any specific scientific claim, is what turned GLP-1 skepticism into a political identity marker.

### Dr. Aseem Malhotra — "a pharmaceutical fix for a food problem"

Malhotra, a British cardiologist known for campaigning against sugar and for low-carbohydrate diets, has argued that the drugs medicalize a dietary problem and that industry influence distorts how their benefits are presented. He is a polarizing figure. His later claims about COVID vaccines were rejected by most of the medical establishment, and his critics say that damages his credibility on other topics. His supporters say it shows his willingness to challenge industry consensus.

### Johann Hari — the ambivalent insider

Hari, a journalist, took Ozempic himself and wrote about it in *Magic Pill* (2024). His book is not an attack. He describes real benefits but also worries about muscle loss, the effect on people's relationship with food and pleasure, possible mental health effects, and how little is known about lifelong use. His central point is that society is making a huge bet on a drug class whose long-term consequences nobody can yet know.

### The BMJ investigations — follow the money

The British medical journal *The BMJ* has published investigative reporting on financial relationships between GLP-1 manufacturers and the experts, advocacy groups and guideline panels that shape obesity treatment. The concern is not that any one payment is a bribe. It's that when much of the expert ecosystem is funded by the companies that profit, the field may lose its ability to be skeptical.

### Plaintiffs and their attorneys — the courtroom critique

Thousands of patients have sued Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly. The main federal case focuses on gastrointestinal injuries, especially gastroparesis ("stomach paralysis"), bowel obstruction and ileus. A second federal track, created in late 2025, covers a form of sudden vision loss called NAION. The plaintiffs' central claim is "failure to warn": that the companies knew or should have known about these risks and did not put them clearly on the label. The companies deny this, saying the known risks are disclosed and that causation has not been proven, especially in diabetic patients who already face higher rates of these conditions.

### Price critics — Bernie Sanders and others

Sen. Bernie Sanders and other lawmakers have attacked the drugs' US prices, which have been far higher than in Europe. Their argument is about cost and access, not safety. They are often lumped together with safety critics, but they want more people on these drugs at lower prices, not fewer.

## Part 3: The side effects and what causes them

### Nausea, vomiting and diarrhea

**How common:** Very. In trials, a large share of users had GI symptoms, especially when doses go up. A meaningful minority stop the drug because of them.

**Mechanism:** GLP-1 receptors sit in the brainstem's area postrema, the brain's "vomiting center," which is unusually exposed to the bloodstream. Activating it directly produces nausea. Slowing the stomach adds to the problem, since food sits longer and creates fullness and discomfort. This is why doses are increased slowly over weeks.

### Gastroparesis, ileus and bowel obstruction

**What critics say:** Some users develop severe, lasting stomach paralysis that doesn't fully resolve after stopping the drug.

**Mechanism:** Slowing gastric emptying is not a side effect. It's one of the drugs' intended actions, working partly through the vagus nerve, which controls gut movement. In most people this effect is moderate and fades somewhat over time. In some, motility slows so much that food stalls in the stomach (gastroparesis) or the intestines stop moving (ileus), which can lead to obstruction. The disputed question is whether the drugs can cause *permanent* damage or whether they unmask or worsen a problem people already had, since long-standing diabetes itself damages the nerves that control the gut.

**A related risk:** Because food stays in the stomach, people on these drugs face a higher risk of inhaling stomach contents during anesthesia. Anesthesiology groups now issue guidance on pausing the drugs or adjusting procedures before surgery.

### Gallbladder disease

**Mechanism:** Two things combine. Rapid weight loss of any kind makes bile more concentrated with cholesterol, which favors gallstones. GLP-1 drugs also appear to reduce how strongly the gallbladder contracts, so bile sits longer. Gallstones and gallbladder inflammation show up at higher rates in trials.

### Pancreatitis

**Mechanism:** Not fully settled. One route is indirect: gallstones can block the pancreatic duct and trigger pancreatitis. Another proposed route is direct stimulation of GLP-1 receptors on pancreatic cells. Large trials and meta-analyses have produced mixed results, and the absolute risk appears low, but pancreatitis remains a labeled warning and the drugs are generally avoided in people with a history of it.

### Sudden vision loss (NAION)

**What it is:** Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, sometimes called an "eye stroke." Blood flow to the front of the optic nerve drops, causing sudden, usually painless and often permanent vision loss in one eye.

**Status:** In 2025, the European Medicines Agency concluded that NAION is a very rare side effect of semaglutide, affecting up to about 1 in 10,000 users, and required it on the label. US labels mention vision changes in general terms.

**Mechanism:** Unknown. Leading hypotheses include:
- **Rapid blood sugar drops** changing blood flow to the optic nerve. A similar phenomenon is well known with diabetic retinopathy, which can temporarily worsen when blood sugar falls quickly. The SUSTAIN-6 trial of semaglutide saw more retinopathy complications for this reason.
- **Changes in blood pressure or fluid balance**, especially low blood pressure at night, which is a known NAION risk factor.
- **Direct effects** through GLP-1 receptors in the eye and optic nerve, which have been found in animal studies.

### Muscle and lean mass loss

**What critics say:** A large share of the weight lost isn't fat. In Novo Nordisk's STEP 1 trial, roughly 40% of the weight lost was lean mass. Critics worry this leaves people, especially older adults, weaker and more frail, and that it could lower metabolism and make regain more likely.

**Mechanism:** As far as anyone knows, the drugs do not attack muscle directly. The loss comes from eating far less, often including too little protein, combined with the fact that the body sheds some muscle whenever it loses a lot of weight, since a lighter body needs less muscle to carry it.

**The debate:** Researchers who defend the drugs point out that "lean mass" on a DXA scan includes water, organs and connective tissue, not just muscle. Some newer studies using MRI or direct strength testing found modest muscle loss without meaningful loss of strength, along with less fat stored inside the muscle. Critics respond that the long-term studies on frailty, falls and fractures have not been done.

### Weight regain after stopping

**Mechanism:** The drugs suppress appetite while you take them but do not appear to permanently reset the body's weight-regulating system. When the drug stops, appetite returns, and in the STEP 1 extension study most participants regained around two-thirds of the weight they had lost within a year. If muscle was lost on the way down and fat is regained on the way back up, some critics argue a person could end up with worse body composition than when they started. This "regain cycle" is the factual backbone of the lifelong-customer argument.

### Thyroid tumors

**What's on the label:** A boxed warning, the FDA's strongest, about medullary thyroid cancer, based on rodent studies. The drugs are not used in people with a personal or family history of that cancer or of the MEN2 syndrome.

**Mechanism:** Rodent thyroid C-cells carry many GLP-1 receptors, and constant stimulation drove them to multiply and form tumors. Human C-cells have far fewer of these receptors, which is why most researchers think the risk in people is much lower. Human data so far haven't settled the question, partly because medullary thyroid cancer is rare and slow-growing.

### Mood, pleasure and suicidal thoughts

**What critics say:** Some users report feeling flat or losing interest in things they used to enjoy, not just food. Reports of suicidal thoughts prompted reviews by European and US regulators.

**Mechanism (proposed):** GLP-1 receptors are found in the brain's reward system, including the ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens, which run on dopamine. That's how the drugs reduce food cravings, and it's why they're being studied for alcohol and drug addiction. Critics argue that a drug that turns down the reward signal for food might also turn it down for other pleasures in some people, a state called anhedonia.

**Status:** The regulatory reviews did not find evidence that the drugs cause suicidal thoughts, and several large observational studies found equal or lower rates among users. The anhedonia question has not been well studied.

### Other effects worth knowing

- **"Ozempic face"**: Rapid fat loss in the face can make people look older or gaunt. It reflects fat loss, not a toxic effect.
- **Kidney injury**: Severe vomiting or diarrhea can dehydrate people enough to injure the kidneys.
- **Low blood sugar**: Mainly a risk when combined with insulin or sulfonylurea diabetes drugs.
- **Compounded and counterfeit versions**: During shortages, many people used pharmacy-compounded or online versions, which led to dosing errors and quality problems that critics say the hype helped create.

## Part 4: The "plot" theories

Many critics go beyond side effects and argue the GLP-1 boom reflects something deliberate. Below are the main versions of that argument and who makes them.

### Theory 1: The "sick them, then sell them the cure" model

**Who makes it:** Calley Means and Casey Means, in interviews and in their book *Good Energy*; Robert F. Kennedy Jr.; and many commentators in the "Make America Healthy Again" (MAHA) movement.

**The argument:** The same economic system profits at both ends of the chronic disease epidemic. Food companies engineer cheap, ultra-processed products that drive overeating and metabolic damage. Drug companies then sell expensive treatments for the resulting obesity and diabetes. Hospitals and doctors are paid for treating disease, not preventing it. In the sharper version of the argument, the same large asset managers, such as BlackRock and Vanguard, hold major stakes in both food and drug companies, so the people at the top profit whether Americans get sick or get treated. In this view, GLP-1 drugs are the ultimate product of that system: a pharmaceutical answer to a problem the food industry created, which leaves the food industry untouched. Critics also point out that food companies have begun marketing "GLP-1 friendly" products, which they see as the two industries adapting to each other.

### Theory 2: The lifelong subscription

**Who makes it:** Calley Means most prominently, echoed by Kennedy and others.

**The argument:** Because weight usually returns once people stop the drugs, the companies have created the most valuable product imaginable: one that a huge share of the population could take for the rest of their lives. Critics argue that reframing obesity as a "chronic disease requiring long-term treatment," a framing promoted by drug-funded medical and advocacy groups, was a deliberate step toward that market. They point to efforts to expand approval to teenagers and children as evidence that the goal is to start customers young. Means has described putting the country on these drugs for life as the opposite of what anyone would design if the goal were actually solving obesity.

### Theory 3: Captured institutions

**Who makes it:** Calley Means, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Tucker Carlson, Aseem Malhotra, and investigative journalists writing in *The BMJ*.

**The argument:** Drug-company money has bought the cooperation of the institutions that should be protecting the public. The critics list several channels:

- **Medical societies and guideline panels**, which receive industry funding and whose members often consult for drugmakers. Means has singled out pediatric guidelines that opened the door to prescribing GLP-1 drugs to adolescents.
- **Patient advocacy groups**, some of which are heavily funded by GLP-1 manufacturers and lobby for insurance and Medicare coverage of the drugs.
- **Regulators**, which critics like Kennedy have long described as captured by the industries they oversee, citing industry user fees that fund much of the FDA's drug review work and the revolving door between agency jobs and industry jobs.
- **News media**, which Carlson and Kennedy argue depend on pharmaceutical advertising and therefore avoid hard coverage of the drugs.
- **Influencers and doctors on social media**, some of whom have been paid to promote the drugs.

The BMJ's reporting focused on financial ties between manufacturers and obesity experts. Critics also cite the FDA's own 2026 letters to Novo Nordisk, which said its Ozempic and Wegovy ads overstated benefits and played down risks, as proof the marketing has crossed lines.

### Theory 4: Chemical control of desire

**Who makes it:** Independent commentators, podcasters and writers in online health and medical-freedom circles. It isn't tied to any single well-known figure, and the named critics above haven't made it themselves, though Johann Hari's concerns about the drugs dulling pleasure are often cited in support.

**The argument:** GLP-1 drugs work in part by turning down the brain's dopamine reward system, which is why they reduce cravings for food and are being studied for alcohol, gambling and drug addiction. Proponents of this theory argue that a drug that dampens desire itself, taken by a large share of the population, could make people more passive, less driven and less likely to resist authority. Some describe it as an intended goal; others say it is a side effect the powerful are happy to accept. They point to user reports of emotional flatness and loss of interest in hobbies, sex and socializing, and to the push to expand the drugs to ever more conditions, as signs of where this is heading.

### Theory 5: Depopulation or deliberate harm

**Who makes it:** Independent online commentators and researchers, many of whom were also skeptical of the COVID vaccine rollout. It circulates mainly through social media, independent podcasts and Substack-style newsletters rather than through any single well-known figure, and the named critics above haven't made it themselves.

**The argument:** In its strongest form, this theory holds that GLP-1 drugs are part of a broader plan to weaken or reduce the population. Its proponents point to the side effects, including gastrointestinal damage, vision loss, muscle wasting and reports of suicidal thoughts, as harms that are being knowingly tolerated. They argue that the speed of the rollout, the aggressive marketing, the expansion to children, and the involvement of large global foundations and investment firms fit a pattern of elites experimenting on the public.

## Part 5: The other side

Most obesity specialists, diabetes researchers and medical organizations defend the drugs strongly. Their main points:

- **Obesity is itself dangerous.** It raises the risk of heart disease, diabetes, many cancers, joint disease and early death. The relevant question is not whether the drugs are risk-free but whether they're safer than staying obese.
- **The heart data are strong.** In the SELECT trial, semaglutide cut major cardiovascular events by about 20% in people with obesity and heart disease. Benefits for kidney disease and sleep apnea have also been shown.
- **Diet and exercise alone rarely work long-term** for people with severe obesity. Defenders see the "just eat better" argument as ignoring decades of evidence that most people regain weight after diets, and as blaming patients for a condition with strong biological drivers.
- **The serious side effects are uncommon.** NAION is estimated at about 1 in 10,000. Severe gastroparesis and pancreatitis are also rare relative to the number of users.
- **Muscle loss can be managed** with resistance training and enough protein, and newer studies suggest strength is largely preserved.

Where both sides largely agree: the drugs are powerful, long-term data are limited, the benefits are clearest for people with significant obesity or diabetes, the case is much weaker for people taking them for cosmetic weight loss, and anyone on them should lift weights, eat enough protein, and take sudden vision changes or severe abdominal pain seriously.
