# Des Moines, 1941: Lindbergh's Case in His Own Words and His Defenders'

## Twenty-five minutes in Des Moines

On the night of September 11, 1941, eight thousand people packed the Des Moines Coliseum for an America First rally. Millions more listened on the radio. The star was Charles Lindbergh, the most famous man in America.

The timing was not an accident of history, his defenders point out. That same evening, Franklin Roosevelt went on the air with a fireside chat announcing his "shoot on sight" order against German submarines, an act of war in all but name, issued without a vote of Congress. The rally was delayed so the crowd could hear the president first. Then Lindbergh stepped up to answer the question in his speech's title: "Who Are the War Agitators?"

For two years he had talked vaguely about "powerful elements" pushing the country toward war. This time he named names. "The three most important groups who have been pressing this country toward war," he said, "are the British, the Jewish and the Roosevelt administration." Behind them, of lesser importance, he listed capitalists, Anglophiles, intellectuals, and Communists.

The reaction was a firestorm. Roosevelt's press secretary, Stephen Early, said Lindbergh sounded like Berlin. Wendell Willkie, the Republican who had run against Roosevelt the year before, called it the most un-American talk made in his time by a person of national reputation. *The Des Moines Register* ran a front-page cartoon of Hitler and Mussolini cheering. Newspapers that had opposed intervention turned on him.

Lindbergh refused to back down. In his diary he wrote that he had worded the address carefully and moderately. To him, and to the writers who have defended him since, he had done something simple and dangerous: he told the truth about who wanted war, in public, to people who were not supposed to hear it.

## The hero they had to destroy

To understand why Des Moines mattered, his defenders say, you have to understand who was speaking.

In May 1927, a 25-year-old airmail pilot from Little Falls, Minnesota, flew alone from New York to Paris in 33 and a half hours. Few Americans have ever been worshipped the way Lindbergh was after that flight. Four million people turned out for his parade in New York. He was the clean-cut Midwestern hero, son of a congressman who had himself voted against entering World War I.

Then the fame turned on him. In March 1932 his 20-month-old son was kidnapped from the nursery of the family home in New Jersey and found dead weeks later. The press turned the family's grief into a circus. Photographers broke into the morgue to shoot pictures of the baby's body. After the trial, with reporters hounding the family and threats against their second son, the Lindberghs fled the country in December 1935 and settled in England. Lindbergh's hatred of the press was earned, and the press knew it.

From Europe came the episode his enemies would use for the rest of his life. Between 1936 and 1938 Lindbergh made several trips to Germany. His defenders, including historian Wayne Cole, stress why: he went at the request of Major Truman Smith, the U.S. military attaché in Berlin, who needed someone the Germans would show their air force to. Lindbergh toured Luftwaffe factories and airfields and fed what he saw back to American military intelligence. His reports helped wake the U.S. Army Air Corps up to how far behind it had fallen.

In October 1938, at a dinner at the American embassy in Berlin hosted by the U.S. ambassador, Hermann Göring pinned the Service Cross of the German Eagle on him without warning. Lindbergh didn't ask for it, and refusing it on the spot in an embassy would have been a diplomatic incident. His defenders point out that Henry Ford and others received the same decoration. But the medal became the photograph his critics never stopped running. When Lindbergh came home in 1939 and began warning Americans against war with Germany, he was the most trusted voice in the country. That, his defenders argue, is exactly why the interventionists had to break him.

## The British: the charge that turned out true

Lindbergh's first named group was the one that drew the least outrage, and his defenders argue it is the one history has most clearly proved right.

In Des Moines he said the British had every reason to want America in the war. Britain could not win alone, he argued, and it was spending heavily on propaganda in the United States to drag Americans in. He said he didn't blame them. Any nation fighting for its life would do the same. But Americans needed to know it was happening.

What he couldn't know was how far it went. In 1940, Winston Churchill sent a Canadian millionaire named William Stephenson to New York to run **British Security Coordination**, a covert intelligence operation headquartered on the 36th floor of Rockefeller Center. At its peak it ran hundreds, by some accounts thousands, of agents and contacts in the United States. Its mission, laid out in the BSC's own secret history, written in 1945 and finally published in 1998, was to bring America into the war.

Historian Thomas Mahl, in *Desperate Deception* (1998), documented what that meant in practice:

- **Planted news.** BSC fed stories to friendly American columnists and wire services, and ran its own news agency to place pro-British material in U.S. papers.
- **Rigged polls.** It worked with pollsters to shape and publicize opinion surveys showing rising support for intervention.
- **Front groups.** It helped fund and organize American interventionist groups, such as Fight for Freedom, to look like a homegrown movement.
- **Dirty tricks against America First.** Its agents disrupted rallies, and it targeted noninterventionist politicians, including Congressman Hamilton Fish, for exposure and ruin.
- **The forged map.** On October 27, 1941, Roosevelt told the nation he had a secret Nazi map carving Latin America into German-controlled states. Mahl and other researchers trace the map to BSC forgers.

To Lindbergh's defenders, this is the heart of the matter. A foreign government ran a secret influence campaign on American soil to push the country into war. Lindbergh said so out loud, and he was called un-American for it.

## The Roosevelt administration: the state against its critics

Lindbergh's third group, he said in Des Moines, was the administration itself. It had promised peace and was delivering war step by step, and it was using the war emergency to pile up power the Constitution never gave it. Every step, he argued, was taken without a declaration of war, so the American people never got the vote they were owed.

His defenders say the administration proved his point by how it treated him.

- **The Copperhead smear.** At an April 1941 press conference, Roosevelt compared Lindbergh to Clement Vallandigham, the Ohio congressman the Lincoln administration had arrested and banished for opposing the Civil War, the original "Copperhead." The president of the United States had publicly labeled the country's greatest hero a traitor. Lindbergh resigned his commission as a colonel in the Army Air Corps Reserve days later. He wrote that he could not serve a commander in chief who had questioned his loyalty.
- **The attack dog.** Interior Secretary Harold Ickes made Lindbergh a personal project. In speech after speech he called him the "Knight of the German Eagle" and the No. 1 Nazi fellow traveler in America.
- **The FBI.** Roosevelt asked J. Edgar Hoover to look into his critics. He forwarded the FBI names of Americans who had sent telegrams opposing his defense policies. The Bureau investigated America First, its leaders, and its money, and kept files on Lindbergh. As Bill Kauffman and other America First historians tell it, the government of the United States was spying on the largest peace movement in its history.

The punishment didn't end with the war's start. After Pearl Harbor, Lindbergh offered to rejoin the Army Air Forces. The administration refused to take him back. When aircraft companies tried to hire him, the White House leaned on them. He finally got war work with Henry Ford, who didn't care what Washington thought.

## The Jewish groups: the three paragraphs

This is the part of the speech that sank it. It ran three paragraphs. Lindbergh's defenders, from America First historian Wayne Cole to antiwar writers like Justin Raimondo and Bill Kauffman, say those paragraphs have been quoted in pieces for eighty years, and they ask you to read them whole.

Lindbergh began with sympathy. It was not difficult, he said, to understand why Jewish people wanted the overthrow of Nazi Germany. No person with a sense of the dignity of mankind, he said, could condone the persecution of Jews in Germany. He said he was not attacking the Jewish people or the British people, and that he admired both. His argument was about their *leaders*, who, he said, for reasons understandable from their own viewpoint but not American ones, wanted to involve the country in war.

Then came the warning. "Instead of agitating for war," he said, "the Jewish groups in this country should be opposing it in every possible way, for they will be among the first to feel its consequences." War, he argued, would bring intolerance at home. "Tolerance is a virtue that depends upon peace and strength." His defenders read this as a man warning a minority that war fever would turn on them, as it had turned on German-Americans in 1917.

And then the line his critics have hung around his neck ever since. Lindbergh said the greatest danger from these groups lay in their "large ownership and influence in our motion pictures, our press, our radio and our government."

His defenders make several arguments about that line:

- **He was naming a lobby, not a race.** Lindbergh treated Jewish organizations as one interest group among several, the same way he treated the British government and the administration. Raimondo argued that saying an organized group advocates a policy is ordinary political speech, and that singling out this one group as untouchable is its own double standard.
- **Hollywood was openly interventionist.** Two days before Des Moines, a Senate subcommittee led by Senators Gerald Nye and Bennett Clark opened hearings on war propaganda in the movies. Studio films like *Confessions of a Nazi Spy* and *Sergeant York* pushed the public toward war. Harry Warner of Warner Bros. told the senators plainly that he opposed Hitler and was proud of his films. Defenders say Lindbergh was describing what studio heads themselves were saying in public.
- **His wife's verdict.** Anne Morrow Lindbergh read the draft and begged him not to give it, predicting he would be branded an antisemite. Yet in her diary she insisted her husband was not one. To his defenders, that's the testimony of the person who knew him best.
- **His own words, unchanged.** Lindbergh never retracted. He maintained to the end that he had said nothing false and nothing hateful, only something that was forbidden to say.

## The smear that outlived the war

To Lindbergh's defenders, the real story of Des Moines isn't what he said. It's what was done with it.

For two years, the interventionists had failed to beat America First on the argument. The public still opposed war by wide margins. Des Moines handed them a weapon. Overnight the debate stopped being about whether America should fight Germany and became about whether the man leading the opposition was a bigot. Raimondo called it the template for every war debate since: you don't answer the antiwar side, you discredit it.

The hypocrisy, his defenders say, was thick. Wendell Willkie called Lindbergh's speech un-American. Months earlier, Willkie had told a Senate committee that his own antiwar pledges in the 1940 campaign were "a bit of campaign oratory." Roosevelt, who had promised the mothers of America that their sons would not fight foreign wars, called Lindbergh the Copperhead.

America First didn't abandon him. The national committee met in Chicago and issued a statement standing by its spokesman. It denied the charge of antisemitism and accused the interventionists of using it to distract from the real issue: whether the president was taking the country into an undeclared war behind Congress's back.

None of it mattered. The label stuck to Lindbergh, to America First, and to the very phrase. Bill Kauffman, in *America First!* (1995), argued that a broad coalition of farmers, students, socialists, pacifists, and Midwestern conservatives was rewritten in memory as a gang of Nazi sympathizers, and that the slur has been aimed at every American who opposed a war ever since, from Vietnam to Iraq.

## What he did next

Three months after Des Moines, Japan bombed Pearl Harbor. America First dissolved within days and told its members to support the war. Lindbergh did too. His defenders point to what came next as the answer to everyone who called him a traitor.

Shut out of the uniform, Lindbergh went to the Pacific in 1944 as a civilian technical adviser. He flew about fifty combat missions with Marine and Army squadrons, as a civilian who could have been executed as a spy if captured. He dive-bombed Japanese positions and, in July 1944, shot down a Japanese fighter. He taught P-38 pilots how to stretch their fuel, adding hundreds of miles to their range and saving American lives.

In 1945 he went to defeated Germany and walked through the underground slave-labor complex at Nordhausen, where prisoners had been worked to death building rockets. His wartime journals record his horror at what he saw.

In 1954 President Eisenhower restored his commission and made him a brigadier general in the Air Force Reserve. That year his book *The Spirit of St. Louis* won the Pulitzer Prize. He spent his last years campaigning for wildlife and wilderness, and died on Maui in 1974.

To Raimondo, Kauffman, and the antiwar writers who still defend him, Lindbergh is the patron saint of a lost cause: a man who told the country it was being maneuvered into war by a foreign government, a president who lied, and the interest groups behind them. The war came anyway, and the one thing most Americans remember about him is the sentence they were told to hate.
