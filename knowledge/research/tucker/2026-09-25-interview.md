# Peace Cannot Be Pro-Russian

Here's something that doesn't make sense to me.

A Ukrainian woman, a self-described patriot, someone who has openly called Putin's actions crimes against humanity, gets invited to speak at the European Parliament about the need for peace in Ukraine. Three days after the speech is announced, her own government sanctions her. When she shows up in Brussels, she's turned away at the door.

She wasn't there to defend Russia. She was there to say that Ukrainians are dying and it should stop.

That woman is Yulia Mendel, who served as Volodymyr Zelensky's press secretary. She recently sat down with Tucker Carlson for the second time this year, and what she described is worth thinking through, because it cuts against almost everything we've been told about this war and about who gets to speak for Ukraine.

## What Happened After the First Interview

Mendel's first interview with Carlson apparently didn't go over well in Kyiv. According to her, Zelensky ordered a so-called "linguistic expertise" on what she said. She describes this as a corrupt practice the Ukrainian government uses to build a case for whatever it already wants to prove. That review is still ongoing. If it concludes the way she expects, she says she faces treason charges, which under martial law can mean fifteen years to life in prison.

Then came the European Parliament invitation. Alternative for Germany (AfD) invited her to speak about the necessity of peace. The speech was announced around September 10th. On September 13th, Zelensky imposed sanctions on her.

When she arrived on September 16th, she was barred from entering. She says they were told the order came personally from Roberta Metsola, the President of the European Parliament. There were also attempts to block the video transmission, and her speech wasn't allowed to be translated into German or French. AfD managed to get the video feed working anyway, and she answered questions.

Afterward, according to Mendel, Metsola's chief of staff went to apologize to the AfD organizer, explaining that they had been told she was going to spread Russian propaganda. Some Ukrainians were saying it was Zelensky himself who asked for the ban.

So think about the sequence here. A Ukrainian government sanctions a Ukrainian citizen for planning to talk about peace. The institution that likes to call itself the heart of European democracy then refuses to let her speak, apparently on the word of that same government.

## What Sanctions Actually Mean

"Sanctions" sounds abstract until you hear what it means in practice. Mendel says she can't make any financial transactions in Ukraine. No buying or selling property or cars. Her personal and business accounts have been cancelled. She can't have a bank account at all.

The part that stood out to me more was this: the sanctions also targeted her Facebook, Instagram, and X accounts, with a request that they be blocked for people inside Ukraine.

She says this isn't unique to her. She knows bloggers who keep having to restart their pages because they call for peace, and then the Ministry of Culture asks for their accounts to be blocked for Ukrainian audiences. Her point is that for Ukrainians right now, social media is basically the only place where people can openly discuss what peace might look like, what they could accept, and what postwar Ukraine could be.

What struck her most was a question she kept hearing from Western journalists: why don't Ukrainians talk about peace? One journalist told her it felt like nobody in Ukraine except Yulia Mendel wanted it.

She used to think the problem was that Ukrainians didn't have the contacts or skills to reach bigger platforms. Now she thinks it's simpler than that:

> "But it happens that there is just a censorship, a huge censorship. Nobody wants to listen to Ukrainians who call for peace."

That's an interesting feedback loop if it's true. The voices get suppressed, and then the absence of those voices becomes evidence that nobody holds that view.

## The Math

Mendel returns several times to a phrase borrowed from J.D. Vance's New York Times essay from a couple of years ago: the math in Ukraine doesn't add up. Her argument is that it still doesn't.

She cites a claim from Poland's prime minister, who said his Ukrainian counterparts told him Ukraine loses about 27,000 soldiers killed and wounded every month. Add roughly 3,000 civilian casualties per month (she specifies these are only UN-verified numbers) and you get around 30,000 Ukrainians dead or wounded monthly.

Then the population numbers. By her count, Ukraine has a maximum of about four million men of conscription age. Russia has somewhere around 38 to 42 million. Roughly ten to one.

The country had around 52 million people at independence in 1991. She says it now has a little more than 20 million people still living in it, and around 12 million of those are pensioners and people with disabilities. Take out the children and there's almost no workforce left. Last year there were three times more deaths than births. In the first half of this year, four times more.

Her conclusion is blunt:

> "The war of attrition with Russia is nothing but suicidal policy, suicidal war."

And she flips the usual argument around. The standard Western position is that Ukraine has to keep fighting to stay independent and sovereign. Mendel argues the opposite. She says Ukraine is on an Afghanistan path, and that continuing the war makes it more likely to end up a vassal state with a degraded society and economy. Whereas if Ukraine finds a realistic path to peace now, restores its economy, holds elections, brings people home, then maybe, with good diplomats and time, it could even get territory back.

> "Right now, we're choosing between the territories and people and we are losing both. But most importantly, we lose our people."

## The Streets

This is the part of the interview that's hardest to square with the image of a unified nation fighting for its survival.

Mendel says that, according to ombudsman data, only two to three percent of men who are forcibly conscripted actually end up fighting on the front. The rest pay bribes. Her team's conservative estimate is that bribes in the forced mobilization system could reach $5 billion a year. Her reasoning is that corruption on that scale can't just be happening in the middle or lower levels. It has to be covered from the top.

She also describes torture and beatings in conscription offices, and men dying there. Women physically defending men on the street. Men sometimes using weapons against the state trying to drag them to the front. And men who haven't left their homes in weeks, months, even years, so they won't be grabbed off the street.

When Carlson asked about Western calls to start drafting Ukrainian women, her answer was that fighting isn't popular for anyone, men or women. She thinks that if the government pushes into mobilizing women or lowering the conscription age further, there could be a revolution.

And then this:

> "I cannot imagine how anyone could say this is Russian propaganda. This is everywhere."

## No Elections

Zelensky's term has run past its expiration, and he's now been in power, as she puts it, for seven and a half years. Mendel claims that around June or July, Zelensky considered holding elections, but not free ones. According to her, he approached the former commander-in-chief and another figure with some public standing and asked them not to run, but to back him as the single candidate of an alliance. At least one of them refused.

Her view is that no matter how the election is run, Zelensky can't win. So instead the public keeps hearing reasons why elections are impossible. Too many people out of the country. Soldiers can't vote from the front. Too much shelling.

Her explanation for why he wouldn't want the war to end is about incentives, not ideology. Ending the war would be political suicide. The war, she says, gives him money, power, red carpets, and fame.

She also claims, and says it's public information in Ukraine, that before he became president, Zelensky was invited to the front in Donbas four times and declined each time. Now he's sending men there by force. "He's very far from Churchill that he is compared to," she says.

## Follow the Money

This is where the interview connects to something I keep coming back to, which is how institutional incentives can explain behavior better than official narratives do.

Carlson asked why so many European leaders, Merz, Starmer, Macron, keep talking about joining the war. Mendel's answer was to look at where the aid money actually goes.

Say Ukraine is promised 45 billion euros a year. According to her, something like 30 billion of that goes to drone and weapons production, and most of it doesn't go to Ukraine at all. It goes to the producers in the countries distributing the money. She names Rheinmetall and French manufacturers as examples.

Then she describes a media pattern, and this is where her background as a PR person matters. Before the NATO summit in Ankara in July, she says, the messaging was that Ukraine was winning. Mark Rutte announced 140 billion euros in aid. Within a week the "Ukraine is winning" stories stopped. Within two weeks the stories were that Ukraine was losing, had no interceptors, and was facing a humanitarian catastrophe. By the end of summer, the headlines were about Russia preparing to attack NATO countries, even while NATO officials were saying they saw no signs of it.

She points out how strange that is. A country that, in this telling, can't even handle Ukraine is supposedly about to attack NATO.

Her theory is that all of this lines up with negotiations over the EU's next seven-year budget, 2028 to 2034, where defense spending is "very tasty." Escalating the rhetoric justifies the budgets.

I'm not saying this proves anything. But the pattern she describes, where the narrative shifts to whatever unlocks the next tranche of funding, isn't a new idea. It rhymes with a lot of wars. The people making the weapons rarely have the least influence over whether the war continues.

She also says the escalation Zelensky pushed for over the summer made things much worse on the ground, while American outlets were reporting that Ukraine was winning. By her account, July saw a record for civilian deaths and injuries since May 2022. More than 90% of warehouses holding everyday goods, food, clothing, electronics, were destroyed. Gas stations, energy infrastructure. She expects a humanitarian catastrophe this winter and more refugees.

## The Corruption Question

When Carlson asked whether Zelensky and his family have gotten rich from the war, she said she'd stick to facts, and then pointed to a company called Fire Point. She says Kyiv Independent reported that a long-term business partner of Zelensky's is behind it, and that wiretaps released by Ukraine's anti-corruption bodies captured conversations between the defense minister and that partner about billions of euros going to Fire Point.

She also brought up Andriy Yermak, Zelensky's former chief of staff, who she says has been accused of $10 million in money laundering to build four luxury mansions in the Kyiv region. Three belong to people close to the Zelensky family. The fourth, she says, belongs to someone called "Vova," the diminutive form of Volodymyr. Most people, according to her, have drawn their own conclusions about whose house that is.

The detail she found most disgusting was the timing. Construction had stopped when the full-scale invasion began, and then restarted in March 2022, while Ukrainians were sheltering from missiles and fleeing their homes.

To be fair to what she actually said, she was careful here. She said she doesn't know whether Zelensky personally benefits, pretends not to know, or doesn't know, and that a lot will have to be learned about his bank accounts after the war. But she clearly believes the corruption goes very high.

Her answer to Carlson's final question, why Zelensky keeps escalating attacks inside Russia, was the darkest part of the interview. "This is how he gets money," she said. Burning Russia makes for videos and photos, and those bring in money. Meanwhile, she claims, 93% of Ukraine's shelters can't protect anyone, and the only thing authorities offer people in frontline regions is to leave. No financial aid, no housing. Just leave.

> "The money that Western people are sending, that Western taxes, they don't help Ukrainians today... What they help is to produce more weapons."

## Who Gets to Be a Patriot?

What I keep coming back to is the framing. In the Western conversation, supporting Ukraine has come to mean supporting the war indefinitely. Anyone who questions that is quickly labeled pro-Russian.

Mendel is trying to break that equation. She tells a story about pitching a peace op-ed to a well-known American outlet. Nothing attacking Zelensky, just a Gallup poll showing most Ukrainians wanted peace and an argument for a ceasefire. Rejected, no explanation. Later, the same outlet ran a piece from a London professor arguing about how much Russia was suffering. She looked him up. He had never been to Russia or Ukraine.

Her conclusion is a pretty devastating one:

> "In the west many more people want Russia burn than Ukraine to be saved."

And her line about the professor who encouraged an American student to go fight, then told the shocked student that everyone makes history in their own place, lands with the question Carlson asked right after. How many of the people pushing this war are risking their own lives for it?

The most recent Gallup poll she cites shows 66% of Ukrainians want peace as soon as possible. Every peace deal gets worse, she says, because every month there's less territory, fewer people, and more debt. External debt is already around 110% of GDP, while Russia, by her account, hasn't even needed to borrow for the war.

## What I'm Still Wondering

I don't know how much of what Mendel says will hold up in every detail. Some of it is clearly her interpretation, some is insider claims, some is numbers from people she trusts. But the core of her argument doesn't depend on any single allegation. It depends on arithmetic, demographics, and incentives, and those are hard to argue with.

What bothers me is how the whole thing is structured. A government that can't win an election suspends elections. Critics get sanctioned and labeled propagandists. The European Parliament takes that label at face value. Weapons makers get paid. The media narrative swings to whatever the next funding round requires. And the people actually dying don't get a microphone.

If a country's own former press secretary can't say "we should stop the war before there's no country left" without being treated as an enemy agent, then who exactly is the war being fought for?

Her line from the interview is probably the best summary of the whole thing:

> "This is very patriotic to call for peace. It's not Russian propaganda. Peace cannot be pro-Russian."
