***Covid Era Stories #xx — by Paul E. Ramirez***

*#2xx, date 2026*

My Covid story started in late February of 2020. That was the time when Covid was really becoming mainstream on TV and it happened to be around the time Kobe Bryant died in his tragic helicopter crash. Also, it was around the time when my Mom passed away. My mom had been battling health issues, including a lifetime of smoking, so it wasn't a huge surprise to anyone. I consider that period as the "BC" times—*Before Covid*. It was a different life for me back then, when worrying about what to cook for my mom was the thing that stressed me out each evening. *[Maybe provide another detail about how your life was different, a little more context (even though you do return to this point—it would be more powerful than just saying that it was.]*

My "Where were you when Covid struck?" story begins in the San Francisco area, where I was visiting my good friend Laura. Laura (a code name) was completing her fellowship to become a radiologist. She and I had been friends for a while, and sometimes more than that. While I was there, California declared a state of emergency due to Covid, and everything changed.

The flight home was surreal. SFO airport was practically empty. The TSA were happy to see me, which is just bizarre in itself (seriously), grateful that they still had jobs, I guess. I wasn't sure whether my flight would even take off, everything seemed so uncertain, and I wondered if I would be stuck in California. But I did make it home to Madison, Wisconsin and, to my surprise, everything seemed normal here. People were doing their usual gatherings, living their normal lives. I found myself in a new type of culture shock, due to the stark contrast between lock-down California and still-free Madison.

Because I had traveled from California, the CDC recommended I self-quarantine for two weeks, which I did. My supervisor dropped off my work laptop. I'm a software developer for University of Wisconsin and, under normal circumstances I would bike commute to work every workday. I'd been doing that since 2012. So life changed for me. I went from spending each evening cooking dinner for my mom, and biking 3 miles back and forth to work each day, to being alone at home every day and trying to figure out what was going on in the world.

Like many people, I felt energized by Covid, and wanted to do my part any way that I could. I was quickly drawn to the likes of Darkhorse podcast. It was my belief that the best way I could contribute was to help relieve psychological and emotional stuff that others were going through, by encouraging them to take part in outdoor activities, like camping, or going on bike adventures. And boy did I! For a while, that is what I did.

*[Maybe provide an example or three, short, they can be vague, of what you did—an overnight camping trip with three friends from college, a 40 mile bike ride with a friend from work, that sort of thing.]*

In mid 2021, after finishing her radiology fellowship, Laura moved back to Madison and became my roommate. She participated in all of the biking and camping adventures. But even though we took tandem bike rides almost every evening after work, we grew apart. I believe it had to do with ideological reasons.

Ideological differences became harder to ignore in the rest of my friend group as well. The real fracture began in earnest after Biden took office. There seemed to be a bloodlust in the air among my friends. Discourse and nuance seemed to be out the window. By the next Summer, when the Covid shots were widely available, Laura was incredibly mad at me for being skeptical of mRNA shots, never mind the Ivermectin pills I took. She took my skepticism personally. It was as if I was undermining her medical authorit, during a time when she was pressuring everyone else to get the shots.

But yeah, before Laura tapped out, and decided to move out, but after she had gotten a couple rounds of mRNA shots *[true?]*, she had begun to complain about unusually heavy menstrual bleeding. Because I was familiar with literature from dissident doctors like Pierre Kory and Peter McCullough, I knew that was the issue that women were reporting the most, post-shot, in VAERS. I tried to communicate this to Laura. But she would have nothing to do with anything I had to say.

Laura moved out and got a fancy pants position as lead radiologist at a hospital outside of town, not far from where I lived. It is what it is! I'm happy for her. I didn't hold any grudges and wrote her cards and letters regularly. We stayed in touch. At some point *[can you pinpoint the year and season?]* we ordered tickets to an X Ambassador concert, intending to go together.

When the weekend of the concert arrived, though, Laura told me she couldn't get out of her bed, because her heart rate was going "insane". Weeks later, she reported that she couldn't walk up a flight of stairs without her heart rate spiking, and almost passing out each time. At the time, I was incredibly worried. In retrospect, she had myocarditis, right? I tried to ask her about it, but she would not answer me. She always changed the topic. Given our history, how much she had come to resent my views, I decided to let it go. We still talk, though. It was less than a year ago, xxx years after those first shots, when she was once again able to complete a 5k run.

The way I look at things is that I'm lucky. Despite whatever hardships I might perceive, I'm far better off than many. It's easy to focus on our faults—even easier to focus on the faults of others. It's harder to focus on solutions, but that's what I'm trying to do now.
