# Tea Boxes, Monkeys, and a Baby That Isn't "Late"

Some of the most interesting livestreams are the ones where nothing goes according to plan. The internet keeps dropping, the photos someone meant to show get wiped by accident, and the host is exhausted after a day hiking in the mountains. What you end up with is less a presentation and more a guy thinking out loud about whatever happened that day.

This one was recorded in rural Shizuoka Prefecture, Japan, the night before his wife Masako's expected delivery date for their first child. Short stream, early night, because the plan was a "family night" of talking to the baby and letting it know they were ready whenever it was. But packed into those 20-odd minutes were a handful of ideas worth pulling apart.

## Why is a baby "due"?

The part I found most interesting was his objection to a single word.

> "Why is the baby due? ... They should say expected. That's more accurate."

His argument is that "due" frames birth like a bill or a library book. If the baby hasn't arrived by the date, it's *late*, and once it's late, something has to be *done*. He sees the word as quietly setting up an intervention before anyone has actually decided one is needed. In his words, it's "more of the brainwashing so they can say oh the baby was due and now we have to do something."

His proposed fix is actually pretty practical. Don't pinpoint a date, bracket it. A range still lets people plan, but it doesn't turn day 281 into a problem.

What strengthens his point is something their midwife, Mayuka-san, told them: first babies often come later. So if that pattern is known, he asks, why isn't the date adjusted for it? Why build in a "late" that's partly an artifact of how the date was calculated in the first place?

There's a nice irony here too. The Japanese term, *shussan yoteibi*, literally means something like "scheduled" or "expected" birth date. And the official English term in medicine is technically "estimated due date." Only a small fraction of babies arrive on the exact day. So the language people actually use has drifted toward something more certain than the underlying reality supports. Whether that drift is deliberate, as he suspects, or just the way language gets lazy, is an open question. But the effect he's describing, where a date becomes a deadline, seems real either way.

## Buy once, cry once

He then took a detour into something he's clearly been noticing for a while: stuff is getting hard to get.

A 9-inch Lodge skillet he ordered came back out of stock. He says a lot of things are like that now, and what is available is often subject to "product fade," the slow decline in quality where the same product with the same name just isn't as good as it used to be.

His advice was blunt. If you need something, whether it's shoes, shirts, or even petroleum-based things like ziplock bags, get it now.

He paired that with a philosophy he lives by: buy quality the first time. He held up a shirt he's worn something like a thousand times with no holes in it. He used the camera world as an example, where people buy a starter camera, then a mid-range one, then finally the good one, when jumping straight to the good one would have cost less overall. He also mentioned a saying he'd heard, something to the effect of being too poor to buy cheap things.

That idea has a long history. Cheap goods that fail are a hidden tax on people who can't afford to replace them.

## The tea boxes

This is where the day got interesting.

In a small town out in the mountains, a woman was selling old tea boxes, the big wooden kind used in the tea trade to store and keep leaves fresh. He says they still smell like tea when you open them. The price was 2,000 yen, roughly $13. He had Masako double-check that the price was right, bought one on the spot, and asked if there were more.

There were. The shopkeeper called a local tea farmer, and suddenly they were at his farm, getting a tour of the whole process, from tea in the ground to vacuum-packed product. They bought a bunch of boxes in different sizes, which he's delivering.

His reasoning was all about food storage. These are made to keep things like tea and mushrooms fresh, somewhat insulated, and good-looking enough that they double as furniture. He compared them to an ice chest of similar size, which he guessed would cost around $200.

These boxes are called *chabako* in Japan, and they have a long history of use beyond tea. Families have traditionally used them to store kimono and other things that need protection from moisture and bugs. So he wasn't just improvising a new use; he stumbled onto a very old Japanese storage solution that most people outside the tea regions probably don't know exists.

Worth noting the contrast he drew himself: in a world where he can't get a skillet, he found something of much higher quality for almost nothing, simply by being out in the countryside and asking questions.

## Why doesn't anything eat the tea?

The tea farmer also grows shiitake mushrooms, in reinforced black structures rather than greenhouses. Those reinforcements exist because monkeys break in to eat the mushrooms. Rabbits too, and he thinks deer.

So he asked the obvious question: does anything eat the tea?

According to the farmer, no. The tea just grows in the open, surrounded by hungry animals that ignore it.

> "Are humans the only ones that eat the tea? That would be noteworthy."

He flagged it as a different subject for another day, but it's a genuinely good question. One likely piece of the answer is caffeine, which plants appear to produce partly as a defense chemical, since it's bitter and toxic to many creatures. That raises a stranger question: why are humans so drawn to a substance that plants seem to have made to keep things away?

There was also a small moment on the mushroom logs that I liked. Some black mushrooms were growing alongside the shiitake, and when he asked what they were, the farmer said he didn't know, and didn't know if they were edible. The logs come from his own forest, and whatever lives in that wood comes along for the ride. Even a professional grower is only in partial control of what shows up.

## Midwives and hospitals

He closed with the strongest statement of the night, and the one he clearly feels most personally. His takeaway from nine months of pregnancy is to find a good midwife, and avoid the hospital route, which he called "extremely dangerous."

He said he felt like he'd missed something important in life by not understanding how vital midwives are until now. Mayuka-san, who spent the day with them and suggested the steep hiking and the hot springs to help labor along, has around 24 years of experience, part of it in a hospital before starting her own practice. According to him, she said she has never seen postpartum depression in a mother who went the midwife route, and that hospital deliveries are where she sees it. He connected this to cases in Japan of mothers taking their own lives, and to his broader distrust of vaccines, which he refers to as "death jabs."

His conclusion was simple: "Our bodies were made by God. They are incredible... don't mess with what's working."

The midwife's observation is striking, and it would be worth hearing her full reasoning when she comes back on for the follow-up series he mentioned. One question I'd want her to address: how much of the difference comes from the care itself, and how much from who ends up in each setting? Midwives typically take lower-risk pregnancies, while hospitals get the complicated ones, so the two groups start in different places. That doesn't dismiss what she's seen over 24 years, but it's the first thing I'd want to untangle if I were digging into it.

## What ties it together

On the surface, this stream was scattered: a train, bear warnings, a skillet, tea boxes, mushrooms, a baby. But there's a common thread. In almost every story, he's contrasting the official, standardized version of something (the due date, the mass-produced product, the hospital protocol) with the older, local, hands-on version (the bracketed expectation, the $13 wooden tea box, the midwife walking you up a mountain).

He doesn't always have the full answer to why the modern version seems worse. But he keeps noticing that it often is, and that the better thing tends to be sitting right there if you go out and ask.

Whether the baby arrived the next day, he didn't know. "Maybe we'll have a baby tomorrow. I don't know. Maybe not."

Which, when you think about it, was the whole point about the due date.
