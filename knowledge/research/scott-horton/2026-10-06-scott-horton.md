# No Evidence, Endless War

### Ted Snyder on the "Russia will attack NATO" narrative and why Ukraine's position keeps getting worse

*Adapted from an interview on The Scott Horton Show with Ted Snyder, fellow at the Libertarian Institute and contributor to The American Conservative and Responsible Statecraft.*

---

## Overview

For months, European leaders and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky have warned that Russia is preparing to attack a NATO country. In this conversation, Scott Horton asks Ted Snyder whether there's anything to it. Snyder's answer is blunt: from an intelligence standpoint, there is no evidence of a planned Russian conventional attack on Europe, and the narrative serves a clear political purpose — keeping Europe financially and militarily committed to a war Ukraine is losing.

From there, the discussion ranges across Russia's actual war aims, the role of ultranationalist formations in Ukraine's military, the corruption scandals stalling European aid, the human cost of conscription, and what a post-war Ukraine might look like.

---

## 1. The "Russia Is Coming for NATO" Claim

Snyder points to Zelensky's recent address at the UN General Assembly, where he made two claims: that Russia is going to invade a European or NATO country, and that there is intelligence evidence to support this. Snyder calls the second claim flatly false.

According to Snyder:

- **The Baltic states' own intelligence services** — Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia, the countries supposedly next in line — say they have no evidence of an imminent conventional attack.
- **NATO's senior leadership**, including the top U.S. commander for NATO in Europe, has likewise said there is no such evidence.
- Some **Baltic leaders have actually pushed back** on the rhetoric, calling it dangerous, even as Poland and France have amplified it.

What the Baltic states *are* worried about, Snyder says, is something different: sabotage, cyberattacks, arson and other low-level "gray zone" activity. That concern grows as Ukraine moves drone production into European countries, since Russia has consistently targeted Ukrainian drone manufacturing with missile strikes.

### The drone and missile incursions

Asked about reports of drones and missiles straying into NATO territory, Snyder says they are genuinely hard to interpret. Any given object could be:

- a Ukrainian drone that went off course (Ukrainian drones cross Baltic airspace on their way to Russian targets),
- a Russian drone or missile that went off course,
- a Russian munition diverted by Ukrainian electronic jamming, or
- an unarmed Russian warning shot.

European leaders, he notes, mostly frame the danger as an *accidental* strike, not a deliberate attack.

### The dog that isn't barking

Horton adds a point of his own: in November 2021, when U.S. intelligence believed Russia was preparing to invade Ukraine, the evidence was splashed across the front page of the Washington Post. If the CIA genuinely believed Russia was about to attack NATO now, he argues, that would be front-page news regardless of the Trump administration's preferences. Instead, Washington — "the world's biggest loudmouth guard dog" — is silent.

### Why the narrative exists

Snyder's explanation is that Ukraine must continually generate reasons for Europe to stay in the war. The previous rationale, that Ukraine had "turned the tide," collapsed after Zelensky's long-range strike campaign against Russia prompted heavier Russian retaliation against Ukrainian infrastructure. The fallback message, he says, is simple: *if you don't help Ukraine, you're next.*

### Why it doesn't make sense

Snyder offers two reasons a Russian attack on NATO is implausible:

1. **Capacity.** Russia has been fighting in Ukraine for four and a half years. Without a major new mobilization, it lacks the manpower to open a second front against a European country.
2. **Motive.** Russia stated from the outset that a central reason for the war was to *prevent* a war with NATO — specifically, to stop Ukraine from becoming a NATO member on its border. Using the war as a springboard to fight NATO would contradict the war's own stated purpose.

## 2. What Russia Is Actually Fighting For

Snyder argues the West consistently mismeasures the war by tracking territory gained or lost. By that metric Russia is advancing only slowly. But, he contends, territory was never Russia's primary goal. Its core demands were:

1. **A legally binding commitment that Ukraine will never join NATO** — which Snyder notes echoes the neutrality Ukraine declared when it became independent.
2. **Protection for the rights of ethnic Russians and Russian speakers** in Ukraine.

Before the war, the second goal might have meant keeping the Donbas inside Ukraine with some autonomy. That option, Snyder says, is now gone; the likely outcome is recognition of Donbas independence or its absorption into Russia.

As evidence that Russia did not originally seek territory, Snyder points to 2014, when Putin had parliamentary authorization to use force, had the military ability to take the Donbas, and could have recognized the region's autonomy referendums — and did none of these, even discouraging the separatists.

### Horton's counterpoint

Horton pushes back partially. Once the war began, he argues, territorial objectives did become important: the land bridge from Donetsk to Crimea along the Sea of Azov, and restoring Crimea's fresh water supply. After Ukraine's successful counteroffensives in Kharkiv and Kherson in September 2022, Putin mobilized 300,000 additional troops and formally annexed Zaporizhzhia and Kherson oblasts — even though Russia controls only part of each. Since that annexation is now enshrined in Russian law, Horton suggests, years of fighting could still lie ahead.

Snyder agrees that territorial goals expanded as the war went on — particularly as Ukraine walked away from early peace talks and began striking Russian oil and civilian sites — and that Russia now seeks a larger buffer zone. But he maintains the bottom line is security guarantees, not land.

### Winning in other ways

Measured by those terms, Snyder argues Russia is winning decisively. Ukraine is:

- running out of soldiers at the front and losing population overall,
- watching its economy shrink as plants close and exports collapse,
- seeing its bridges, roads, railways, energy grid and even data centers destroyed,
- facing local food shortages.

Every intervention to keep Ukraine fighting, Snyder says, only prolongs an inevitable outcome and makes it worse. "It's not only time to settle Ukraine," he says, "it's time to apologize to Ukraine."

## 3. Zelensky's Dilemma and the Nationalist Factor

Both men argue that Zelensky is trapped. He sustained Ukrainian morale with promises of recovering all lost territory and joining NATO. Neither is happening, and Snyder suggests that simply signing a settlement now could put Zelensky's life at risk.

Horton names the danger directly: far-right figures such as Dmytro Yarosh and Andriy Biletsky, who he argues could turn on Zelensky if he negotiated a deal. Rather than confronting those elements, Snyder says, Zelensky has increasingly leaned on them. When Ukraine reports battlefield gains, Snyder notes, they are frequently attributed to the Third Army Corps commanded by Biletsky, a formation with roots in the original Azov movement.

Horton adds a claim he says he has read but cannot currently source: that the Third Army Corps operates with a separate chain of command and supply lines directly linked to U.S. forces in Germany, because American officials see it as effective and less corrupt. Snyder says he had not heard this. *(This claim is unverified.)*

Snyder also cautions that this unit's battlefield role has limits. Ukraine's front is so thinly manned that moving elite units to plug one gap opens wider gaps elsewhere.

### The rift with Poland

Ukraine's honoring of World War II–era nationalists is causing a serious rift with Poland, one of its most important backers. The Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists and affiliated forces carried out mass killings of Poles in what was then eastern Poland and is now western Ukraine. Horton cites Ukraine's ceremonial reburial of OUN leader Andriy Melnyk, which he says received little Western coverage.

Horton observes that Poland has every strategic reason to overlook the past and help Ukraine resist Russia — which makes Kyiv's insistence on these commemorations all the more self-defeating.

### Canada's blind spot

Snyder, who is Canadian, discusses a piece he wrote for the Libertarian Institute about Canada's relationship with Ukraine. When Prime Minister Mark Carney describes a "hundred-year partnership" based on shared values, Snyder argues, he overlooks a fundamental contradiction: Canada is officially bilingual and pluralist, while Ukrainian policy has suppressed the Russian language and culture. Poland's leadership, he notes, does not see its values as aligned with this aspect of Ukrainian policy either.

## 4. Corruption and the Money Problem

Snyder argues that two issues are keeping Ukraine out of the European Union: its human rights record and endemic corruption.

On corruption, he describes a standoff in which:

- the EU is demanding anti-corruption reforms before releasing further funds,
- members of Zelensky's own party are refusing to vote for those reforms, in Snyder's telling because they benefit from the status quo,
- Ukraine is therefore heading toward bankruptcy without fixing the problem that would unlock relief.

Snyder says Europe recently provided Ukraine roughly $90 billion, only for Kyiv to request about $27 billion more — prompting European officials to ask where the first sum went. He describes a pattern of military contracts awarded to companies that deliver nothing, then awarded again.

Horton raises the firing of Ukraine's defense minister Mykhailo Fedorov, who was praised for building Ukraine's drone force but, according to reports Horton has seen, spent a year's military budget in half a year. Both men say they cannot confirm that this was the actual reason for his dismissal.

### The economics of the drone war

Snyder argues the cost-benefit balance of the air war is turning sharply against Ukraine:

- As Russian air defenses adapt, Ukraine must launch ever-larger and more expensive drone swarms to achieve fewer hits.
- Russia relies on comparatively cheap weapons, including jet-powered Geran drones and inexpensive "Banderol" cruise missiles, and with Ukraine's air defenses depleted, these increasingly reach their targets.

Meanwhile, after Ukraine began attacking Russian shipping, Russian retaliation against Odesa has, by some estimates Snyder cites, closed ports, blocked roughly 70 percent of grain exports, shut down steel and mining operations, and cost Ukraine around $70 million per day.

## 5. The Human Cost

Both men dwell on the scale of losses. Snyder cites figures from Ukrainian sources:

- Poland's president said Ukrainian intelligence put Ukrainian losses at about **27,000 per month**.
- Kyrylo Budanov, head of Ukrainian military intelligence, reportedly said Ukraine must recruit about **30,000 per month** just to hold even.

Snyder clarifies these figures refer to total casualties — dead plus those too badly wounded to return — not deaths alone. Sustained over a year, that pace would mean several hundred thousand people removed from the front. He also cites estimates of roughly 2 million Ukrainians evading the draft and around 200,000 soldiers who have abandoned their posts, and says male life expectancy in Ukraine has fallen to about 57.

Horton describes the widely circulated videos of "busification" — men seized off the street by recruitment officers and forced into vans — and argues that the war is less a national volunteer effort than a conscription machine. Snyder agrees that Ukrainians did volunteer en masse at the war's start, but says that is no longer true.

Horton also notes that millions of Ukrainians have fled abroad, many permanently, in a country that already had one of Europe's lowest birth rates. He speculates that a generation of young ultranationalist fighters indoctrinated in the post-Maidan era has likely already been largely killed at the front.

### Fighting for a right Ukraine was willing to give up

Snyder returns to a point he considers central: in the first negotiations after the 2022 invasion, Ukraine was prepared to give up NATO membership. Western governments encouraged it to keep fighting anyway, framing the war as a matter of principle. He calls a U.S. State Department statement from the early days of the war — that the conflict was "bigger than Russia" and "bigger than Ukraine," concerning nations' right to choose their alliances — "the most horrible quote from the war."

He adds that polling in government-controlled Ukraine shows only around 60 percent support for NATO membership even now.

Horton notes that NATO's "open door" doesn't actually give countries a right to join; it gives NATO a right to invite them. In practice, he argues, the phrase mainly means no outside country's objections will be considered. He also points out that President Biden later said he was the one who had decided Ukraine could never join NATO — yet his administration refused to put that in writing when Russia demanded it in late 2021, telling Moscow it wasn't even on the table.

### No military way out

Horton compares Ukraine's position to South Vietnam and Afghanistan: Western support cannot overcome an enemy's overwhelming power on the ground. The only thing that might, direct U.S. military intervention, risks nuclear war — which is why both Biden and Trump have ruled it out. Snyder notes Russia's nuclear doctrine is explicit on this point.

## 6. What Happens After the War?

### Snyder's thesis: Ukraine may turn east

Snyder discusses an argument he credits to historian Geoffrey Roberts of University College Cork: that a post-war Ukraine may end up tilting back toward Russia. His reasoning:

- After the war, Ukraine may find itself told it cannot join NATO and cannot join the EU — effectively abandoned by the West it fought for.
- With nowhere else to go, it might turn to the one neighbor offering an open door, where familial, cultural, religious and linguistic ties remain.

Snyder says this depends on two things. If the war drags on and Ukraine is devastated, hatred of Russia will become entrenched. But if there is a settlement that leaves Ukraine sovereign, with security guarantees, and Russia plays a leading role in reconstruction, a gradual rapprochement becomes possible. It would take time, he stresses.

### Horton's counter-thesis: the trap deepens

Horton offers the opposite scenario. The more territory Russia takes, the more of Ukraine's pro-Russian population becomes part of Russia. What remains of Ukraine becomes proportionally more hostile and more nationalist. Russia might then argue it must protect Russian speakers in oblasts like Sumy and Dnipropetrovsk east of the Dnipro, pulling it toward the river — a long process.

Even then, Horton argues, a rump western Ukraine run by hardline nationalists, with forests, swamps and the Carpathian Mountains, and NATO neighbors willing to arm an insurgency, would present Russia with an Afghanistan-style problem. He notes this "insurgency" scenario was reportedly part of Western planning at the start of the war. Russia, in his view, may have painted itself into a corner, reinforcing a mistake in the way American planners often have.

Snyder partly agrees, noting Ukraine had an internal conflict before Russia's invasion and could face civil war or nationalist rule afterward. But he argues Russia can likely live with that outcome so long as Ukraine stays out of NATO, which was its core fear all along.

### The role of the ultranationalists

Horton mentions the assassination of Andriy Parubiy, a former parliamentary speaker and prominent nationalist figure, shot in Lviv by an attacker reportedly connected to a fallen soldier. He also notes analyst Gordon Hahn has identified Biletsky as the figure most likely to attempt a coup.

Snyder says these groups enjoy only low levels of popular support but wield outsized influence because of their military capability, their radicalism and Zelensky's dependence on them. They will likely remain a real force after the war, and could obstruct its end.

## Key Takeaways

- **No intelligence basis for an imminent Russian attack on NATO**, according to Snyder, who says Baltic intelligence services and NATO leadership have said as much. European concern centers on sabotage and cyberattacks.
- **The narrative is politically useful** for keeping European aid flowing after the "turning the tide" story collapsed.
- **Russia's core aims are Ukrainian neutrality and protections for Russian speakers**, not maximum territory — though both men acknowledge territorial goals expanded during the war.
- **Ukraine faces mounting crises**: manpower losses, draft evasion, infrastructure destruction, an export collapse, and a corruption standoff blocking EU funds.
- **Reliance on ultranationalist units** is straining relations with Poland and complicating Ukraine's EU bid.
- **The post-war future is uncertain**: Ukraine could drift back toward Russia if abandoned by the West, or a hostile rump state could saddle Russia with a long-running insurgency.
