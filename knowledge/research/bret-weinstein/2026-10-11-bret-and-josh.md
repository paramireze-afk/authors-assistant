# Beyond Flock: Toka, Maven, and the Fight Over Who Controls the Cameras

Over the past year, Flock Safety's network of license plate reader cameras has become a lightning rod. Communities across the United States have argued about thousands of cameras feeding a database that police can search retroactively, nationwide. But on a recent episode of the *DarkHorse* podcast, evolutionary biologist Bret Weinstein and writer Josh Walkos argued that Flock is the least of it. In their view, the public is fixated on a license plate reader while far more powerful tools are being built and sold to governments with almost no public debate.

This article summarizes their conversation: the technologies they discussed, the concerns they raised, and what they think should be done.

## From Flock to Toka

Walkos says the Flock controversy is what led him down the rabbit hole. If one company had quietly built a searchable national camera network, he reasoned, others were likely doing similar things. A TechCrunch article pointed him to **Toka**, and what he read there, he says, "blew my mind."

According to Walkos, Toka was founded in 2018 and co-founded by former Israeli Prime Minister **Ehud Barak** alongside a former Israel Defense Forces officer. (Press reports identify the co-founder as Yaron Rosen, former head of the IDF's cyber command.) Walkos says the company has since relocated its operations from Israel to Maryland. Its early backers included the venture capital firm **Andreessen Horowitz**, which Walkos says invested $12.5 million in 2018.

The capabilities described in Toka's own materials, and in a 2023 *Haaretz* investigation based on a company pitch deck, are what alarm both men. As they describe it, Toka claims it can:

- locate internet-connected cameras and other "Internet of Things" devices,
- gain access to those networks,
- view live feeds,
- disable cameras, and
- alter video feeds, including in real time, without leaving a forensic trace the camera's owner could detect.

Both speakers are careful to say they cannot verify that Toka actually does what it says. But Walkos argues that a firm like Andreessen Horowitz would not put eight figures into a company it didn't believe could deliver. Weinstein adds that the capabilities are not a leak or a rumor: they are claims the company itself makes about a product it sells. Toka says it sells only to governments and law enforcement, not private citizens, and that its clients include U.S. government agencies, though it does not name them.

## The End of Video Evidence?

For Weinstein, the central problem is simple. If a tool exists that can undetectably alter surveillance video, then video evidence no longer means what we think it means.

He sees two possible outcomes, both bad. Courts could recognize that video can no longer be trusted, in which case any defendant convicted mainly on video evidence has a ready-made challenge. Or, more likely in his view, courts will carry on as if the technology doesn't exist, because no one can produce evidence that it was used in a particular case.

He illustrates the trap with a hypothetical. A defense team realizes that, without the video, the prosecution can't prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. They ask to introduce Toka's own marketing as evidence that such footage can be fabricated. The judge asks for proof it was used here. There is none, because the technology is designed to leave none, and contracts with the company are not disclosed. The argument fails.

"Do you want to live in a world," Weinstein asks, where whoever controls government power can "put an individual in jail... by constructing evidence for a crime that they did not commit?" The same tool, he notes, can work in reverse: removing someone from footage could exonerate the guilty.

Walkos adds that ordinary avenues of accountability don't reach this. Agencies don't disclose that they've contracted with the company, so there is nothing obvious to request through FOIA, and courts have no way to reverse-engineer whether footage was altered.

## High-Profile Cases, Read Differently

The two men raise several recent events in which video played a central role and ask how differently they would read if such a tool had been available to someone at the time:

- the January 6 pipe bomb investigation,
- Jeffrey Epstein's death in jail, where cameras were reportedly not recording (Walkos refers to "a couple minutes missing"),
- the assassination of Charlie Kirk, for which Tyler Robinson has been charged, and
- the attempted assassination of Donald Trump in Butler, Pennsylvania.

Both frame this explicitly as a thought experiment. Walkos says the Utah officials presenting campus footage in the Kirk case "could be presenting this in good faith," and the point is that no one, including investigators, would necessarily know if footage had been tampered with upstream. Weinstein goes further, saying he finds it hard to understand how the Robinson case is being discussed without any mention of Toka. Walkos says he does not believe the official account of the Kirk assassination.

Weinstein's broader point is that this matters even if every one of those investigations was handled perfectly. A free society, he argues, cannot rely on electing only good people. It must be designed so that bad people who gain power don't get unlimited power. A tool that lets its holder convict anyone or clear anyone gives them "the cryptic powers of an emperor."

## The Epstein Connection

Walkos points to Ehud Barak's well-documented relationship with Jeffrey Epstein and says an article reported that Barak ran the Toka idea by Epstein, either seeking investment or advice. Barak's business dealings with Epstein have been reported elsewhere; *Haaretz* has reported that Epstein financed much of Barak's investment in an earlier security startup, Reporty Homeland Security.

Weinstein connects this to a darker scenario. If you can fabricate or erase evidence at will, you don't need people to actually commit crimes to control them. You can simply show them a preview of what a jury might see. The worst kind of totalitarian regime, he says, is one that locks up the people who disagree with it while everyone else falls in line to keep the liberty they still have.

## Why Is No One Talking About It?

Both men are puzzled by the silence. Flock has generated hundreds of conversations; Toka, almost none. Weinstein says that when he mentioned the company on Julian Dorey's podcast, the host had never heard of it and looked it up live. Walkos notes his original thread on X drew only about 5,000 views until Weinstein amplified it, and he suspects the algorithm was suppressing it.

Weinstein acknowledges that journalist Whitney Webb had been writing about Toka for years, though he says he has reservations about her work and finds her prone to connecting dots that don't connect.

Walkos's explanation is a kind of "willful amnesia." People don't want to believe it, he says, so they avoid the cognitive dissonance. He compares the public's relationship with government to that of an abused spouse who keeps believing the next time will be different, and points to MKUltra as an example of abuses that were once dismissed and later confirmed. He says the original *Haaretz* article has been "basically scrubbed" and had to be retrieved from an archive.

## Fake Cell Towers and Spy SUVs: A "Privatized Panopticon"

Walkos describes the broader picture as a "privatized panopticon." Government surveillance tools aren't new, he notes; WikiLeaks' Vault 7 release exposed CIA cyber tools, including ones designed to obscure or falsely attribute the source of a hack. What's new, he argues, is the government buying from private companies capabilities it may not legally be able to build or use directly, and treating the purchase as if it sidesteps Fourth Amendment protections.

He names another Israeli firm, **Cognyte**, sometimes described as "the Palantir of Israel." According to Walkos, Cognyte sells:

- backpack-sized devices that act as fake cell towers, which could be carried into a crowded venue to access nearby phones, and
- a product he calls **FalcoNet**, SUVs outfitted as mobile surveillance units that perform the same function over a radius and are being sold to police departments around the country.

Walkos admits he isn't an expert on the technical side. He also suspects, though he says the companies don't state it, that systems like Flock, Toka, and Cognyte's products can share data or interoperate when sold to the same agencies.

The result, in his words, is the Fourth Amendment "being liquidated" without anyone voting on it. Weinstein says he is encouraged that many people are reacting strongly to Flock cameras going up around them, which suggests there is still resistance to build on.

### Worse than a panopticon

Weinstein pushes back on the word "panopticon," arguing the reality is worse in two ways. First, in Jeremy Bentham's panopticon you never knew *when* you were being watched; in this system you effectively always are. Second, the panopticon only observed. It did not fabricate. A tool that can alter footage can tell any story, from "entirely innocent" to "entirely guilty," about anyone.

## Maven and the OODA Loop

The conversation then turns to the **Maven Smart System**, an AI platform used by the U.S. Department of Defense and associated with Palantir. Walkos describes it as a large AI system used in DoD operations, including, he believes, in Ukraine.

Weinstein explains it through the military concept of the **OODA loop**: observe, orient, decide, act. (The concept comes from U.S. Air Force strategist John Boyd.) Two fighter pilots in a dogfight: the one who cycles through that loop faster usually wins. Maven, as Weinstein understands it, tightens that loop by fusing many streams of data into one dashboard. Point at a vehicle and the owner's identity, travel history, and other details appear. The end purpose is military targeting.

On a real battlefield against a legitimate enemy, Weinstein says, he wants the U.S. to have this advantage. The problem is that the system doesn't care what it's pointed at, and there's no technical line between a battlefield and a city. Who decides, he asks, what counts as "terrorism" serious enough to justify using it at home? Walkos cites the scale of the January 6 investigation, in which people were located simply for having been on the premises, as an example of how broadly such tools can be applied.

## Clean Hands, Distant Wars

Walkos argues that AI gives decision-makers a way to escape moral responsibility: the system made the call. Weinstein agrees and places it in a long historical arc. Close combat made people reluctant to fight. Each technology that added distance (firearms, long-range weapons, drones piloted through VR headsets, robotic soldiers) made killing more impersonal. Autonomous AI targeting, he says, is the final step, where someone can "sip your wine and feel like your hands are clean" while people die thousands of miles away.

His argument is that the grief of soldiers coming home in caskets, terrible as it is, has historically acted as a brake on war. Remove it, and conflict becomes a game of *Risk*. He warns that the indifference powerful people show toward foreign victims is the same indifference they may show toward their own citizens.

## Experimenting Without Consent: The PolyHeme Story

Walkos uses the story of **PolyHeme** to argue that governments have long been willing to override consent at home, not just abroad.

PolyHeme was an experimental blood substitute developed by **Northfield Laboratories**, the kind of product the military has long wanted for battlefield use. It was tested in trauma patients under the FDA's **exception from informed consent** rule, which allows certain emergency research on patients who can't consent because their lives are at risk.

As Walkos describes it:

- The rule requires "community consultation," including public meetings, before a trial begins in a city. Few people attend or even hear about them.
- People who don't want to be enrolled can request an opt-out bracelet telling paramedics not to use the product.
- In several U.S. cities, people injured in car crashes and other trauma were given PolyHeme in ambulances, and the product continued to be given in the hospital, where standard blood would have been available.
- Walkos says the product was linked to adverse effects including cardiac events and that "a bunch of people died." Senator Chuck Grassley raised concerns about the trial.
- PolyHeme never came to market, but the consent exception remains in use today.

Walkos asks listeners to imagine it was their own child, and to consider that this was all legal.

### The Hitchhiker's Guide to consent

Weinstein compares this to the opening of Douglas Adams's *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*, where Arthur Dent's house is to be bulldozed for a bypass and the notice was technically "on display" in a place no one would find it. Moments later, the Vogons destroy Earth for a hyperspace bypass, telling the protesting humans the plans had been on file at a faraway planetary office for years.

The trick is the same, he says: create a mechanism that lets you claim people were informed, then treat their silence as consent. He points out that after World War II, seven defendants in the Nuremberg Doctors' Trial were hanged for violating informed consent, and asks why no one now seems worried about consequences. He also notes the dark irony of the wristband: a powerful person who knew about the trial could simply wear one.

Both men list other historical examples: the Tuskegee syphilis study, CIA LSD experiments on unwitting subjects, and Cold War radiation experiments. They also include COVID-era policies in this list.

Weinstein draws a moral comparison. A parent has a recognized right to use deadly force against someone threatening their child. He stresses he is not advocating violence, but asks why a bureaucrat who knowingly approves a risk that might stop that child's heart is treated as innocent simply because the harm is hidden. Walkos calls it what Hannah Arendt called "the banality of evil."

## Faking CCTV Is a Question of Price

Weinstein then steps back to reframe the whole debate. Arguing about whether people in power *would* abuse these tools is a waste of breath, he says. History shows that some people will do monstrous things whenever they can get away with it. The only real questions are whether the technology makes it possible and whether protections are strong enough to stop it.

To show how plausible fake surveillance footage is, he points to his iPhone, which can turn any photo into a 3D image by inventing what's hidden behind the subject, using a fraction of the phone's processing power. CCTV footage is low quality. Placing a figure of a certain height, wearing certain clothes, running at a certain pace, is a much smaller task. With serious computing resources and high enough stakes, he says, it's a matter of price, not possibility. The only surprising part of Toka's claim is doing it in real time without a trace, and if that isn't possible now, he expects it soon will be.

Walkos agrees that AI video has crossed the uncanny valley. He mentions nearly being fooled by an AI-generated video of a mountainside temple, and recalls an AI-generated image of an explosion near the Pentagon that briefly moved markets.

## When AI Takes the Jobs

Weinstein believes AI will cause job losses unlike any previous technological disruption. The usual reassurance, that new jobs nobody can yet imagine will appear, doesn't hold, he argues, because those jobs would need to be something humans do better than machines. Humanoid robots combined with superhuman AI, he says, is "game over."

That leaves the very wealthy facing a large population with nothing to do and growing reasons to be angry. Promises like universal basic income, he says, are a "low-quality fantasy" that wouldn't work. He expects living standards to fall, people to resist, and the surveillance infrastructure now being built to look very much like preparation for that moment. If so, he says, "the time to fight is now."

Walkos points to billionaires buying bunkers in New Zealand as a sign that they see trouble coming. He notes Flock is moving into police drones and "real-time crime centers," and predicts that weaponized drones are only a matter of time. He also mentions Axon as one more company offering similar products, and says that whenever one company is exposed, another steps in.

## Fight It at the Level of Rights

Weinstein's main prescription is to fight this at the level of rights, not technology by technology. New tools will keep appearing faster than the public can learn about them, and if public awareness only has to lag behind deployment for rights to be lost, he says, "we can't live that way."

He proposes clear principles instead:

- Military technology may be needed on real battlefields, but it must never be used against American citizens or citizens of the West.
- The public's right to know whether surveillance video has been altered outweighs any government's right to buy the ability to alter it.
- The right of informed consent should be backed by the most serious consequences.

Walkos issues an open invitation to Toka to send a representative on *DarkHorse* to explain what its technology does, who uses it, and what protects Americans from its misuse. Weinstein adds that anyone building these tools who doesn't respect Western values shouldn't be operating inside the U.S. government's orbit.

## Five Revolutions in One Lifetime

Weinstein closes with an evolutionary argument. Humans evolved to handle perhaps one major upheaval in a lifetime; the world you grow up in is supposed to resemble the one you live in as an adult. He says he has lived through four revolutions (the home computer, the internet, the smartphone, and social media) and is now watching a fifth: AI.

That pace, he argues, is part of why older generations are struggling to recognize, for example, AI-generated video. He claims former Congressman Thomas Massie lost his seat partly because older voters believed a fake AI video involving Ilhan Omar and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. Weinstein calls for younger generations to take power from the baby boomers, who he says are steering the country into danger.

Walkos is more skeptical that voting can accomplish this, describing himself as "a little bit more black-pilled." Weinstein, who says he too has been disappointed by Trump's second term, argues voting is still the first and cheapest tool available. He points to forced conscription in Ukraine, changes to the U.S. conscription system, closer U.S.–Israeli military integration, and the war with Iran, and warns Generation Z that they are "in the bullseye": the young men who would be sent to fight.

Walkos, who has two young children, says that is why he is speaking out. People his and Weinstein's age, he says, can act as "Paul Reveres," giving younger people the historical context (such as memories of 9/11 and its aftermath) they lack.
