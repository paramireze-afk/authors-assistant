# H.R. 1776: The Case Against Lend-Lease, From Those Who Fought It

## A weapon, in more ways than one

Franklin Roosevelt sold Lend-Lease with a garden hose. If your neighbor's house is on fire, he told reporters in December 1940, you don't haggle over the price of the hose. You lend it, and he gives it back when the fire is out. The "Arsenal of Democracy" would keep America out of the war by arming those who were fighting it.

His critics called this the biggest lie of the prewar years. To them, Lend-Lease was a weapon several times over:

- **A weapon of war.** It made the United States an open supplier of one side in a world war, and everyone on both sides knew where that led.
- **A weapon of presidential power.** It handed one man the authority to arm any nation he chose, with almost no limit and no further vote of Congress.
- **A weapon against an ally.** British critics later argued Washington used Britain's desperation to strip it of its gold, its bases, and its empire.
- **A weapon handed to Stalin.** Cold War critics argued that billions in aid helped the Soviet Union seize half of Europe.

What follows is their case, in their words where possible.

## The bill numbered 1776

The bill landed in the House in January 1941 with a number its sponsors chose on purpose: H.R. 1776. Its critics saw nothing patriotic in it. Senator Burton K. Wheeler of Montana, a progressive Democrat who had once been Roosevelt's ally, said the number was the only American thing about it.

What alarmed them most was Section 3. It let the president manufacture or acquire any "defense article" and sell, transfer, exchange, lease, or lend it to "the government of any country whose defense the President deems vital to the defense of the United States," on whatever terms he thought satisfactory. There was no dollar limit in the original bill and no list of countries. The president decided who got what, how much, and what America got back.

Historian Charles Beard, testifying before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee in February 1941, put it bluntly. He told the senators the bill would place the wealth and resources of the United States at the free disposal of the president. It would let him wage undeclared war anywhere he chose. Beard called it a bill for the suspension of Congress's war power.

The New York *Daily News* and the *Chicago Tribune* called it the "dictator bill." Opponents across the spectrum, from conservative Republicans to Norman Thomas's Socialists, saw the same thing. A president who had just won a third term on a promise of peace was asking for powers no peacetime president had ever held.

## "Plow under every fourth American boy"

No critic hit harder than Wheeler. In a radio address in January 1941 he compared the bill to the New Deal farm program that had paid farmers to plow under crops. Lend-Lease, he said, was "the New Deal's triple-A foreign policy; it will plow under every fourth American boy."

Roosevelt exploded. He called it the rottenest thing said in public life in his generation. Wheeler didn't take it back. His point, his defenders say, was not rhetoric but arithmetic. A nation that arms one side of a war it claims to be neutral in has already chosen a side. Sooner or later, it would send the men to use the weapons.

Senator Robert A. Taft of Ohio, the leading Republican in the Senate, made the same point with a sharper wit. "Lending war equipment is a good deal like lending chewing gum," he said. "You don't want it back." Taft wasn't against helping Britain. He offered to support a straightforward loan or gift of money. What he opposed was the fiction. If America was going to give away its weapons, he said, call it what it was and let Congress vote on it honestly, rather than dress up a war measure as a rental agreement.

Senator Arthur Vandenberg of Michigan wrote in his diary on the day the bill passed that America had torn up 150 years of traditional foreign policy. He said they had thrown Washington's Farewell Address into the discard and put themselves into the power politics of Europe and Asia.

Congressman Hamilton Fish of New York led the fight in the House. He tried to amend the bill to forbid using the U.S. Navy to escort shipments. Because he knew, as they all did, what came next.

## Lindbergh, Hoover, and the witnesses

The hearings drew the biggest names in the antiwar camp.

**Charles Lindbergh** testified before the House Foreign Affairs Committee in January 1941. He argued that America could not win a war on the European continent, that Britain could not win without an American invasion, and that the bill would draw the country into exactly that. Asked which side he wanted to win, he said he wanted neither, and favored a negotiated peace. His critics never forgave the answer. His defenders say it was the honest answer of a man who had seen the Luftwaffe up close and thought a war to the finish would wreck Europe.

**Herbert Hoover** opposed the bill as written. The former president, who had run relief for starving Europe after World War I, argued that if Congress wanted to help Britain it should vote direct loans of money, under its own control. He warned that the powers in the bill went far beyond aid and amounted to a grant of war-making authority to the president.

**John T. Flynn**, the journalist who chaired the New York chapter of America First, argued that the whole program was a stepping stone. Lend-Lease, he warned, was not a substitute for war but a down payment on it.

Outside the Capitol, women from mothers' groups across the Midwest held vigils and protests against what they called the "kill mothers' sons" bill. Some knelt in prayer in the halls of Congress.

It passed anyway. The Senate voted 60 to 31, and Roosevelt signed it on March 11, 1941. Within hours, the first shipments were authorized.

## Hose, then convoy, then shooting

The critics had laid out the sequence in advance: if you send the goods, you have to make sure they arrive. If you protect the ships, you end up shooting.

That is how it went, they argue, step by step in the eight months after the bill passed:

- **Spring 1941:** Roosevelt extended American naval "neutrality patrols" deep into the Atlantic, reporting German submarine positions to the Royal Navy.
- **July 1941:** U.S. troops occupied Iceland, and the Navy began escorting convoys that far.
- **September 1941:** The destroyer *Greer* trailed a U-boat for hours, radioing its location to British aircraft, until the submarine fired. Roosevelt went on the air, called it piracy, and ordered the Navy to "shoot on sight."
- **October 1941:** The destroyer *Kearny* was torpedoed while escorting a convoy, and 11 American sailors died. Two weeks later the *Reuben James* was sunk, and about 100 more died.

Wheeler, Taft, and Fish said this proved their point. The garden hose had become a shooting war in the North Atlantic, fought without a declaration of war or a vote of Congress. Charles Beard, in his 1948 book *President Roosevelt and the Coming of the War, 1941*, put Lend-Lease at the center of his indictment. It was the moment, he argued, when the administration stopped pretending to be neutral and began fighting an undeclared war.

## The squeeze on Britain

A different set of critics, many of them British, came at Lend-Lease from the other direction. To them, Washington's generosity came with a knife behind its back.

**The gold.** Before Lend-Lease, Britain had to pay cash for every American weapon. By late 1940 it was nearly broke. In January 1941, while the bill was still in Congress, the American cruiser USS *Louisville* docked at Simon's Town in South Africa and loaded about $50 million in British gold for shipment to the United States. Churchill was furious. He compared it, in a letter he never sent, to a sheriff collecting the last assets of a helpless debtor.

**The bases.** In the 1940 destroyers-for-bases deal, Britain handed over 99-year leases on bases from Newfoundland to the Caribbean in exchange for fifty old U.S. destroyers, many of them barely seaworthy. Critics in Britain called it the worst bargain of the war.

**The fine print.** Article VII of the Lend-Lease agreement, which Britain signed in 1942, committed it to dismantle "imperial preference," the system of trade privileges that held the British Empire together economically. Conservative historian John Charmley, in *Churchill: The End of Glory* (1993) and *Churchill's Grand Alliance* (1995), argued that Churchill traded Britain's independence and empire for American support. Washington, he argued, had every intention of replacing Britain as the world's leading power, and Lend-Lease was one of its tools.

**The cutoff.** Six days after Japan surrendered, on August 21, 1945, President Truman ended Lend-Lease without warning. Ships at sea were turned back. Britain, exhausted and in debt, had to send John Maynard Keynes to Washington to beg for a loan. It got one, on hard terms that forced it to make the pound convertible, which nearly wrecked it in 1947. Britain finished paying off that debt in December 2006. Historian Correlli Barnett, in *The Collapse of British Power* (1972) and *The Audit of War* (1986), traced how Britain came out of the war as the world's largest debtor, while its American ally came out as the world's creditor.

## Arming Stalin

The last group of critics came later, in the Cold War. Their charge was that Lend-Lease helped one dictator beat another, then left him strong enough to swallow half of Europe.

The numbers were huge. Of roughly $50 billion in Lend-Lease aid, more than $11 billion went to the Soviet Union. That included around 400,000 trucks and jeeps, some 14,000 aircraft, thousands of tanks, millions of pairs of boots, and enough canned food to feed the Red Army for much of the war. Critics argued that Stalin could not have pushed all the way to Berlin without American trucks. Then, at the end, the Red Army stayed in Warsaw, Budapest, Prague, and East Berlin.

Many recalled what Senator Harry Truman of Missouri said in June 1941, when Germany invaded the Soviet Union. If Germany was winning, he said, America should help Russia, and if Russia was winning it should help Germany, and that way let them kill as many as possible. To critics like Hamilton Fish, the administration didn't follow even that cynical advice. It poured aid into the Soviet Union with almost no conditions, while asking Britain for its gold.

The most explosive charges came from **Major George Racey Jordan**, a Lend-Lease liaison officer at the air base in Great Falls, Montana, the main gateway for planes flown to the Soviet Union through Alaska. In *From Major Jordan's Diaries* (1952) and in testimony to Congress, he claimed he saw suitcases of classified documents pass through to Moscow. He said that among the shipments were uranium compounds and other atomic materials, approved, he said, from Washington. His claims fed directly into the McCarthy-era investigations of Soviet influence in the government. Fish and others argued that Lend-Lease to the Soviet Union had been run by people who cared more about Moscow's interests than America's.

The Soviets never paid most of it back. In 1972 Moscow agreed to pay $722 million to settle the entire Lend-Lease debt. Russia made the final payment in 2006.

## The critics' verdict

Put together, the critics' case runs like this. Lend-Lease was sold as a way to keep America out of the war, and it was the step that put America into it. It was sold as a loan between friends, and it gave one man authority over the wealth and weapons of the nation. It was sold as generosity to Britain, and Britain ended the war broke, in debt, and stripped of its empire. It was sold as a fight for freedom, and its largest single beneficiary outside the British Empire was Joseph Stalin.

Wheeler, looking back in his memoir *Yankee from the West* (1962), saw it as the moment the Republic changed. After H.R. 1776, he argued, presidents would decide when and where America went to war, and Congress would follow. In his view, the country never got that power back.
