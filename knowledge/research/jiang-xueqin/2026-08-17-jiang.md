# The War That Isn't About Land

Jiang opens by telling his audience what kind of argument they're about to get. He isn't a historian delivering peer-reviewed research, he says — he's working from "game theory," meaning the talk should feel intuitive, like a chain of incentives snapping into place, rather than a footnoted dossier. His claim for the evening: the war between the United States, Israel, and Iran cannot be explained as a contest over oil, territory, or regional power. In his view it only makes sense as an *eschatological* war — one being fought, consciously or not, to bring about a religious end-times scenario. He spends the rest of the lecture building toward that claim in stages: first the economy, then the war itself, then a sweeping history of capitalism, secret societies, and messianic religion that he says explains why the war is happening at all.

## Part One: How the World Economy Actually Works (In Jiang's Model)

Jiang starts with a simplified map of the global economy, built around two "pillars": the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) — the oil-rich Gulf Arab states — and East Asia. The GCC sells the world cheap energy. East Asia buys it, turns it into manufactured goods, and sells those goods to the West, which consumes them. Holding the system together is the fact that oil is priced in U.S. dollars, and those dollars flow back into the American financial system once spent — a pattern often called **petrodollar recycling**.

The dollar's role as the world's **reserve currency** is central to his argument, and he treats it as a poisoned gift rather than a privilege, for three reasons.

**1. Loss of financial control.** Because so much world trade runs through dollars, the U.S. must keep dollars flowing freely worldwide — including dollars that never touch American soil. This is the **Eurodollar system**: U.S. dollars held and lent between banks outside the United States, largely outside the Federal Reserve's direct oversight. Jiang's point is that nobody, including the U.S. government, really knows how many dollars exist in this offshore system or how they move — which he argues makes the dollar's global role less a tool of American control than a structure America can't fully see.

**2. Financialization.** To sustain its role at the center of this system, Jiang argues, the U.S. has had to specialize in finance over manufacturing, "hollowing out" its industrial base in favor of speculation. He connects this to inequality by invoking Thomas Piketty's argument, from *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, that returns on capital tend to outpace returns on labor — a stock portfolio might earn 5% a year, in his example, against roughly 2% for running an actual factory. In a financialized economy, he argues, asset owners pull further ahead of wage earners, producing what he calls a "K-shaped economy." He points to the 2008 financial crisis — when the government bailed out banks but not homeowners — as the clearest visible symptom of that imbalance, attributing it to **regulatory capture**, the idea that financial institutions gain enough influence over regulators to shape policy in their own favor.

**3. Pax Americana's military burden.** Jiang argues the system depends on the U.S. military guaranteeing open shipping lanes worldwide — costly, he says, citing a roughly $1.5 trillion Pentagon budget — and, in his view, unnecessary for America's own security, since the U.S. is protected by two oceans and, in his phrase, "a lot of guns." That spending, he argues, is a byproduct of dollar hegemony rather than a genuine defense requirement.

Jiang's overall picture is a system that benefits Wall Street enormously but is, in his words, "not sustainable" — corrupt, unequal, and debt-driven. He argues the Iran war is what happens when that system starts to break.

## Part Two: The Iran War as a Chokepoint Crisis

Jiang describes the original U.S. war aim as **decapitation** — removing Iran's leadership on the assumption the population would rise up and install a friendlier government. In his account this failed because Iran proved more resilient than expected, and the conflict widened.

His central argument is about geography and shipping. Iran, he says, can strike back by threatening the world's energy chokepoints:

- **The Strait of Hormuz**, at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes.
- **The Houthis** in Yemen, who Jiang says threaten the **Bab-el-Mandeb strait**, a chokepoint that matters enormously to Saudi shipping.
- **Hezbollah**, engaged with Israel from Lebanon.
- **Iraq**, which he says is also drawn into the war.

Jiang argues this gives Iran "four points of attack" that together can squeeze the region's ability to move energy to market, and that proposed workarounds like new pipelines don't really solve the problem, since a pipeline is an easier target for a drone than a moving tanker. He also notes a recently signed military alliance between Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan, which he expects Saudi Arabia to eventually use against the Houthis, likely with Israeli backing.

He argues the conflict is built to go global rather than stay regional, because Iran has two backers who can keep supplying it regardless of maritime blockades: Russia, via the Caspian Sea, and China, via Belt and Road rail links. This, in his telling, is the mechanism by which the Iran war and the war in Ukraine could merge into one larger conflict.

## Part Three: The Yen, Liquidity, and an AI Bubble

This section rests on a specific, real piece of financial plumbing: the **Japanese yen carry trade**. The Bank of Japan has kept interest rates near zero for years, partly to fight deflation. Japanese banks lend to Japanese corporations at that near-zero rate; those corporations often take the cheap yen and buy U.S. Treasury bonds instead, which pay a much higher yield (Jiang says around 5%). Multiplied across the Japanese financial system, he estimates this supplies roughly $4 trillion in global liquidity — money that, in his account, helps fund U.S. government debt and the capital spending behind America's AI data-center boom, which he considers a bubble (he claims roughly half of recent U.S. GDP growth traces to it).

Japan, however, imports most of its oil from the Middle East. Jiang argues that as the Iran war disrupts Middle Eastern energy exports, investors grow wary of holding yen, the currency weakens, and the Bank of Japan may be forced to pull that liquidity home to defend its own currency — draining capital that has been propping up U.S. asset markets. Combined with Gulf states and China turning more inward under wartime pressure, he argues this produces a liquidity crunch serious enough to threaten U.S. economic stability.

## Part Four: The "Solution" Nobody Will Take

Jiang offers what he presents half-seriously as a fix. First, give Iran roughly $2 trillion. His model is the **Plaza Accord** of 1985, a real agreement in which Japan — then running large trade surpluses — agreed with the U.S. and other G7 nations to let the yen appreciate, a deal widely credited with contributing to Japan's subsequent decades of stagnation while easing pressure on the U.S. economy. Jiang's proposal is a modern echo: flood Iran with dollars that would eventually recycle back into the global financial system through reconstruction spending. Second: restrain Israel, cutting off weapons and imposing sanctions to force an end to the fighting.

Jiang is explicit that neither will happen — and that gap, between what he says would rationally end the war and what he expects will actually occur, is the pivot of the lecture. If neither self-interest nor conventional strategy explains why the U.S. keeps backing a war that, by his own analysis, is wrecking the economic system it depends on, something other than realism must be driving events. That "something else" is the rest of the talk.

## Part Five: Jiang's Deep History of Capitalism

Jiang's transition is blunt: capitalism, in his view, is "a historical accident" — the byproduct of several unrelated religious and political crises that happened to line up. This section is far more interpretive than the economics above, and Jiang presents it as his own reading rather than settled history.

**Calvinism and "double predestination."** Jiang starts with a real theological doctrine: in certain strands of Calvinist Protestant theology, God is understood to have already decided, before time began, who is saved and who is damned. He frames this, as many historians of religion have, as partly a reaction against the medieval Catholic practice of selling **indulgences**, in which wealthy sinners could pay their way toward salvation. If salvation is predetermined and can't be bought through the church, a new anxiety follows: how does anyone know if they're among the elect? Jiang's answer — echoing Max Weber's *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* — is that worldly success came to be read as a sign of God's favor, while frugality became a virtue. Money that can't be spent has to be reinvested — and that reinvestment impulse, he argues, is one seed of capitalism.

**The Dutch Republic and the Glorious Revolution.** Jiang traces this Calvinist accumulation impulse to the Dutch Republic, which built enormous wealth (he cites its spice trade monopoly) and developed what he calls the world's first real middle class during the Dutch Golden Age. Threatened by the Catholic Habsburg Empire, the Protestant Dutch elite, in his account, effectively merged their financial interests with Protestant England through the real **Glorious Revolution of 1688**, when the Dutch stadtholder William of Orange took the English throne — which Jiang frames as Dutch capital moving to England for protection under the Royal Navy.

**The Bank of England.** From here Jiang moves to a documented institution, the **Bank of England**, chartered in 1694. His emphasis is structural: unlike earlier arrangements where merchants lent directly to kings — risky, since a king could die, lose a war, or refuse to repay — the Bank of England lent to Parliament, effectively to the English nation as an ongoing institution. Jiang argues this durable, nation-backed public debt is what let Britain outlast Napoleon despite losing most individual battles, since it could always borrow to raise fresh coalitions. He treats the Federal Reserve as a direct descendant of this model, and sees Calvinism, the Dutch Republic, the Glorious Revolution, and the Bank of England together as the unplanned building blocks of the modern financial system.

**Philosophy as cover story.** Jiang argues that once these institutions existed, societies needed a philosophy to justify them. He points to the genuine Enlightenment-era separation of "reason" from "emotion," but gives it his own political reading: "reason," in his framing, came to mean the free movement of capital, goods, and people, while "emotion" became a label for whatever obstructs that movement — family loyalty, religious tradition, national identity. He names real philosophical traditions associated with this shift — empiricism (John Locke), skepticism (David Hume), utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham) — as the public-facing justification for the new order.

## Part Six: Freemasonry and the "Secret Layer"

Alongside this public philosophical layer, Jiang argues there was a "secret layer" managing the same project: **Freemasonry**, a real fraternal organization with roots in medieval stonemasons' guilds that evolved, especially from the 18th century, into a network of lodges built around Enlightenment ideals, secrecy, and ritual. He calls it "the religion of reason," citing real Masonic authors — Albert Pike and Manly P. Hall — and their writings' recurring references to a "holy empire of reason," which Jiang reinterprets as an unstated reference to a "holy empire of capital."

He illustrates the practical requirements of transnational coordination with a real, often-repeated (and disputed) historical anecdote: the Rothschild banking family's reputed use of fast couriers to learn the outcome of the 1815 Battle of Waterloo before the news reached London by official channels, allegedly letting them trade on the information first. From this he names three capabilities he says any successful transnational network needs — **information** (learning things before competitors), **infiltration** (placing trusted people inside institutions), and **coordination** (acting across borders in a unified way) — and argues secret societies are simply the most effective vehicles for all three, not because of one grand plan, but because organizations built this way outcompete those that aren't.

He extends this to other transnational networks of the era — Jesuits and what he calls the Jewish diaspora — arguing that when interests aligned, these networks cooperated, and that this convergence is what conspiracy literature has historically labeled the **Illuminati**. Jiang is explicit he doesn't think this was ever one planned organization with a membership roll; his claim is that secret, cross-border coordination confers a structural advantage, and whichever groups had it tended to prosper, whatever they called themselves.

## Part Seven: Why Eschatology Wins — Jiang's Case for Kabbalah and Chabad

This is where Jiang narrows toward his ultimate thesis, and it's the most speculative section of the lecture by a wide margin. His claim is that among competing secret societies, ones organized around an **eschatological** belief — a shared conviction that history is moving toward a scripted religious climax — outcompete purely secular ones, for three reasons he states explicitly: an eschatological project survives any single leader's death because it's a script rather than a personality cult; it can recruit far more people than a project built around personal enrichment; and believers who think they're hastening the end of history work with more intensity and willingness to sacrifice than people working for ordinary gain.

He identifies **Kabbalah** — a real, centuries-old body of Jewish mystical and esoteric interpretation — as the specific tradition he considers most historically potent. In his summary (which compresses a diverse and internally contested scholarly tradition into a single account), Kabbalah holds that God is fundamentally "the will to bestow," generous by nature, and creates a first being — **Adam Kadmon**, the "cosmic man" — to receive that generosity; when that reception fails, the created world fractures, and the purpose of existence becomes repairing the breach and reuniting with God. Jiang argues that some radical factions read this framework literally through the lens of the Bible, treating it as a step-by-step plan: the Jewish people must return to the land of Israel, then build a Third Temple, which will trigger a climactic war Jiang calls **Gog and Magog**, after which God intervenes to save the world. He explicitly frames this belief as confined to a minority within both Judaism and Israeli society — he estimates roughly 40% of Israelis as secular — and argues, in response to audience pushback, that Shia Islam has a comparable eschatology centered on a hidden Imam, and that history, in his account, is generally driven by small, highly committed religious minorities within each nation rather than national majorities.

**Chabad-Lubavitch.** Jiang then turns to a real Hasidic Jewish movement, **Chabad-Lubavitch**, founded in what is now Russia. He notes, accurately, that it was persecuted for much of its history — first under the Russian Tsars, later under the Soviet Union — and argues that its survival and subsequent global growth (he says it is now active in Ukraine and has reported proximity to figures including Vladimir Putin, Donald Trump, and Argentine president Javier Milei, whom he describes as a Chabad member) is treated within the movement itself as a kind of divine vindication. Jiang's own interpretation goes further: he argues Chabad's brand of Kabbalah is specifically messianic, oriented toward hastening the end times, and that this messianic energy — which he explicitly, and provocatively, describes in sexual terms as a drive toward "reunification" with God — helps explain both the movement's intensity and, in his view, the visible intensity of figures like U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth at press briefings about the war, which Jiang reads (his interpretation, offered with a laugh, not a documented claim) as evidence of the same excitement about an approaching end times.

**The Zuckerberg narrative.** As his central worked example, Jiang builds an extended argument around Mark Zuckerberg and the founding of Facebook, which runs as follows:

- *Documented:* Facebook began as a project built on Harvard student data, and its early operation raised real, publicly reported privacy and intellectual-property concerns relative to Harvard's own policies.
- *Documented:* Zuckerberg has publicly described participating in Chabad's Harvard student group ("Harvard Chabad") during his time at the university.
- *Jiang's claim, not independently verified in the lecture:* that fellow students Natalie Portman and Jared Kushner were senior members of Harvard Chabad at the same time, that Alan Dershowitz served as a faculty advisor to the group, and that Larry Summers (Harvard's president during Zuckerberg's time there) was a patron of it.
- *Documented:* Zuckerberg has recounted that his parents, who had three daughters before him and wanted a son, sought a blessing from the Lubavitcher Rebbe (Chabad's late spiritual leader) before he was born.
- *Jiang's interpretation, not a documented claim:* that this story is "coded language" deliberately echoing biblical narratives — Abraham and Sarah's conception of Isaac, and Hannah's conception of the prophet Samuel, whom she dedicated to God's service — and that Zuckerberg's own career (Jiang points specifically to his pursuit of "metaverse" and AI projects) should be read as fulfilling an implied religious role rather than ordinary entrepreneurship.
- *Documented:* Zuckerberg has retained majority voting control over Meta despite not holding majority equity, an unusual corporate structure. *Jiang's inference:* that ordinary venture-capital logic doesn't explain why investors would allow a very young founder that much unchecked control, and that this, too, points to network backing beyond conventional business explanation.
- *Documented:* the 2016 Cambridge Analytica scandal, in which a political consulting firm obtained data from roughly 50 million Facebook users without their knowledge and used it for political micro-targeting; Jared Kushner did lead digital strategy for the 2016 Trump campaign. *Jiang's claim:* that the fact both Zuckerberg (running Facebook) and Kushner (running the campaign's data operation) were, in his account, part of the same Harvard Chabad network is significant and not coincidental. The lecture does not present direct evidence of coordination between the two beyond the shared Harvard Chabad claim and the outcome itself.

Jiang also names **Jeffrey Epstein** as someone he believes was connected to the same Chabad network, offering no supporting detail for that claim in the lecture beyond the assertion itself.

Jiang is explicit that he considers this pattern-matching rather than proof: his repeated framing throughout the lecture is that once you "connect the dots," a story emerges, and he invites the audience to draw their own conclusions. He does not present documentary evidence — internal communications, financial records, or on-the-record confirmation — that any of the named individuals coordinated with one another on the basis of shared religious affiliation; the argument rests entirely on the claimed pattern of overlapping affiliations and Jiang's interpretation of what that pattern implies. (A handful of further contemporary figures were raised as asides during the audience Q&A, including one unverified and unsubstantiated claim about a private individual's fundraising contacts; those asides added no further reasoning to Jiang's core argument and are not reproduced here.)

**Dugin and the katechon.** Jiang closes the section by naming a real contemporary Russian philosopher, **Alexander Dugin**, and his concept of Russia as a **katechon** — a term from Christian theology for a force that "restrains" or delays the arrival of the Antichrist. In Dugin's framework, as Jiang summarizes it, Western capitalism itself occupies the Antichrist role. Jiang treats this as a rival eschatology competing with the Christian Zionist one, evidence, in his view, that multiple nations are currently operating from within their own end-times scripts simultaneously — Russia's Third Rome tradition, in which Russia is the katechon, versus a Christian Zionist tradition in which Russia and Iran are cast as Gog and Magog, the very forces that must be defeated to bring the end times about.

## Part Eight: Where Jiang Thinks This Is All Heading

Jiang closes his prepared remarks with a forecast, and it's genuinely dark. He compares the present moment not to the Great Depression or the aftermath of World War II, but to the **Bronze Age Collapse** — the still-debated historical event around 1200 BCE in which several interconnected Eastern Mediterranean civilizations broke down within a short span, amid waves of migration, famine, and warfare. His logic: the world currently supports roughly 8 billion people largely because of cheap, reliable energy and fertilizer, much of it tied to Middle Eastern supply chains. If war disrupts that supply at scale, he argues, the result isn't a recession — it's famine-driven migration and cascading conflict, the pattern he attributes to the actual Bronze Age Collapse. Global interconnection, in his framing, isn't resilience but fragility, since a shock anywhere can cascade everywhere.

He weaves earlier threads back in here: economic collapse, he suggests, could eventually produce something like a modern military draft — not necessarily combat service, but a large-scale government works program for a population that can no longer rely on family wealth as a safety net. He argues that engineered financial bubbles, including the current AI investment boom in his view, tend to be popped deliberately by the interests that benefit from the resulting government bailout, rather than bursting by accident. And he frames artificial intelligence not primarily as a surveillance or productivity tool but, in keeping with his "holy empire of reason" reading of Freemasonry, as a technological continuation of the same project: eliminating the friction of human emotion, bias, and local loyalty in favor of a more seamless, capital-friendly world.

His advice to the audience, repeated across the closing Q&A, isn't primarily political. He tells people to reclaim their attention from institutions and platforms built to capture it, to invest in direct human relationships over material wealth, and to cultivate what he calls a connection to "the monad" — his term, drawn loosely from traditions like Neoplatonism and Leibniz's philosophy, for a universal consciousness he believes underlies reality and can guide a person toward their purpose. Practically, he frames spiritual conviction — rather than stockpiled supplies or geographic relocation — as the decisive factor in who navigates coming instability well, arguing that most people who struggle in a crisis do so not from a lack of resources but from an unwillingness to accept that the world they knew is already ending.

## How to Read This Lecture

Jiang's talk operates on two different registers. The first — dollar hegemony, the Eurodollar system, financialization, the yen carry trade, and the chokepoint geography of the Iran war — draws on real, documented economic structures and events (the Plaza Accord, the 2008 crisis, the Glorious Revolution, the Bank of England's founding) and applies a consistent, if contestable, interpretive lens to them.

The second — that a covert alliance between transnational capital and messianic religious movements, evidenced by figures like Zuckerberg, Kushner, and Chabad's institutional history, is steering world events toward a scripted apocalyptic climax — is something Jiang himself frames as pattern-matching and interpretation rather than documented fact. The specific overlapping affiliations he cites are, by his own account, largely a matter of public record; the coordination and religious intentionality he infers from them is not something the lecture supports with direct evidence. Whether that inference illuminates something real about power, religion, and history, or is a pattern imposed on unrelated facts, is a judgment this piece leaves to the reader.
