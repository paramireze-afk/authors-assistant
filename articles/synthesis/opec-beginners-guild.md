# OPEC: The Club That Tries to Run the World's Oil Taps

Imagine a group of oil-producing countries that mostly don't like each other very much, don't share a language, a religion, or a political system, and are scattered across three continents — but they all agree to get on a phone call every so often and decide, together, how much oil to pump out of the ground. That's OPEC in a sentence. It sounds almost too simple to matter. And yet, for more than sixty years, this loose club of oil-exporting nations has been able to move the price of one of the world's most important commodities, shake stock markets, help start recessions, and occasionally get blamed for nearly everything wrong with the global economy.

This chapter is a tour of that club: why it exists, how it actually operates, and how much power it really has — because the honest answer to that last question is a lot messier than either OPEC's fans or its critics usually admit.

## What OPEC Actually Is

**OPEC** stands for the **Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries**. It was founded in 1960 in Baghdad by five countries — Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela — and has grown and shrunk over the decades as members joined, left, and occasionally rejoined. As of 2026, OPEC has 11 member countries: Algeria, the Republic of the Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Libya, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela. The United Arab Emirates was a member for decades but formally withdrew in May 2026, following Qatar (2019) and Angola (2024) out the door — a reminder that OPEC has been shrinking almost as often as it's grown lately.

OPEC's headquarters sits in Vienna, Austria — a neutral, boring, bureaucratically convenient location, chosen deliberately so no single member country could be seen as hosting (and therefore dominating) the organization.

At its core, OPEC exists to do one thing: **coordinate how much oil its members produce**, so that instead of each country pumping flat-out and crashing the price for everyone, they can act more like a single, price-conscious supplier.

## Why It Was Created

To understand OPEC, you have to understand what the oil business looked like before it existed. In the mid-20th century, oil production in the Middle East, Latin America, and elsewhere was largely controlled by a handful of Western oil companies — nicknamed the **"Seven Sisters"** — including forerunners of today's ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, and BP. These companies decided how much oil to extract, and crucially, they set the *posted price* of oil, which determined how much tax revenue and royalties the host governments received.

In 1959 and 1960, these companies cut posted prices without asking the producing countries — directly hitting the budgets of governments that depended on oil income. Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela had had enough of being price-takers in their own resource, and in September 1960 they formed OPEC in Baghdad. The founding goal was straightforward: give oil-producing nations, rather than foreign corporations, a real say over prices and production of their own natural resource.

This is a genuinely important historical fact, not interpretation: OPEC was born out of a struggle over *sovereignty* — who gets to decide the value of a country's own resources. That theme never really goes away; it resurfaces throughout this chapter.

## How OPEC Actually Works

OPEC isn't a company, and it doesn't own any oil itself. It's better understood as a **standing negotiation** between sovereign governments. Here's the basic mechanic:

- **The Conference.** Oil ministers from member states meet periodically — sometimes in Vienna, often now by video call — to review supply, demand, inventories, and price forecasts.
- **Production quotas.** The group agrees (or argues its way toward) a total production target, then divides it among members as individual quotas — "Saudi Arabia can pump this much, Nigeria that much."
- **Voluntary compliance, no enforcement.** OPEC has no army, no court, and no way to physically stop a member from cheating on its quota. Compliance depends on self-interest and peer pressure, and cheating has historically been common, especially among smaller members.
- **Consensus, mostly.** Decisions are formally reached by consensus rather than majority vote, so a single determined holdout can gum up the works.

In other words, OPEC is less a strict cartel headquarters and more a permanent, recurring summit where oil-dependent governments try to align their self-interest without any court to enforce the deal.

## OPEC vs. OPEC+: Enter Russia

Since 2016, you'll often see **"OPEC+"** in the news instead of plain OPEC. This isn't a rebrand — it's a bigger coalition.

**OPEC+** is OPEC's members *plus* a group of non-OPEC oil-exporting countries that agreed to coordinate production alongside them. The most important of these partners is **Russia**, one of the top three oil producers on Earth (alongside the U.S. and Saudi Arabia). Other participants have included Kazakhstan, Mexico, Oman, Azerbaijan, Bahrain, Malaysia, Brunei, South Sudan, and Sudan. Brazil sits somewhere between the two tiers: it joined OPEC+'s broader "Charter of Cooperation" in 2025 but stayed outside OPEC itself and outside the core production-quota arrangement — a reminder that OPEC+ membership isn't all-or-nothing.

Why did this expansion happen? By the mid-2010s, OPEC alone no longer controlled enough of world oil supply to move prices reliably on its own — U.S. shale production (more on that shortly) and other non-OPEC output had grown too large. Bringing Russia and other big non-member producers into a coordinated framework gave the coalition a much larger share of global supply to work with, and therefore more leverage.

Russia's role deserves its own note: Russia is **not** an OPEC member and never has been. It's a powerful, semi-independent partner with its own interests, which don't always align neatly with Saudi Arabia's. This tension has occasionally spilled into public view — most dramatically in early 2020, when a Saudi-Russian disagreement over production cuts briefly collapsed the OPEC+ deal entirely, triggering an oil price crash that coincided with the early COVID-19 demand shock. The relationship recovered, but it's a useful reminder that OPEC+ is a coalition of convenience, not a merger.

## Why Saudi Arabia Is the One That Matters Most

Every OPEC member has a vote and a seat at the table, but they are not remotely equal in influence, and Saudi Arabia sits well above the rest for a few concrete reasons:

1. **Sheer volume.** Saudi Arabia has consistently been one of the top two or three oil producers in the world, often producing more than 9 million barrels a day on its own — a huge share of OPEC's total output.
2. **Spare capacity.** This is the crucial one. Saudi Arabia is one of the only countries that deliberately keeps *unused* production capacity in reserve — oil wells and infrastructure it *could* turn on quickly if it wanted to flood the market, or hold back to tighten it. That flexibility is a kind of power no other producer has at the same scale: the ability to swing supply up or down and actually move the global price.
3. **Low production costs.** Saudi oil is remarkably cheap to extract compared to many other sources (like U.S. shale or Canadian oil sands), which means Saudi Arabia can tolerate lower prices for longer than most competitors — a strategic advantage in any price war.
4. **De facto leadership.** As a founding member with the most output and spare capacity, Saudi Arabia has functioned as OPEC's unofficial leader and most influential voice since the beginning, informally setting the tone for what the group does.

None of this means Saudi Arabia can simply dictate outcomes — its own budget depends heavily on oil revenue, and it has to balance its own fiscal needs against those of the rest of the coalition. But when people say "OPEC decided X," they usually mean "Saudi Arabia, with everyone else's grudging agreement, decided X."

## The 1973 Oil Crisis: OPEC's Coming-Out Party

If OPEC had a moment when the whole world suddenly noticed it existed, it was October 1973.

During the Yom Kippur War (Arab states versus Israel), the **Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC)** — a related but distinct group of *Arab* oil exporters — announced an oil embargo against countries seen as supporting Israel, chiefly the U.S. and the Netherlands. Around the same time, OPEC members raised posted oil prices sharply. Together, these moves roughly quadrupled oil prices within months, leaving countries dependent on imports facing gas lines, rationing, and a nasty combination of inflation and recession economists later dubbed "stagflation."

This is a well-documented historical fact, not interpretation: the embargo showed, for the first time in dramatic fashion, that oil-producing nations could use control over supply as leverage against powerful importing nations — reshaping how Western governments thought about energy security for a generation.

A few other turning points worth knowing, briefly:

- **1979–80, Iranian Revolution & Iran-Iraq War:** oil supply disruptions triggered a second major price shock.
- **1986, the price collapse:** Saudi Arabia, tired of cutting its own output to cover other members' cheating, opened the taps and let prices crash — a warning that Saudi restraint has limits.
- **1990–91, Gulf War:** Iraq's invasion of fellow OPEC member Kuwait showed OPEC unity breaks down when members go to actual war with each other.
- **2014–16, the shale price war:** OPEC chose not to cut production even as prices fell — a stated "defend market share" strategy that many analysts read as an attempt to squeeze out higher-cost shale producers, though Saudi officials publicly denied "declaring war" on shale. Either way, it cost OPEC members enormous revenue and only partly slowed shale's growth.
- **2020, the COVID crash:** demand collapsed so fast that U.S. oil futures briefly went *negative* for the first time in history, prompting historic OPEC+ production cuts.

## Petrodollars: Oil, Power, and the U.S. Dollar

Here's a piece of financial plumbing that quietly shapes the whole system: for decades, the overwhelming majority of the world's oil has been priced and sold in **U.S. dollars**, no matter which two countries are actually doing the trading — so a refinery in Japan buying Saudi crude settles the invoice in dollars, not yen or riyals. This arrangement, and the flows of dollar revenue it produces for oil exporters, is often called the **petrodollar system**.

It's worth being careful about how this system came to be, because the popular version — "Saudi Arabia secretly agreed to sell its oil only in dollars in exchange for U.S. military protection" — oversimplifies real history. Oil contracts had already been priced mostly in dollars for decades before the 1970s, simply because the dollar was the world's dominant trading currency. What *is* well documented: in 1971, Nixon severed the dollar from gold (the "Nixon Shock"), and in 1974 — after the 1973 crisis — the U.S. and Saudi Arabia formalized deeper financial and military cooperation, including an arrangement, kept confidential for years and only confirmed through declassified records decades later, that let Saudi Arabia funnel much of its oil earnings into U.S. government debt (**Treasury securities**, essentially IOUs the U.S. sells to raise money) outside the normal public auction process, in return for continued American military hardware, training, and security backing. That's the documented core. Whether it amounted to one explicit, exclusive "dollars-for-protection" bargain, versus a looser relationship that reinforced an already-dollarized oil trade, is where historians genuinely disagree — and where thinkers like Hudson and Griffin, discussed below, read the same limited evidence very differently.

Why does any of this matter?

- **It boosts demand for dollars.** Countries need dollars to buy oil, which helps keep the dollar the world's dominant reserve currency — the currency other countries hold in reserve and trust for major transactions.
- **It recycles "petrodollars" back into Western economies.** This is **petrodollar recycling**: oil exporters take dollars earned from selling crude and reinvest them into U.S. and European financial markets, real estate, and government debt, rather than letting the money sit idle.
- **It gives the U.S. financial system unusual leverage.** Because so much of global trade runs through dollars, the U.S. can cut a country off from the dollar-based banking system as a financial weapon — something used against Iran, Russia, and others.

There's ongoing, genuinely unsettled debate about whether this system is eroding, as China pushes for oil trade in other currencies and sanctioned states like Russia and Iran route around dollar-based trade. As of the mid-2020s, the dollar still dominates oil trade by a wide margin, but that trend is worth watching, not a settled fact.

## OPEC, Washington, and Big Oil: An Uneasy Triangle

OPEC's relationship with the United States is genuinely complicated: the U.S. is historically the world's largest oil *consumer*, now also the largest oil *producer*, and the country whose currency the whole trade runs through. U.S. administrations have often pressured Saudi Arabia and other producers to pump more when prices spike at the pump — while higher prices simultaneously benefit U.S. domestic producers, so American interests aren't fully unified either.

The major international oil companies — ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, BP, and others — tell the other half of the sovereignty story. In the 1960s and 70s, most OPEC countries **nationalized** their oil industries, taking direct government ownership of extraction and export and creating state-owned companies like Saudi Aramco or Venezuela's PDVSA. Today, Western majors mostly operate as service providers, technical partners, or crude buyers — not owners of Middle Eastern oil fields the way the Seven Sisters once were.

## Cartel, Monopoly, or Something Else?

People throw around the word **cartel** to describe OPEC constantly, and it's not wrong exactly — but it's worth being precise about what that means and what it doesn't.

- A **monopoly** is a single seller controlling an entire market. OPEC is clearly not this: its members supply a large chunk of world oil, but not all of it, and plenty of oil is produced entirely outside their control (the U.S., Canada, Brazil, and others).
- A **cartel**, in economic terms, is a group of independent producers who agree to coordinate output or pricing to raise their collective profits above what open competition would produce. This is a much closer fit for OPEC's actual design — its members explicitly set production targets with the goal of influencing price, which is the textbook definition in action — and mainstream economists generally describe it this way without much controversy.
- But OPEC is an unusually weak and leaky cartel by textbook standards, because:
  - It has no legal mechanism to punish members who cheat on their quotas.
  - Its members are sovereign governments with their own domestic political pressures, wars, and budget crises that regularly override collective agreements.
  - It doesn't control the whole market — non-members can and do simply produce more when OPEC tries to restrict supply, undercutting the strategy.

So most economists would call OPEC a cartel in structure and intent, but an imperfect and often dysfunctional one in practice — closer to a coalition of governments trying to act like a cartel, with mixed and inconsistent success.

## So How Much Power Does OPEC Actually Have?

This is the question everyone actually wants answered, and the honest answer is: **less than it used to, but still real — especially in the short term.**

A few concrete anchors:

- OPEC's members control a large majority of the world's **proven oil reserves** — oil that's been confirmed to exist and is judged economically recoverable with current technology. Estimates commonly put OPEC's share somewhere in the 70–80% range (the exact figure shifts as membership and reserve estimates change), which matters enormously for the long-term future of oil supply.
- But OPEC's share of *current daily production* is much smaller than its share of reserves — roughly a third of world output, depending on the year — because non-OPEC producers, especially the United States, have ramped up output dramatically.
- OPEC+ decisions can still move prices significantly in the short run, especially through Saudi Arabia's unique spare capacity, and especially when the market is already tight (low inventories, high demand).
- OPEC has much less power to keep prices *high* for long, because high prices invite more production from producers outside its control — a self-limiting effect that has gotten stronger over time.

## The Shale Revolution: OPEC's Biggest Rival

Nothing has changed OPEC's position more, in the last two decades, than the American **shale revolution**. New drilling techniques — mainly **hydraulic fracturing ("fracking")** combined with horizontal drilling — unlocked huge volumes of oil trapped in shale rock formations across Texas, North Dakota, and elsewhere. The U.S. Energy Information Administration confirms the United States surpassed both Saudi Arabia and Russia to become the world's largest crude oil producer in 2018, and it has held that position since — a firm, well-documented fact, not an approximation.

This mattered enormously for OPEC's power for a structural reason: shale wells are relatively quick and cheap to bring online (compared to giant offshore or Middle Eastern mega-projects), which means U.S. producers can respond to price signals much faster. When OPEC cuts production and prices rise, U.S. shale drillers can ramp up in months rather than years, eating into the price gains OPEC was trying to create. Economists sometimes describe shale as having turned U.S. producers into a kind of "swing producer" that competes with, and partially checks, OPEC's own swing-producer role.

This is why the 2014–2016 price war happened at all: rather than cutting production to prop up prices, OPEC, led by Saudi Arabia, let them crash and framed it as "defending market share." Many analysts read it as a bet that low prices would squeeze out higher-cost shale drillers, though Saudi officials denied that was the explicit goal. Either way, it inflicted real pain on shale producers but didn't eliminate the sector, which recovered and grew further once prices stabilized — a fairly clear demonstration of shale's staying power as a structural constraint on OPEC.

## Oil, War, Sanctions, and Sovereignty

It's impossible to talk honestly about OPEC without talking about geopolitics, because oil and international power have been tangled together since long before OPEC existed.

A few threads worth naming:

- **Sanctions as a weapon.** Because oil trade runs through the dollar system, cutting a country off from it — as done to Iran, Venezuela, and, at unprecedented scale, Russia after 2022 — can cripple an oil exporter without firing a shot.
- **Oil as a target in war.** Oil infrastructure has repeatedly been struck directly in Middle Eastern conflicts (the Iran-Iraq War, the Gulf Wars, attacks on Saudi and Emirati facilities), and oil revenue has funded and prolonged conflicts across producing regions.
- **Resource sovereignty as a throughline.** OPEC's origin story is producing nations asserting control over their own resources against foreign interests — the same instinct visible in 1970s nationalizations and in members periodically walking away from OPEC (Qatar in 2019, Angola in 2024) when they judge the arrangement no longer serves their national interest.
- **The 2022 Russia episode.** When Western nations tried to isolate Russian oil after Ukraine, OPEC+ chose to *cut* overall production rather than fill the gap — read by many as prioritizing group price stability, and implicitly Russian interests, over Western strategic goals.

## Competing Perspectives, In Brief

Rather than one "correct" reading of this system, it helps to hold several lenses side by side — they disagree, but each is looking at something real:

- **Daniel Yergin**, whose Pulitzer-winning history *The Prize* is the standard mainstream account, treats the Seven Sisters' decline, OPEC's rise, and the petrodollar bargain as chapters in a continuous story about energy security. His narrative is the baseline most of the other perspectives here are reacting to, extending, or pushing back against.
- **Michael Hudson**, an economist focused on international finance, looks past the production quotas to the plumbing behind them: he argues that Gulf oil revenue recycled into U.S. Treasury securities let Washington finance persistent deficits and a global military footprint using surpluses the dollar-oil system itself generated — a structurally lopsided arrangement he calls a form of financial dominance, not a mutually neutral bargain.
- **Murray Rothbard** and other Austrian economists come at OPEC from the opposite direction: their core claim is that cartels rarely hold together on market discipline alone, since every member has an incentive to cheat, unless a state actively shields the arrangement from competition. In this reading, OPEC's staying power says more about diplomatic and military backing than about the strength of coordinated pricing itself.
- **G. Edward Griffin**, author of *The Creature from Jekyll Island*, ties oil to the broader critique of central banking. The documented part of his account — the 1971 Nixon Shock severing the dollar from gold, and debt issuance financing a large share of U.S. military spending since — is established history. His further claim, that this system reflects a deliberate design by coordinated financial elites rather than an emergent policy outcome, is speculative interpretation and should be read as such.
- **Scott Horton**, a noninterventionist foreign-policy commentator, focuses on the security half of the bargain: U.S. arms sales, basing rights, and defense commitments to Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states are, in his view, hard to explain without energy security, and have repeatedly pulled Washington into regional wars, sanctions regimes, and blowback it might otherwise have avoided.
- **Vivek Ramaswamy** connects a more recent dot: he argues that ESG-driven pressure discouraging Western banks and asset managers from financing fossil-fuel projects can inadvertently hand pricing power to OPEC+ and other producers unconstrained by similar pressure, since global oil demand doesn't shrink just because Western supply does.

These views genuinely conflict in places — Rothbard's market skepticism sits uneasily with Hudson's emphasis on structural financial advantage — and overlap in others, since Horton and Hudson both see the security-for-petrodollars bargain as generating distorted incentives, just from different angles. None of them is "the" answer; each is a different place to stand while looking at the same system.

## The Short Version

OPEC was born from a fight over who controls the value of a country's own natural resources, grew into an organization capable of shocking the world economy in 1973, and expanded into the broader OPEC+ coalition to keep pace with a changing market. It still matters today — but far less unilaterally than it once did, thanks largely to American shale and the basic reality that no cartel fully escapes a global market with alternatives. It sits at the intersection of economics, sovereignty, and geopolitical power — exactly why it remains one of the most argued-about institutions in the modern world.
