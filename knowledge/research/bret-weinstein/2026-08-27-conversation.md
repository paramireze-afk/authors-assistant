# Lineage, Not Race: Inside Bret Weinstein and Nathan Cofnas's Collision Over Genes, IQ, and Morality

## Setting the stage

In late July, philosopher Nathan Cofnas — a postdoctoral researcher whose specialty is the philosophy of biology — published an article about Jason Arday, a young Black academic who had risen to a professorship at Cambridge before resigning and, shortly afterward, taking his own life. The article was explosive enough that Cofnas's own university, Kent, suspended him. Weeks later, evolutionary biologist Bret Weinstein invited Cofnas onto his *DarkHorse Podcast* — not, he insisted at the outset, as a hostile interview of the kind Cofnas had been enduring elsewhere, but as "a conversation between colleagues that disagree."

That framing matters, because the two men agree on a surprising amount. Both describe themselves as opponents of what they call "wokeism," both regard the ideology as an existential threat to Western civilization, and both accept — as a matter of basic evolutionary biology — that human populations differ in measurable ways as a result of having adapted to different environments. Where they part ways is on a handful of specific, high-stakes questions: whether "race" is a scientifically honest concept or a corrupted one; whether morality can be grounded in anything beyond human preference; whether a famous evolutionary theory of incest aversion actually holds up; and — the conversation's real center of gravity — whether the well-documented IQ gap between racial groups in the United States is best explained by genetic differences that never spread to fixation, or by developmental and cultural damage inflicted by slavery and its aftermath.

What follows is a guided reconstruction of that argument, not a transcript. Contested claims are attributed to whichever man made them; nothing here should be read as an adjudicated finding.

## "Race" versus "lineage": a fight about words as much as biology

Their first real disagreement is almost linguistic, but it carries enormous weight for everything that follows.

Cofnas calls himself a "race realist," and he offers a philosopher's justification for the term: in philosophy, "realism" about X simply means believing X exists — a moral realist believes moral facts exist, an X-realist believes X is real. By his account, race realism has two versions: a modest one (racial categories track *something* biologically real) and the stronger one he holds, which adds that there are also meaningful differences in socially relevant traits — including intelligence — between racial groups.

Weinstein pushes back hard on the label itself, arguing that calling a contested empirical claim "realism" is a rhetorical trick — it puts a thumb on the scale before any evidence has been examined, the same way it would be odd to call belief in the resurrection "resurrection realism." Cofnas counters that this is simply standard philosophical usage and implies no unfair advantage.

But Weinstein's deeper objection isn't about vocabulary games — it's that the word "race" itself has been so mangled by history (he points to the "one-drop rule" as an example of a folk category built on racism rather than biology) that using it in public discourse guarantees confusion. His preferred term is **lineage**: a population's biological continuity traced backward through time. Lineages and populations, he argues, are uncontroversial concepts that population geneticists use constantly — the trouble is that critics who insist "race doesn't exist" (he singles out biologist Richard Lewontin's famous argument that within-group genetic variance exceeds between-group variance) turn around and use "population" or "ancestral population" to describe the exact same biological reality, just with a more respectable-sounding label.

Cofnas, in turn, warns against what linguist Steven Pinker called the "euphemism treadmill": if "race" is replaced by "population," population will eventually accumulate the same stigma "race" now carries, and society will be back where it started, one word poorer. He notes that "retarded" was once the polite clinical term before it became a slur — and that he recently got in trouble for describing Arday as "mentally disabled," a phrase Arday had used of himself.

Weinstein's rejoinder is that this particular treadmill argument, while generally correct, doesn't rescue "race" specifically — the term predates any scientific understanding of the underlying genetics and carries baggage that "lineage" or "population" simply don't. Neither man fully concedes the point, but the exchange sets up a recurring pattern: Cofnas treats precise definitional distinctions as evasive hair-splitting, while Weinstein treats loose or historically loaded terminology as a trap that guarantees bad-faith interpretation by the public.

## Is morality real, or just useful?

This next stretch of the conversation is philosophical rather than biological, but it's essential background for everything that follows about "should."

Cofnas identifies as an anti-realist about morality: he doesn't believe there are "mind-independent" facts about right and wrong that would hold regardless of what any mind believed. Moral judgments, on his view, are non-arbitrary *relative to* some standard — human interests, evolutionary pressures — but there's no way to certify one standard (say, "maximize the number of people fed") as objectively better than a totally different one (say, "let an asteroid wipe out all life").

Weinstein tries to construct a foothold for something sturdier. He proposes, as a test case, that a moral rule counts as non-arbitrary if it demonstrably serves a measurable outcome — his example is a Guatemalan farming tradition of planting and harvesting by the phase of the moon, which synchronizes crop maturity across a region and thereby limits how much pests can multiply as fields ripen at different times. Cofnas agrees this rule isn't arbitrary with respect to crop yield — but insists that doesn't get you to "true" in any deeper sense, because you still have to ask why "maximize crop yield" (or, in Weinstein's more provocative proposed standard, "expand the wealth and reach of one's lineage") deserves to be privileged. Taken literally, Cofnas points out, a morality built purely around lineage expansion could just as easily justify killing the unproductive, the disabled, or the infertile — which is not what either man actually believes is moral.

Weinstein's counter is a kind of pragmatic bootstrap: yes, you cannot defend "being alive is better than being dead" from some view from nowhere — but every living thing that exists is, by definition, descended from ancestors who acted as though staying alive mattered, so it's reasonable to reason forward from that shared premise. Once you grant that life is preferable to death within the community of living things, propositions like "being well-formed is better than being malformed" or "starvation is bad" follow as close to universally as anything in ethics gets. Cofnas isn't satisfied: he notes that even "pain is bad" isn't universal in practice, since people routinely regard an enemy's or out-group member's suffering as good. Weinstein replies that this is a conflict of interest, not evidence that pain itself isn't aversive — but the exchange ends in a stalemate that both men seem to accept, with Weinstein summing up the disagreement as being over whether "mind-independent" moral facts are even the right question to be asking, versus Cofnas holding that without such facts, "true" adds nothing that "useful" and "evolutionarily successful" don't already capture.

## The Westermarck effect: a textbook theory Cofnas says is a myth

Before turning to IQ, the two spend considerable time on a specific evolutionary-psychology case study that Weinstein raises as a template for how genes might encode moral intuitions: the **Westermarck effect**, the well-known claim that children raised together from an early age develop a mutual sexual aversion, regardless of whether they're actually biologically related. The textbook story treats this as a mechanism for avoiding inbreeding depression — the elevated risk of harmful recessive genetic conditions when close relatives reproduce — and treats the resulting incest taboo as evidence that a moral rule can be traced directly back to a genetically encoded heuristic ("feel averse to whoever you grew up with").

Cofnas calls this account, drawn from the two classic bodies of evidence — Arthur Wolf's studies of Taiwanese "minor marriages" (in which a future bride was adopted into her groom's family as a child) and observations of Israeli kibbutz children raised in communal peer groups — essentially a myth. On the kibbutz evidence, he cites a study in the *American Journal of Sociology* in which former kibbutz members, when actually interviewed, reported plenty of attraction to their peer-reared companions and, in many cases, described active taboos against pursuing it — undermining the claim that aversion arose spontaneously and needed no taboo. On the Taiwanese evidence, he argues the "minor marriages" were lower-status, last-resort arrangements to begin with, and the fertility difference Wolf reported (roughly four children versus five and a half) is modest and confounded by other factors.

Cofnas adds a philosophical objection, tracing an argument to classicist James Frazer (often mis-attributed to philosopher Bernard Williams): even if childhood co-residence really did trigger sexual aversion, that aversion targets *childhood co-residents*, not *biological siblings* — yet the incest taboo explicitly concerns biological relatedness (it still applies to a sibling raised apart, who would trigger no Westermarck aversion at all). Getting from "I feel averse to the person I grew up with" to "sex between biological siblings, in general, is wrong" requires an extra interpretive leap that the theory doesn't explain.

Weinstein doesn't defend the Westermarck literature point for point, but presses a separate observation: whatever the mechanism, there plainly *is* a cultural incest taboo that correctly distinguishes siblings from unrelated co-residents — and that taboo is clearly non-arbitrary with respect to the real genetic hazard of inbreeding. Cofnas's response draws the conversation back to his broader anti-realist point: this shows morality isn't arbitrary, which he already conceded, but it doesn't establish that the rule is *objectively true* rather than simply useful and inherited (whether genetically or culturally) because it worked.

## The heart of the disagreement: IQ, race, and how differences arise

The conversation's central and most consequential disagreement concerns the Black-white IQ gap in the United States, which Cofnas puts at roughly 15 points and describes as "stable for decades," having "not closed" despite social change — a figure and trend widely reported in the psychometric literature, though its interpretation is exactly what's in dispute. Both men agree the gap itself is real and measurable, and both agree that IQ, while imperfect and incomplete as a measure of intelligence, is unusually predictive of a wide range of life outcomes. Their disagreement is entirely about **cause**.

### Cofnas's case: ordinary Darwinism applied to humans

Cofnas frames his position as nothing more exotic than standard evolutionary theory: populations separated for long enough periods diverge and adapt to different local conditions, exactly as they do for skin tone, disease resistance, or body shape. If selection can shape body form, he argues, there's no principled reason it can't also shape the distribution of genetic variants related to cognition, and he points to ongoing scientific debate over the origins of high average Ashkenazi Jewish IQ (which some researchers attribute to selection pressures favoring certain occupations) as one example of this kind of argument already being made in mainstream behavioral genetics.

### Weinstein's central objection: why haven't the "smart genes" gone to fixation?

Weinstein's central counter-argument is technical but crucial, and it recurs throughout the episode as his single strongest line of attack. In population genetics, a trait that provides a strong, unambiguous survival or reproductive advantage — with no compensating downside — should, over enough generations, spread until essentially everyone in an interbreeding population carries it. Geneticists call this **going to fixation**.

It's worth flagging, as neither man does explicitly, that fixation is a cleaner concept for a single gene with a large, isolated effect than for a trait like intelligence, which behavioral genetics generally treats as **polygenic** — shaped by many thousands of variants, each contributing a tiny effect, interacting with one another, and subject to forces (stabilizing selection toward an intermediate value, a steady trickle of new mutations, linkage with other genes) that can slow or complicate a clean sweep to fixation. That complication doesn't resolve the debate — a slow, incomplete, still-in-progress sweep is compatible with Cofnas's account, and Weinstein's "why hasn't this happened already" objection remains a fair question under a polygenic model too — but it means the tidy either/or logic both men sometimes lean on is a simplification of messier population genetics.

Weinstein's own argument, as he presents it, runs like this: unlike physical traits such as running speed or heat tolerance — which come with clear biological trade-offs (fast-twitch muscle versus endurance muscle; a compact heat-retaining body versus a heat-shedding one) — raw general intelligence doesn't seem to have an equivalent cost that would cap its spread. If that's true, then any genetic variant that reliably boosted cognitive capacity, in *any* human population, should have spread through that population and, given that human groups have never been fully reproductively isolated from one another for the relevant time periods, should plausibly have spread through humanity as a whole. The fact that intelligence still visibly varies a great deal between individuals — with some people, in Weinstein's framing, simply "born to be more intelligent" — is, in his view, much easier to explain by genetic *defects* (mutations that break a gene and thereby lower cognitive performance in a measurably heritable way) than by *advantageous* variants that have somehow persisted at intermediate frequencies for millennia without spreading to everyone.

To illustrate why "heritable" doesn't mean what people assume it means, Weinstein offers a thought experiment that becomes the conversation's most vivid image:

> **The black cats in the dark.** Imagine an island where, by local custom, every black-furred house cat is kept in total darkness for its first year of life, then released. Those cats will show severe, measurable visual impairment — and if you calculate the heritability of that visual deficit, it will come back very high, because coat color (a genetic trait) predicts almost perfectly who ends up visually impaired. But the genes involved aren't "vision genes gone wrong" — they're ordinary coat-color genes that happen to trigger a specific developmental environment (permanent darkness) that then damages vision. The heritability statistic is completely accurate; what's false is the intuitive inference that the trait is "written into the genes" for vision itself.

Weinstein uses this to argue that heritability estimates — including those from twin and adoption studies that Cofnas cites — measure how well genes *predict* an outcome within a given population and environment, not whether genes directly encode that outcome in some fixed, environment-proof way. He extends the point with two blunter historical examples: being a slave, and being illiterate, would both have registered as "highly heritable" by the same statistical logic in earlier centuries, for reasons that had nothing to do with genes for servility or genes for illiteracy.

Applied to the U.S. Black-white IQ gap specifically, Weinstein's proposed alternative mechanism is the deliberate destruction of cultural inheritance during slavery: enslaved people speaking different languages were mixed together, and children were forcibly separated from parents, disrupting the normal, near-universal human process by which a functioning cultural toolkit — including the specific skills and knowledge that Western IQ tests are calibrated to reward — gets passed from one generation to the next. He contrasts this with the experience of other American immigrant groups (he mentions the Irish and European Jews), who arrived facing real hardship and discrimination but nonetheless maintained an intact, if hobbled, culture that their children could inherit and build on.

### Cofnas's counters

Cofnas responds to the fixation argument on several fronts. First, empirically: if slavery-driven cultural disruption were the operative mechanism, he argues, it predicts the wrong direction for a known comparison — African Americans, several generations removed from slavery and exposed to the disrupted culture Weinstein describes, actually score *higher* on IQ tests than many populations in Africa itself, which complicates a simple "disruption caused the gap" story. Weinstein's rejoinder is that Western IQ tests were built and calibrated to predict success within a Western cultural and economic environment, so applying the same instrument to a population operating in a different habitat and expecting equivalent validity is itself methodologically shaky — "you're applying it somewhere else and expecting it to work just as well."

Second, Cofnas argues that similar population-level patterns recur across Sub-Saharan African, East Asian, and European populations regardless of country, culture, religion, or specific colonial history — which he suggests is hard to explain purely through the trauma of American slavery. He cites Ethiopia, a nation he notes was never durably colonized, as an example of a population whose outcomes on certain measures resemble those of colonized African nations, which he offers as evidence against a purely colonial-disruption story. Weinstein counters that these aren't independent data points: the populations in question have interacted, traded, warred, and influenced one another for centuries in ways that could transmit similar cultural and economic patterns without any shared genetic explanation.

Third, and most directly, Cofnas presses Weinstein on individual cases: was John von Neumann — the mathematician and physicist renowned as one of the most intelligent people of the twentieth century — more intelligent than average *in part because of his genes*? Weinstein, notably, declines to affirmatively say yes, insisting instead that genes are obviously *involved* in producing any human mind (nobody is a blank slate) without conceding that von Neumann carried some *specific advantageous cognitive variant* not present, or less common, in others. Cofnas presses further with Srinivasa Ramanujan, the self-taught Indian mathematician who, with no formal training or family advantages, independently rediscovered large swaths of higher mathematics — a case Cofnas selects precisely because it removes the usual objection that privilege or elite schooling explains the outcome. If Weinstein won't attribute Ramanujan's or von Neumann's extraordinary ability to fortunate genetic variation, Cofnas argues, his position is out of step with the mainstream of behavioral genetics, where individual (not population) differences in intelligence are widely accepted as partly genetic, on the strength of adoption studies and genome-wide association studies (GWAS).

Weinstein's response leans on his fixation argument again: he's not denying that genetic *defects* (bad code, in his phrase) can and do create heritable differences in cognitive ability — that part is easy to explain. What he doubts is the harder claim that unusually *advantageous* combinations of genes, capable of producing a von Neumann or a Ramanujan, could exist in a population without every non-isolated human population eventually acquiring them too, since nothing about raw cognitive advantage seems to carry the kind of trade-off that would keep it from spreading. His proposed alternative is that human beings possess what he calls a **second mode of inheritance** — cultural transmission — that is unique in scale among all species and does at least as much work in shaping who becomes a von Neumann or a Ramanujan as any specific genetic endowment. He points out that his own fluency is entirely a function of exposure: "English, smart; French, stupid" — a fact about developmental environment, not genetics. Human childhood, on this view, is unusually long precisely because so much cognitive capability is "offloaded" onto culturally transmitted knowledge rather than hard-coded genetically, and figures like Ramanujan may reflect rare, currently unidentifiable developmental circumstances rather than a unique genetic endowment.

### The Inuit/Ethiopian example, and why Weinstein thinks sports and cognition differ

To sharpen the contrast between traits he thinks *can* differ between populations and ones he doubts can, Weinstein offers the comparison of Inuit and Ethiopian body types, which both men accept without dispute. In extreme cold, survival favors a compact, low-surface-area body that retains heat efficiently — closer to a sphere. In hot climates, survival favors a tall, lanky, high-surface-area body that sheds heat efficiently, functioning almost like a radiator. Because these two environments select in opposite directions, marathon running — which rewards the heat-shedding Ethiopian-type body — will predictably favor different populations than, say, arctic survival tasks, and Weinstein readily agrees that visible athletic differences between populations, including the prominence of West African-descended sprinters, reflect real, non-arbitrary physical trade-offs of exactly this kind.

His argument is that cognition doesn't work the same way. Every environment he can think of — farming, hunting, fishing through ice, navigating slavery, tracking game, building tools, even learning a language — poses what he calls, provocatively, "IQ tests," and he argues that once a population reaches the local carrying capacity of its environment (the point at which resources become genuinely scarce), the efficiency with which its members solve these problems becomes a matter of survival everywhere, without exception. He extends this into what might be called an argument with no ceiling: once humans became each other's main competitors and collaborators — an "arms race" against other increasingly clever humans, mediated by concepts like engineer Buckminster Fuller's "ephemeralization" (doing ever more with ever less) — there is, in Weinstein's view, no point at which additional intelligence stops paying off, unlike a physical trait such as speed, which does eventually hit diminishing or even negative returns.

Cofnas's response is that this proves too much for Weinstein's own argument: if every environment favors intelligence without any offsetting cost, that should, on Weinstein's own logic, predict rapid convergence toward maximum intelligence everywhere — precisely the outcome ("we should all be born as von Neumann") that obviously hasn't happened, which Cofnas takes as evidence that trade-offs and constraints on genetic spread do, in fact, exist and are simply less visible than the ones governing running speed or body shape.

## What they actually agree and disagree about

By the end of the conversation, Weinstein tries to state the terms of disagreement precisely, and it's worth preserving that clarification rather than flattening it. Neither man disputes that:

- Individuals differ enormously in measured intelligence and in facility with language.
- IQ is a genuinely predictive, if incomplete, measurement in psychology.
- Human populations differ in visible, evolved physical characteristics, including ones relevant to athletic performance.
- DEI-style policies, as currently practiced at institutions like Harvard, often diverge sharply from their stated ideals — both cite Harvard's own 2013 internal finding that its undergraduate population would be roughly 0.7% Black under grades-and-test-scores-only admissions, versus an actual Black enrollment closer to 14%, as evidence of how large a role non-merit factors currently play. Cofnas says he broadly favors individual merit but is open to some demographic consideration in a limited set of "high-profile positions" for the sake of social cohesion — an exception Weinstein finds notable, given Cofnas's reputation, without fully endorsing it himself.

What divides them is not whether population-level differences exist, but **why**. Cofnas holds that ordinary Darwinian logic — separated populations facing different selective pressures — applies to cognitive traits exactly as it does to physical ones, and that behavioral genetics research (heritability studies, adoption studies, GWAS) supports a partly genetic explanation for the Black-white IQ gap specifically. Weinstein holds that the fixation problem makes this explanation biologically implausible for *advantageous* cognitive variants specifically (as opposed to harmful mutations, which he readily accepts can create heritable deficits), and that the far more parsimonious explanation lies in humanity's unusually powerful second channel of inheritance — culture — and its violent disruption during slavery.

Running underneath the IQ argument is the earlier disagreement about moral realism, which the two never fully resolve either: Cofnas maintains that even a "true" moral judgment adds nothing beyond "useful, and favored by selection," while Weinstein insists that once you accept the premise that living, coherent beings prefer existence to non-existence, a genuinely non-arbitrary — if not absolutely, cosmically "true" — foundation for morality becomes available.

The episode ends without either man conceding the central point. Weinstein closes by restating his skepticism that advantageous, non-fixated cognitive genes are a plausible explanation for population differences in intelligence; Cofnas restates that denying a genetic role in individual genius, as in the cases of von Neumann and Ramanujan, is itself the more radical and evidentially unsupported position. As Weinstein puts it, in a rare moment of concession to procedural fairness rather than substance: the audience can evaluate the argument for themselves.
