# Why the U.S. Just Spent Billions Rescuing Japan's Currency — And What It Might Be Telling Us

On the last day of July, the United States did something it hadn't done in 28 years: it stepped directly into the currency market to prop up someone else's money. Alongside Japan's government, U.S. officials spent tens of billions of dollars buying Japanese yen, and both sides said they're prepared to spend a lot more.

It's easy to file this under "foreign financial news" and move on. But the story underneath it touches U.S. government borrowing costs, global stock markets, and a decades-old trading strategy involving trillions of dollars — one most people have never heard of but have almost certainly benefited from without knowing it.

This article is based on a breakdown by financial commentator Jay Martin, host of *The Jay Martin Show*. The mechanics below — Japan's debt levels, its oil dependence, how bond prices work, the Federal Reserve facility Japan used — are documented, verifiable facts. Martin's *interpretation* of why the U.S. acted, and what it might mean going forward, is a reasoned argument built on those facts, not a confirmed statement of policy from the Fed or Treasury. We'll flag that distinction where it matters most and otherwise let you read his case on its merits.

## The Puzzle: Why Rescue Someone Else's Currency?

The yen had been sliding for months, hitting its weakest level against the dollar in 40 years. The Japanese government asked the U.S. for help, and Washington agreed — the first coordinated currency intervention of its kind since 1998.

On its face, this is strange. The United States is already dealing with its own inflation concerns and a national debt that recently passed $39.8 trillion, growing by roughly $2 trillion a year. Spending billions defending another country's currency isn't an obvious move for a government with its own financial headaches.

Publicly, U.S. officials framed it as friendship. President Trump told reporters, "We're always there for Japan. Japan's been very good to us." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pledged the U.S. would "not hesitate" to join further joint intervention if needed.

Martin's argument is that governments don't typically spend tens of billions of dollars purely out of goodwill — something more structural was at stake. To see what, it helps to know a fact most people don't: Japan isn't just an ally. It's one of the largest financiers of the U.S. government itself.

## ELI5: How This Whole Machine Works

Before the details, here's the whole story in one analogy.

Imagine Japan is a banker who lent a huge sum of money to a customer — the United States — by buying that customer's IOUs (U.S. Treasury bonds). Japan now holds $1.1 trillion of these IOUs, more than any other lender on Earth.

But the banker has a cash-flow problem of its own. Every month, Japan has to pay a massive bill in a foreign currency (dollars) for oil, because Japan barely produces any energy of its own. To cover that bill, Japan keeps selling its own currency (yen) for dollars — and doing that so often has driven the value of the yen down to a 40-year low.

Normally, a banker in cash trouble has an easy fix: sell some of the customer's IOUs for cash. But here's the catch — if Japan dumped hundreds of billions of dollars of U.S. Treasury bonds onto the market all at once, it would flood the market with sellers, push bond prices down, and drive up the interest rate the U.S. government has to pay on *all* its future borrowing, at a moment when the U.S. is already paying near-record rates on nearly $40 trillion of debt.

So the customer (the U.S.) doesn't want the banker (Japan) making a withdrawal that big. Instead, the U.S. helped set up a way for Japan to get cash *without* selling the IOUs — essentially letting Japan pawn the bonds at the Federal Reserve for freshly printed dollars, then use those dollars to buy back yen and prop up its currency.

That solves the banker's immediate cash problem, but it doesn't touch why the banker needed the cash in the first place — Japan still burns through borrowed money to pay its oil bills, and its currency is still fundamentally weak.

Now layer on the final piece: for thirty years, investors all over the world have borrowed cheap yen from Japan and used it to buy stocks, bonds, and real estate everywhere else — because Japan's interest rates have been close to zero, making yen the cheapest money on the planet to borrow. This is the "yen carry trade," and it means trillions of dollars sitting in markets worldwide are, in effect, borrowed from Japan. If yen ever stops being cheap — because rates rise or the currency suddenly strengthens — all those investors have to rush to sell what they bought and buy back yen to repay their loans, all at the same time. That's the thread connecting a currency most Americans never think about to the value of their 401(k).

With that picture in mind, here's how each piece actually works.

## Fact: Japan Is America's Biggest Foreign Lender

Here's a genuine puzzle in the data. Japan's government debt is enormous relative to its economy — about 230% of Japan's annual economic output (GDP), compared to roughly 125% for the U.S. government. By that measure, Japan looks like the more indebted country by a wide margin.

And yet, for over three decades, Japan has also been the world's largest net lender — the country other nations, including the U.S., owe the most money to.

How can both be true? The answer is that a country's *government* and its *people* are separate financial actors. Japanese households and companies are famously heavy savers, built up over decades of exporting cars, electronics, and machinery to the world. That savings pool is large enough to do two things simultaneously: fund most of what Japan's own government borrows, *and* leave plenty left over to invest abroad.

A favorite destination for that money has been U.S. Treasury bonds. Today, Japan holds about $1.1 trillion of U.S. debt, more than any other country, including China. In effect, when the U.S. government spends more than it collects in taxes (which it does every year) and covers the gap by borrowing, Japan is one of its biggest creditors. That's the basis for calling Japan "America's banker."

## The Oil-and-Dollars Squeeze on the Yen

If Japan is such a major lender, why is its own currency in trouble? The mechanism traces back to energy dependence.

Japan produces almost no oil of its own — it pumps around 100,000 barrels a day but burns roughly 3 million. About 97% of the oil Japan uses arrives by ship, and crucially, oil is priced and paid for in U.S. dollars, not yen.

That creates a feedback loop:

1. When oil gets more expensive (as it has amid conflict in the Middle East this year), Japan needs more dollars to pay for it.
2. To get dollars, Japan's importers sell yen and buy dollars.
3. Selling yen pushes the yen's price down, because more sellers than buyers in any market pushes the price down.
4. A cheaper yen means the *next* tanker of oil costs even more in yen terms, which forces even more yen-selling to cover the bill.

The cycle feeds on itself. By late July, it took about 164 yen to buy one U.S. dollar, with oil around $90 a barrel. At 3 million barrels a day, that's an oil bill exceeding a quarter-billion dollars daily — nearly $100 billion a year, paid in a currency Japan doesn't produce.

The sensitivity here is stark: a single one-point slip in the exchange rate (from 164 to 165 yen per dollar), with nothing else changing, adds roughly 100 billion yen to Japan's annual oil bill. The exchange rate acts almost like an invisible tax that rises every time the yen weakens.

## Why Japan Can't Simply Raise Interest Rates

Every country has one classic tool for defending a falling currency: raise interest rates. An interest rate is essentially the reward a saver earns for holding a currency instead of spending it or holding something else. If Japan offered savers, say, 5% interest to hold yen, money from around the world would flow in to capture that reward — and that buying pressure would push the yen's value back up.

But Japan's interest rate sits around just 1%, while domestic prices have been rising faster than that. A saver holding yen is effectively losing purchasing power even while technically earning interest — their money buys less at the grocery store than the interest gained. That makes yen unattractive to hold, discouraging exactly the kind of buying that would prop up its value.

So why not just raise rates? An interest rate cuts two ways: it's the reward savers earn, but it's also the cost borrowers pay — and the biggest borrower of yen is the Japanese government itself. Raising rates to attract savers would simultaneously spike the government's own borrowing costs on a debt pile already among the largest in the world relative to its economy. Japan is boxed in: it can't easily reward yen-holders without also punishing its own government's finances.

Compounding this, the Bank of Japan (Japan's central bank) has continued creating new money — about 2.5 trillion yen a month — to buy government bonds and artificially keep the government's borrowing costs low, a policy tool known as yield curve control. A weakening currency alongside a central bank still adding more of that currency to circulation is, Martin argues, the kind of pattern that usually shows up in fragile, developing economies — a notable claim to make about the world's third-largest economy, and one worth sitting with.

## Why America Reportedly Couldn't Let Japan Sell Its Treasuries

This is where the story pivots from "Japan's problem" to "everyone's problem."

If Japan needed dollars to defend the yen, the seemingly obvious move would be to sell some of that $1.1 trillion in U.S. Treasury bonds it holds, convert the proceeds to dollars, and use those dollars to buy yen. Textbook currency defense.

But that option was effectively off the table, because of basic bond math.

**A quick primer on bonds, prices, and yields:** A Treasury bond pays a fixed dollar amount of interest over its life. If a wave of selling pushes the bond's *price* down, that same fixed interest payment becomes a larger *percentage* of the new, lower price. Falling bond prices mean rising yields (effective interest rates) for anyone buying at the new price — that's how bond math works, full stop.

Had Japan started selling U.S. Treasuries by the hundreds of billions, it could have pushed bond prices down and yields up — meaning the U.S. government's own future borrowing would become more expensive. The timing made this especially sensitive: U.S. debt just passed $39.8 trillion, the government's annual interest bill is running near $1 trillion, and the yield on 30-year Treasury bonds recently climbed above 5.2% — the highest borrowing cost the U.S. has faced since 2007. Avoiding any additional upward pressure on Treasury yields in that environment is a clear priority for U.S. officials, and it's the throughline connecting Japan's currency trouble to America's own balance sheet.

## The Machine: Joint Intervention and a Little-Known Fed Facility

Here's where the mechanics get genuinely interesting, and this part is documented policy rather than speculation.

Beyond the direct joint U.S.-Japan currency purchases, Japan turned to a lending program at the Federal Reserve known as the **FIMA Repo Facility** (Foreign and International Monetary Authorities repo facility), created during the 2020 financial panic. Think of it as a pawn shop for governments: a foreign central bank brings its U.S. Treasury bonds to the Fed as collateral and receives freshly created dollars in return, without having to sell the bonds outright. When the loan is repaid, the country gets its bonds back.

By using this facility, Japan can raise dollars to buy yen without dumping Treasuries into the open market, sidestepping the bond-price problem entirely. The facility has a cap — currently $60 billion per country — and the U.S. Treasury Secretary has publicly asked the Fed to consider raising that limit.

### Where do the new dollars actually come from?

When the Fed lends against that collateral, it creates new dollars — a normal function of central banking. Those dollars flow to Japan, get sold for yen, but then don't simply disappear — they circulate into the broader U.S. economy and financial markets.

Martin points to two ironies worth noting:

- **It may undercut its own goal.** A currency strengthens when the world wants to hold more of it. But if the newly created dollars flow into red-hot U.S. markets, that makes dollar assets even more attractive relative to yen — potentially encouraging more selling of yen, not less.
- **It works against the Fed's other stated goal.** The Fed has said it wants to fight inflation, which generally means making dollars scarcer, not more abundant. Creating fresh dollars to fund this rescue pulls in the opposite direction.

What's not in dispute: none of this rescue changes Japan's underlying structural problems. It's still 230% debt-to-GDP, still importing 97% of its oil, and its central bank is still printing new yen every month even as the rescue tries to prop the currency up.

## The Big One: What Is the "Yen Carry Trade"?

This is the mechanism that connects a currency crisis in Tokyo to portfolios everywhere else.

The **yen carry trade** is a strategy that's existed in various forms for roughly three decades, built on Japan's historically ultra-low interest rates:

1. **Borrow cheap.** Investors around the world borrow yen because Japan has charged next to nothing in interest for decades.
2. **Convert to dollars.** The borrowed yen gets converted into dollars (or other currencies).
3. **Invest for a higher return.** That money then buys assets that pay more than the cost of the yen loan — U.S. Treasury bonds, tech stocks, real estate, you name it.

The profit is the gap between what the investment earns and what the yen loan costs — like borrowing money interest-free from a relative and lending it to a friend at 5%. Over 30 years, this has added up to trillions of dollars of borrowed yen quietly flowing into markets worldwide, helping support asset prices broadly. This is a widely recognized phenomenon among economists and market strategists, not a fringe theory.

**The catch:** the trade only works while yen stays cheap to borrow. If Japanese interest rates rise — even a little — that "free" loan starts costing real money, and investors holding the trade have an incentive to unwind it: sell the stocks and bonds they bought, and buy back yen to repay the loan. If many investors do this at once, it can hit multiple markets simultaneously — not because anything is wrong with those assets, but because leveraged positions are being closed out in a rush.

### The 2024 Preview

This isn't hypothetical. In August 2024, the Bank of Japan raised interest rates by just a quarter of one percent — a small move by most standards. Japan's stock market fell 12.4% in a single day, its worst day since 1987, and the S&P 500 had its worst day in about two years shortly after. That's documented history, and it's the clearest real-world evidence that yen carry-trade unwinds can transmit stress globally within hours.

Martin also cites data showing that bets against the yen (traders "shorting" it, expecting further declines) currently sit at their highest level on record, going back to at least 2010. That kind of positioning matters because it means a huge number of traders are on the same side of a bet that can only be closed one way: by eventually buying yen back. If the yen unexpectedly strengthens — from a surprise rate move or an effective rescue — those short sellers get squeezed and have to buy yen fast, a scenario that could ripple into a broader carry-trade unwind resembling August 2024. If instead the yen keeps falling, Japan's oil bill keeps growing, and the rescue effort would likely need to expand further, with more newly created dollars and probably a higher borrowing limit at the Fed's facility. Either direction keeps this story live for months to come.

## Japan as a Preview of the American Playbook

One of Martin's more provocative arguments is that Japan effectively pioneered the very tools Western central banks — including the U.S. Federal Reserve — now use routinely: near-zero interest rates (Japan, 1999; the Fed, roughly a decade later), central-bank bond-buying to stimulate the economy (Japan, 2001; the term "quantitative easing" entered common U.S. use around 2008), and deliberately capping government borrowing costs (Japan, 2016).

These dates and policy innovations are broadly accurate. Japan really did pursue zero rates, large-scale bond purchases, and yield-curve control years ahead of most Western economies, largely in response to its own asset bubble collapsing in the early 1990s after a period when Japanese markets and real estate were, by some famous estimates, valued extraordinarily high.

Martin's broader thesis — that the U.S. is now walking a version of that same road, with its own high debt-to-GDP ratio, rising interest costs, and elevated 30-year Treasury yields — is an interpretive comparison, not a proven prediction. The U.S. does have real structural advantages Japan lacks, most notably that the U.S. dollar remains the world's dominant reserve and trade currency, giving the U.S. more room and more sustained global demand for its debt. Whether that advantage is durable enough to change the ending is the open question at the heart of his argument.

## The Speaker's Bottom Line

Martin's ultimate claim is that the intervention itself is a signal. When the world's largest borrower creates new money specifically to make sure its largest lender never has to sell its Treasury holdings, that tells you what officials are most worried about — not really a falling yen, but the possibility of a disorderly U.S. bond market. It's a reasoned argument built from real facts (the size of Japan's holdings, the mechanics of the FIMA facility, the bond-price math), but it's ultimately his judgment about motive, not a confirmed statement of policy intent from the Fed or Treasury.

He extends this into a broader claim about how heavily indebted governments tend to manage debt they can't realistically repay in full: rather than defaulting outright, they let their currency's value erode gradually over time, effectively repaying old debts with money that buys less than it used to — a pattern economists sometimes call "financial repression" or, informally, "inflating away debt." Whether it plays out again in the specific way and timeframe Martin suggests remains to be seen.

## Why This Might Matter to You

Strip away the acronyms, and the throughline is this: a strategy built on cheap Japanese money has quietly helped inflate asset prices around the world for decades. If that strategy starts unwinding — whether triggered by a stronger yen, a Japanese rate hike, or simple investor panic — the effects likely wouldn't stay confined to Japan. The August 2024 episode is real-world proof that even a small shift in Japanese rates can ripple into U.S. stock markets within hours.

At the same time, the U.S. Treasury market — the foundation many other financial markets are priced against — depends heavily on large, stable buyers like Japan continuing to hold rather than sell. The mechanisms built to keep that buyer in place — joint intervention, the Fed's repo facility, newly created dollars — are real and documented, buying time without solving the underlying problem.

What's not in dispute is that a 40-year low in the yen, a record U.S. national debt, and a positioning bet at its most extreme level on record are all happening at the same time. What that combination ultimately produces is something markets — and this article's readers — will simply have to watch unfold.
