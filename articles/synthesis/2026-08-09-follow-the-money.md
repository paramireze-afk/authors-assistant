# Follow the Money: How Pro-Israel Political Spending Actually Works in American Elections

## Introduction

In 1982, an Illinois congressman named Paul Findley — a Republican who'd met repeatedly with Yasser Arafat and called himself "Arafat's best friend in Congress" — lost his seat to a young challenger named Dick Durbin. In 2026, a Kentucky libertarian named Thomas Massie, who'd never expressed much interest in the Israeli-Palestinian conflict one way or the other but voted against unconditional military aid and opposed U.S. strikes on Iran, lost his seat too. Forty-four years apart, opposite parties, opposite ideological traditions — and in both cases, organizations describing themselves as pro-Israel played a documented, and in Massie's case massively larger, role in the defeat.

What changed between 1982 and 2026 is not the goal but the mechanism: from personal fundraising networks and legislative arm-twisting, through a proliferating web of small PACs, through a presidential election where the lobby's influence ran almost entirely through donor relationships and personnel rather than dollar totals, to a single super PAC now capable of outspending most House campaigns many times over. That evolution is this article's spine. Each stage gets a dollar figure wherever the record supports one — **documented**, **estimated** (a defensible range), or **not reliably quantifiable** — and money bundled through an organization is counted once, not double-counted as individual giving. Where organizations pursuing *opposite* outcomes both describe themselves as pro-Israel, their figures are kept separate rather than merged into a single total implying unity of purpose. "Israeli-government spending: none documented in the records reviewed" is used throughout rather than a flat zero, since foreign-national contributions are illegal and therefore structurally hard to observe even if they existed. And a donor is only counted as "pro-Israel" where there's a documented organizational tie or stated motivation — never merely because the donor is Jewish.

Four interpretive lenses recur throughout rather than getting a separate theory section: Mearsheimer and Walt's argument that <cite index="60-1">a loose coalition of organizations, not a foreign government, shapes U.S. policy toward Israel</cite>; Scott Horton's view that campaign spending is one tool among several — think tanks, personnel networks — that a foreign-policy faction uses; Murray Rothbard's prediction that such spending's value lies less in what it buys any single candidate than in what it signals to everyone watching; and Max Blumenthal's investigative reporting, used here only where independently corroborated.

## Part One: The mechanism, stage by stage

### Stage 1 — Origins: the 1962–63 FARA episode

<cite index="184-1">In November 1962, the Justice Department under Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy ordered the American Zionist Council to register as a foreign agent, based on funding from the Jewish Agency for Israel; former AZC personnel then reorganized its lobbying under a newly incorporated entity, the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, and the registration order was never enforced against it</cite>. This settled, administratively, the question Massie tried to reopen sixty years later — whether advocacy supporting a foreign state's interests counts as domestic or foreign agency. **No dollar figure applies; institutional, not financial.**

### Stage 2 — Legislative pressure and the first electoral trophies (1978–1984)

Through the late 1970s, AIPAC's influence ran through lobbying against specific arms sales (F-15s to Saudi Arabia under Carter, AWACS aircraft under Reagan in 1981) rather than campaigns. Then came two defeats still cited today. <cite index="169-1">AIPAC declared Findley its "number one enemy" and coordinated pro-Israel PAC fundraising for Durbin, who raised over 80 percent of his $750,000 nationally and won by fewer than 1,500 votes</cite>.

> **Documented pro-Israel political support (Findley/Durbin, 1982): approximately $600,000** — an estimate from contemporaneous reporting, not an itemized FEC total.

Two years later, <cite index="170-1">Senator Charles Percy of Illinois lost partly over Middle East policy, prompting AIPAC executive director Thomas Dine to tell an audience, "All the Jews in America, from coast to coast, gathered to oust Percy"</cite> — a deliberate public statement of cause and effect. <cite index="169-1">The Washington Post later termed the pattern the "Percy Factor," describing a chilling effect on congressional Israel speech that outlasted both races by decades</cite>. **Documented dollar figure: not reliably quantifiable** — the clearest case in this article where the claimed *effect* is better documented than the spending that supposedly produced it, worth remembering in Part Three.

### Stage 3 — The PAC-bundling era (mid-1980s–2002)

Influence now ran through a proliferating network of small, state-based PACs — <cite index="158-1">NORPAC, MOPAC, SUNPAC, CITYPAC, Washington PAC, Mid-Manhattan PAC</cite> — each capped per candidate but coordinated enough to add up. In 2002, two Southern Black Democrats critical of Israeli policy lost primaries the same summer: <cite index="164-1">Earl Hilliard of Alabama to Artur Davis</cite>, backed by <cite index="160-1">$61,567 in pro-Israel PAC money</cite>, and weeks later <cite index="165-1">Cynthia McKinney of Georgia to Denise Majette, who received over $250,000 from pro-Israel donors including the AIPAC network</cite>, in a race where <cite index="162-1">Majette outraised McKinney roughly 7-to-1</cite> — aided by heavy Republican crossover voting in Georgia's open primary, a confound <cite index="161-1">contemporaneous reporting credited alongside pro-Israel spending, not instead of it</cite>.

> **Documented: Hilliard/Davis, at least $61,567; McKinney/Majette, over $250,000.** Broader network total: **not reliably quantifiable.**

### Stage 4 — 1992: donors, personnel, and a three-way race (before spending totals meant much)

By the early 1990s the lobby's leverage ran almost entirely through relationships and fundraising networks rather than independent expenditures — which barely existed yet as a legal category — making 1992 the clearest case for why searching for an "AIPAC spending total" in this era misses most of what actually happened.

**Bush** entered his re-election campaign fresh off a public war with the lobby. In September 1991, resisting Israel's request for $10 billion in loan guarantees while Yitzhak Shamir's government continued West Bank settlement construction, <cite index="200-1">Bush told reporters, "There are 1,000 lobbyists up on the Hill today lobbying Congress for loan guarantees for Israel and I'm one lonely little guy down here asking Congress to delay its consideration... for 120 days"</cite> — <cite index="196-1">a mobilization organized by the Conference of Presidents of Major American Jewish Organizations and AIPAC</cite>. <cite index="199-1">A poll immediately afterward found 86 percent of Americans backed Bush's request</cite>, and <cite index="200-1">Congress and AIPAC ultimately dropped the fight over the delay</cite> — a rare instance in this article of the lobby losing a direct confrontation with a sitting president. <cite index="203-1">The U.S. eventually guaranteed the loans in 1992 under a formula offsetting Israeli settlement spending</cite>. The consequence wasn't fundraising totals but the Jewish vote itself: <cite index="229-1">Bush's share collapsed to 11 percent in 1992</cite>, and <cite index="202-1">political scientist Michael Desch and others have argued the episode contributed meaningfully to his defeat</cite> — a claim about electoral consequence, not a dollar figure, and treated here as contested rather than proven, since 1992 turned on many issues (the economy above all).

**Clinton's** relationship with the lobby ran through personnel more than direct contributions. <cite index="219-1">Steve Grossman — who as Massachusetts Democratic chair had helped deliver Clinton's 20-point victory in his home state — simultaneously sat on AIPAC's board and became its president in 1992</cite>, later <cite index="220-1">resigning AIPAC to chair the DNC at Clinton's request in 1997</cite>. <cite index="231-1">Hollywood donors including David Geffen, Steven Spielberg, and Barbra Streisand fundraised enthusiastically for Clinton</cite>, though no consolidated Israel-specific dollar total from this network survives in available records. The episode that best illustrates how access-based influence actually operated — and its risks — came from Grossman's predecessor: <cite index="206-1">on October 22, 1992, outgoing AIPAC president David Steiner, unaware he was being recorded by New York businessman Harry Katz, boasted of a "dozen people" already inside Clinton's campaign headquarters "going to get big jobs," claimed input on the choice of secretary of state, and said AIPAC had secured "$10 billion in loan guarantees... $3 billion in foreign, in military aid, and... almost a billion dollars in other goodies that people don't even know about"</cite>. <cite index="211-1">When the tape surfaced two days after Clinton's election, Steiner resigned, apologizing to Clinton and Baker and admitting his statements "went beyond over-zealousness and exaggeration and were simply and totally untrue"</cite> — a crucial distinction: Steiner's *access* to campaign staff was real (Grossman's dual role alone establishes that), but his specific claims of brokered appointments and secret deals were self-described boasting, not verified fact, and should be read as evidence of how AIPAC insiders *wanted to be perceived* rather than proof of an actual quid pro quo.

> **Documented pro-Israel support for Bush (1992): not reliably quantifiable — the defining dynamic was confrontation and lobbying pressure, not campaign spending.**
> **Documented pro-Israel support for Clinton (1992): not reliably quantifiable in dollars — the defining dynamic was personnel overlap (Grossman) and donor enthusiasm (Hollywood network), neither itemized by Israel-specific motivation in available records.**

**Perot's** case tests the claim directly, and the evidence doesn't support it. <cite index="216-1">Perot personally expressed strong pro-Israel sentiment</cite> and <cite index="214-1">donated to an Israeli hospital and admired Ariel Sharon</cite>, but <cite index="213-1">JTA described him in April 1992 as "an unknown quantity when it comes to Israel," a cipher on Middle East policy specifically</cite>. No source reviewed shows AIPAC, NORPAC, or any pro-Israel PAC contributing to or coordinating with Perot's campaign — structurally unlikely in any case, since <cite index="102-1">Perot's campaign was self-funded, leaving little room for PAC influence of the kind that mattered in Findley or Percy's races</cite>. What the record *does* show is strategic calculation on the sidelines: <cite index="213-1">AIPAC executive director Tom Dine called Perot "a believer in the Israeli way of defending itself" and said he'd "probably be very friendly," while National PAC coordinator Stuart Levine welcomed Perot precisely because "he's somebody that the process has to pay a lot of attention to"</cite> — with <cite index="213-1">reporting noting some pro-Israel activists saw Perot as a means of blocking a Bush second term</cite>, given the loan-guarantee fight still fresh. This is strategic hedging by individuals reacting to a three-way race, not organizational funding of Perot.

> **Documented pro-Israel support for Perot: $0 in identified PAC or organizational contributions.** Individual sentiment and strategic commentary favoring him existed and is documented above, but does not constitute financial support. The accurate story is that pro-Israel organizations and commentators *responded* to an unpredictable three-way race by hedging their rhetoric — not that they built or bankrolled Perot's candidacy.

Was 1992 unusual, or the norm? The evidence says it was the norm through at least 2008 — relational, personnel-based, hard to itemize in dollars — and that the real break came later. <cite index="245-1">In 2012, Sheldon and Miriam Adelson, Sheldon self-described as a "one-issue person. That issue is Israel," became the largest individual donors in American political history, giving over $93 million to super PACs opposing Obama's reelection</cite>, first Newt Gingrich's, then Mitt Romney's. **Documented, Adelsons vs. Obama, 2012: $93 million** — a genuine break from 1992's model, since it's personal-fortune spending enabled by *Citizens United* (2010), running alongside a candidate rather than through the party-personnel channels that defined Grossman and Steiner. <cite index="238-1">The Adelsons then gave $25 million directly to Trump's 2016 campaign and another $75 million in 2020</cite>. **Documented: $25M (2016), $75M+ (2020).** A third, distinct mechanism appears in those same two elections: white evangelical voters, for whom Israel support is often a stated priority, backed Trump by wide margins, while Christian Zionist organizations' own disclosed FEC spending stayed near zero (see below) — turnout, not dollars, doing the work. Presidential-level influence has thus run through at least three separate channels — relational access (1992), megadonor personal fortunes (2012–2020), and bloc voter turnout (2016, 2020) — none resembling the targeted, single-race independent-expenditure model that reached House primaries only after 2021.

### Stage 5 — Citizens United and the transitional case (2010–2020)

The 2010 *Citizens United* ruling legalized unlimited independent expenditures, but AIPAC didn't use the tool for another decade. The transitional case: <cite index="54-1">Jamaal Bowman's 2020 defeat of longtime incumbent Eliot Engel, while AIPAC still avoided direct campaign spending to preserve its neutral image</cite>. Adjacent groups filled the gap — <cite index="49-1">DMFI spent over $600,000 for Engel, NORPAC gave $132,509</cite> — but Bowman won. <cite index="54-1">AIPAC's own account treats this loss as the direct trigger for abandoning lobbying-only strategy</cite>.

> **Documented (Engel/Bowman, 2020): at least $732,509.** The same era shows the tool's limits even after AIPAC formed its PAC: <cite index="185-1">Americans for Tomorrow's Future spent roughly $2.5 million trying to defeat Ilhan Omar in 2020 — and she won anyway</cite>.

### Stage 6 — The super PAC era (2021–present)

AIPAC formed a PAC in 2021 and, with United Democracy Project, <cite index="187-1">spent nearly $50 million across the 2022 cycle, $26 million of it UDP's independent expenditures</cite>. That cycle produced both a marquee win — **Andy Levin vs. Haley Stevens** (Michigan), where <cite index="34-1">UDP spent $3.7–4 million</cite> backing Stevens over Levin, notably <cite index="32-1">backing the non-Jewish candidate over the Jewish one</cite>, while <cite index="33-1">J Street spent $700,000 the other direction</cite> (hawkish and dovish figures kept separate, not summed) — and a clear failure: <cite index="194-1">an AIPAC-backed challenger to Rashida Tlaib lost comfortably despite $100,000+ in PAC support</cite>; by 2024 <cite index="169-1">Tlaib ran entirely unopposed by pro-Israel spending</cite>.

By 2024, scale jumped again: <cite index="4-1">$126.9 million combined across the cycle</cite>. **Jamaal Bowman vs. George Latimer** (NY-16) set the modern record — <cite index="12-1">UDP's $14.5 million was more than any outside group had ever spent on a single House race</cite>, plus <cite index="20-1">$1.1 million from DMFI</cite>, in ads that <cite index="18-1">never mentioned Israel</cite>. **Cori Bush vs. Wesley Bell** (MO-1) drew <cite index="21-1">$8.5–9 million, split roughly $3.4 million supporting Bell and $5.2 million opposing Bush</cite>. The same cycle: <cite index="18-1">UDP spent $4.6 million trying to stop Dave Min's primary and lost</cite>, prompting <cite index="110-1">a Prospect analysis to call the group's first 2024 attempt so unsuccessful its material impact might be overstated relative to dollars spent</cite>. A bridge case, **Nina Turner vs. Shontel Brown** (2021 special) — **documented range $1.4–2.4 million** <cite index="42-1">(DMFI $941K–$1.9M)</cite> plus <cite index="44-1">Pro-Israel America $500K+</cite> — shows the small-PAC pattern scaling toward, but not yet reaching, super PAC totals.

The 2026 cycle produced this article's two most important modern cases. **Cori Bush's comeback** drew <cite index="26-1">just $3 million from UDP — a third of 2024's total — yet Bell won by a wider margin (59–37) than in 2024</cite>, a genuinely puzzling result revisited below. **Thomas Massie vs. Ed Gallrein** (KY-4) set the modern spending record: <cite index="76-1">total race spending reached $32–37 million, with pro-Israel-specific spending at $9–15.8 million</cite> depending on outlet (excluding <cite index="77-1">$7 million+ from explicitly Trump-loyalty-motivated PACs</cite>, kept separate). Chronology matters: <cite index="182-1">Massie introduced H.R. 8809, the "Americans Insist on Political Agent Clarity Act," on May 14, 2026 — five days *before* the May 19 primary, not after losing it</cite>. <cite index="184-1">The bill would amend FARA's "foreign principal" definition to explicitly cover domestic organizations whose stated mission advances a foreign state's interests</cite> — a direct legislative attempt to reopen Stage 1's 1962 question. He lost anyway.

### The Christian Zionist gap

<cite index="137-1">Christians United for Israel — roughly 10 million claimed members — reported $0 in campaign contributions and $0 in outside spending in the 2024 cycle</cite>, with only <cite index="70-1">$260,000 in lobbying</cite> disclosed. Documented Christian Zionist campaign spending across every case study above: **none found in the records reviewed** — a movement whose method is mobilization and lobbying, not FEC-disclosed campaign finance.

## Part Two: Deterrence — three levels of evidence

**Demonstrated capacity** is best documented: most targeted candidates across six decades lost, from Findley and Percy through Bowman, Bush, and Massie. **Explicit statements attributing behavior to the spending** are rarer but real: Dine's 1984 remark was a deliberate public message; <cite index="44-1">Justice Democrats explicitly cited being "outgunned" as why it skipped Turner's 2022 rematch</cite>; <cite index="112-1">Congressional Progressive Caucus leadership recommended banning primary super PAC spending after the 2024 losses</cite>. **Circumstantial deterrence** — members moderating positions without saying so — is real in kind but unverifiable by definition. Levin's diagnosis gets closest: <cite index="31-1">"AIPAC never runs on AIPAC... if you're just a voter and you see a million ads, you don't know who paid for it"</cite>. What the record doesn't support is extrapolating from these specific, recurring cases to most members of Congress, who have never been targeted at all. The Bush 2026 data point — a third of 2024's spending, a wider margin of victory — is consistent with Rothbard's framework that once a demonstration has been made, credibility may not require repeating it at full scale.

## Part Three: Counterexamples and complications

Failures matter as much as successes: <cite index="185-1">$2.5 million failed against Omar in 2020; AIPAC stayed entirely out of her closer 2022 race "apparently to avoid an embarrassing loss"</cite>; Tlaib beat an AIPAC-backed opponent and later ran unopposed; <cite index="18-1">$4.6 million failed against Dave Min</cite>; even Bush's own 1991–92 loan-guarantee fight shows a sitting president successfully facing the lobby down, at real but limited political cost. A documented counterweight exists: <cite index="111-1">J Street raised $3 million for a 2026 super PAC explicitly framed against AIPAC's spending, after bundling $6 million for Harris in 2024</cite> — still a fraction of UDP's totals but proof "pro-Israel" spans organizations that spend against each other. And a meaningful share of nominally pro-Israel money now traces to donors whose primary loyalty is partisan rather than Israel-specific (Republican megadonors in Bowman's race, Trump loyalists in Massie's) — a blurring the tighter, more consistently Israel-focused donor networks of 1982, 1984, and 2002 show less of.

---

## Conclusion

The mechanism jumped roughly an order of magnitude at each structural change: personal networks and lobbying pressure in 1992 (no reliable dollar total), low six figures in Findley's 1982 race, several hundred thousand by McKinney's 2002 defeat, and $126.9 million across 2024 alone once AIPAC built its super PAC. What didn't change is the underlying claim, stated explicitly by Dine in 1984 and implicitly by every ad since: that crossing this line carries a public, demonstrated, durable cost. What also didn't change: the Israeli government itself is not a documented participant in any of it, the spending fails often enough to falsify a deterministic reading, and a real if outspent domestic counter-coalition — from J Street to a president willing to say no in 1991 — has existed at every stage.
