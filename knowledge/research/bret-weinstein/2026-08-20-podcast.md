# Evaporative Cooling and the Making of Fanatics

In late August 2026, Bret Weinstein and Heather Heying opened an episode of their DarkHorse Podcast with a promise: what they were about to describe would change how many listeners saw the political and cultural landscape around them, and possibly their own place in it. The occasion was grim. Jason Arday, who in 2023 had become one of the youngest Black professors ever appointed at Cambridge University, had resigned his position and, days later, died by suicide. In between, his career had been dismantled in public — page by page, claim by claim — by critics who argued that his scholarship was built on plagiarism and his biography on fabrication.

Weinstein and Heying use the Arday story as a doorway. Their real subject is bigger: a model for why fanaticism — genuine, self-reinforcing extremism — keeps breaking out across so many unrelated arenas at once, from COVID policy to race science to climate change to urban crime, and why the people caught in the middle of these fights so often find themselves accused not of being wrong, but of being bad.

## The Arday Case: What Happened

The public unraveling began with a lengthy essay published in July by Nathan Cofnas, an academic with a background in biology, who went through the ten publications Arday himself had listed on his Cambridge faculty page as his most notable work. According to Cofnas's accounting, four of the ten showed clear evidence of plagiarism, eight had no data or scientific analysis at all, and in three, Arday's own contribution appeared to be minimal — he was one of several listed authors on projects he seemingly did little to produce.

Alongside the scholarship questions came a set of increasingly outlandish personal claims: brain tumors, a testicular tumor, an assault by four youths that supposedly caused epilepsy, a coma after being hit by a car, months of illiteracy followed by a rapid rise to academic stardom, and endurance feats — hundreds of miles run in a matter of days, dozens of marathons in a matter of weeks — that shifted in the retelling and that journalists were unable to verify beyond Arday's own say-so. When the Guardian's Lanre Bakare investigated some of his more elaborate harassment claims and found they didn't hold up, Arday's response, as Weinstein and Heying note with some astonishment, was essentially that he had assumed people would simply take his word for it.

In a Times of London interview published shortly before his death, Arday attributed errors in his early work to autism and inadequate supervision, and argued that anyone's work would show similar flaws under the same level of scrutiny. He also described the campaign against him as racially and ableism-motivated. Separately, a university misconduct panel that had earlier cleared him of a plagiarism complaint — despite textual overlap with another scholar's work that was calculated to have roughly a one-in-100-billion chance of coincidence — concluded that the overlap was within the "accepted range" of academic standards at the time.

Following his death, mourners gathered by the thousands in Trafalgar Square. A rival narrative also emerged, published in the left-leaning outlet Byline Times, which attempted to tie Cofnas and his allies to a network of race-science figures connected to Peter Thiel.

## Two Readings, and Where the Hosts Land

Weinstein and Heying treat this as a story with real tension in it rather than a clean morality tale for either side. They're unpersuaded by Arday's defense — Heying is blunt that the claim "everyone's work would look this bad under scrutiny" is simply untrue, and that academic work is not, in fact, built on a foundation of unverifiable gossip. At the same time, they're equally unimpressed by the Byline Times piece, which they describe as relying heavily on guilt-by-association reasoning — noting who appeared at events with whom — rather than engaging seriously with the substance of the plagiarism findings. Weinstein draws a comparison to the O.J. Simpson trial: just as the discovery of a racist detective on that case didn't make Simpson innocent, the fact that Cofnas has an ideological axe to grind (he has said, apparently at least partly in jest, that he hopes to one day chair a "Department of Eugenics and Race Science") doesn't make his documentation of plagiarism wrong. Both things, in their view, can be true at once, and treating the story as though it must resolve into one clean narrative or the other is itself a symptom of the larger problem they want to describe.

Where the hosts push furthest past the available facts is in their causal story about *why* Arday ended up where he did. Their argument is that Cambridge, and elite academia more broadly, effectively "hired him to do exactly what he was doing." In their reading, DEI-era institutions created a niche in which certain protected candidates were exempted from the ordinary standards of scrutiny applied to scholarship, and Arday — whatever his underlying talents or disadvantages — rose by exploiting that niche, producing papers that recycled fashionable claims about racism in the academy without data or argument to back them, because that is what the institution rewarded. Heying goes further, describing him at one point as having been treated like "Cambridge's mascot" — celebrated, promoted, and displayed, but never actually held accountable, which is its own form of institutional cruelty. This is an interpretation, not a documented fact about Cambridge's hiring intentions, but it is the interpretive lens through which the hosts view the entire episode, and it sets up the larger argument that follows: that the tragedy isn't really about one man's fabrications, but about an institution that built the conditions for exactly this outcome and will, in their view, decline to reckon with its own role in producing it.

## The Core Model: Why Fanaticism Spreads

From here, the conversation pivots to the piece Weinstein calls the real point of the episode: a general model of how ordinary people — not obviously unstable or extreme people — end up becoming fanatics, and why that process seems to be happening simultaneously across so many disconnected topics.

His starting premise is that fanaticism tends to appear specifically around *complex* subjects, not simple ones. Simple, well-understood questions admit of being simply right or wrong. But complex systems — whether an academic discipline, a public health emergency, or a national economy — rarely resolve cleanly to one side. Given genuine complexity, you would expect to see a wide spectrum of reasonable positions, with a few people at either extreme and most people somewhere in the middle, weighting the available evidence in slightly different ways.

### Evaporative Cooling

The mechanism the hosts propose for why that spectrum collapses into two hardened camps borrows from an idea Weinstein credits to the writer Eliezer Yudkowsky: evaporative cooling. Yudkowsky's original illustration involved a doomsday cult that predicts the world will end at a specific hour. The hour passes; the world doesn't end. Some members of the cult — the ones with the least invested belief — conclude they were wrong and leave. The most committed true believers stay. The group that remains is, on average, more extreme than the group that existed a day earlier, not because anyone became more radical, but because the moderates self-selected out.

Weinstein extends the physical metaphor: in a container of gas, the most energetic particles are the ones most likely to escape, leaving behind a cooler, calmer-seeming — but actually more concentrated and volatile — remainder. Applied to ideological or institutional communities, the mechanism is the same. Every time a movement's predictions fail or its evidence weakens, the people with the least fixed commitment to the belief tend to drift away, while the truest believers double down, and the community that's left behind is, structurally, more extreme than before.

The hosts extend this to real-world geography and politics. They point to the pattern of people frustrated by policy failures on the West Coast leaving for states perceived as better governed, which — in their account — concentrates the most committed true believers in the places left behind, unlike a physical gas, "the particles vote," reinforcing the same policies that prompted people to leave in the first place. They apply an asymmetric version of the same idea to the two major U.S. political parties: the Republican coalition, in their telling, has become filled with people who left other camps and now resist consensus as committed individualists, while the Democratic coalition has become comparatively more homogeneous and collectivist — making it easier, in their account, to coordinate and enforce internal conformity.

### From Analysis to Morality

The second piece of the model concerns what happens to disagreement once a group has been evaporatively cooled toward one pole. In an ordinary analytical conversation between people who trust each other, disagreement is just disagreement — evidence of interesting complexity, something to dig into together. But inside a cooled, concentrated group, the hosts argue, dissent stops being read as an analytical claim and starts being read as a moral one. Someone who raises a nuanced or contrary point isn't treated as offering an alternative reading of the evidence; they're treated as having revealed something defective about their character. The telltale sign, in Weinstein's words, is the experience of suddenly having to defend your decency in what started out as a conversation about facts.

This matters because it changes the incentives facing everyone watching. Punishing nuance doesn't just silence the person expressing it — it teaches an entire audience that straying from the two accepted camps carries real social and professional cost, while going along with whichever camp is dominant in your environment costs nothing. Most people, quite rationally, choose the second option. The middle position — often the analytically strongest one — depopulates, not because it's wrong, but because occupying it is expensive.

Heying connects this to a separate, longstanding observation of hers about the modern academy: a widening gap, as you move from the hard sciences through the social sciences into the humanities and arts, between analysis and story. In her account, the social sciences in particular have increasingly substituted narrative and "lived experience" for hypothesis-testing and data — treating a personal account as inherently truthful and its interpretation as beyond dispute. That substitution, she argues, is a big part of why Arday's most-cited papers could consist of interviews with a small, conveniently accessible group of people and sweeping claims about systemic racism with little supporting evidence: the discipline itself had stopped requiring analysis as a condition of being taken seriously.

## Case Studies in the Pattern

The hosts run the model against several live controversies to show how they think it plays out:

- **COVID and vaccination.** They argue that a reasonable, evidence-weighing middle position — broadly pro-vaccine but skeptical of mandates for people who'd already had the disease, for instance — was effectively unspeakable during the pandemic, because voicing any doubt about vaccination got recoded as being anti-vaccine altogether, and voicing any doubt about the severity of the disease got recoded as COVID denial. The two poles enforced a binary where none needed to exist.
- **Homelessness and urban policy.** They point to drug use and disorder in cities like Seattle and Portland as a test the housing-first consensus keeps failing, arguing that a genuinely evidence-driven movement would have revised its model well before now — and that its failure to do so is itself evidence of the evaporative-cooling dynamic at work, rather than of the policy being straightforwardly correct or incorrect on its own terms.
- **Climate.** Weinstein describes being criticized, in his account, for skepticism about aspects of climate science despite never having denied an anthropogenic effect on climate — because, he argues, the discourse allows only two positions (roughly, "the crisis is exactly as described" or "there's no real problem at all") with no acknowledged room for accepting the human effect while doubting particular predictions or the reliability of certain published research.
- **The "woke versus race-realist" split.** The hosts treat the reactionary edge of the anti-woke backlash — represented in their telling by figures like Cofnas — as a mirror image of the phenomenon they're describing on the identitarian left: an extreme, largely unfalsifiable claim (in this case, that population-level differences are essentially genetic and therefore not worth intervening on) that has hardened in reaction to an equally extreme claim on the other side (that competence differences are illusory and purely a function of power). Neither pole, in their reading, is actually engaging with the more complicated, more defensible position in between — that both cheating/credential inflation and real, potentially remediable developmental disparities are simultaneously in play.

## The Middle Ground Scramble

The final piece of the model addresses what happens after an extreme position finally runs its course — when, as Weinstein puts it, the bridges start to actually fall down and reality forces a reckoning.

The naive expectation is that once a movement's predictions are shown to be false, people who dissented early would be vindicated and their critics would credit them. The hosts argue this almost never happens, because the original accusation against the dissenter was never really analytical — it was moral. Being right doesn't undo having been "disloyal" or "bad," so there's no natural mechanism for rehabilitating heretics. Instead, what happens is what Weinstein calls the "middle ground scramble": the people who were most invested in the failed extreme position quietly stake out a new, more moderate-sounding stance, without acknowledging that the people they spent years attacking held something close to that position all along.

As an illustration, the hosts point to a recent clip of Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez discussing the "defund the police" era, in which she acknowledges — with a local official's phrase "Woke 1 was crazy" offered half-jokingly — that the movement's rhetoric around policing went further than was warranted, partly as a product of the extraordinary conditions of 2020 and the pandemic. Weinstein and Heying's objection isn't to the substance of that concession; it's to the absence of any acknowledgment that people who said as much in real time were, at the time, treated as morally suspect for saying it — including, they note pointedly, people who lost relationships over vaccine status during the same period.

## Why the Hosts Think This Matters

The stakes, as Weinstein frames them, go beyond any single controversy. If disagreement is reliably recast as a referendum on someone's character rather than a question about evidence, then a society loses its ability to use its own truth-seeking institutions — journalism, courts, universities — for their actual purpose: sorting out what's true when the answer isn't obvious. That, in his view, is precisely why free-speech fights matter so much right now, not just as an abstract civil-liberties principle but because the capacity to voice uncomfortable, non-conforming analysis is the only mechanism a "cooled," polarized system has left for correcting itself before it fails outright.

It's worth being clear about where the model sits epistemically. The mechanics of the Arday case — the plagiarism findings, the university panel's conclusions, the shifting endurance claims — are reasonably well documented in contemporaneous reporting. Weinstein and Heying's broader claims — that Cambridge effectively hired Arday to produce exactly this kind of work, that evaporative cooling explains polarization across COVID, climate, and urban policy alike, that "Woke 1" and race-realism are mirror-image fanaticisms of equal and opposite extremity — are their own interpretive framework, built out of analogy and pattern-matching across cases that most other commentators would treat separately. Listeners sympathetic to the hosts' general politics may find the model illuminating; listeners who don't share their read on any one of the underlying issues (vaccines, climate policy, group differences in ability) will likely find the framework harder to accept wholesale, since it leans on those same contested judgments to hold together. What the episode offers, in the end, is less a proof than a lens — one designed to make a familiar and often bewildering social experience (getting morally attacked for what felt like a factual disagreement) legible as the predictable output of an underlying game-theoretic process, rather than a series of unrelated, isolated incidents.
