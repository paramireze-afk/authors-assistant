# Joseph de Maistre's Defense of the Spanish Inquisition: A Beginner's Guide

*A summary of the "Letters on the Spanish Inquisition," based on the Imperium Press edition in its "Studies in Reaction" series*

---

## Why This Text Matters (and Why You Haven't Heard of It)

If you know the Spanish Inquisition at all, you probably picture torture chambers, burnings at the stake, and hooded monks handing down death sentences on a whim. That's the popular image — and it's the exact image Joseph de Maistre set out to dismantle, letter by letter, in 1815.

Despite being one of the most provocative documents of its kind, this text has stayed relatively obscure compared to de Maistre's more famous political writings. One suggested reason: the work defends a Catholic institution at a time — and in a publishing world — shaped by centuries of Protestant suspicion toward Catholicism, which may have discouraged wide readership.

This report walks through the text as a newcomer, laying out who wrote it, why, and what his five letters actually argue.

---

## Who Was Joseph de Maistre?

De Maistre wasn't a detached academic. He was a Savoyard nobleman who lived through the French Revolution and its aftermath, and he had a genuine legal and political career — serving the Sardinian court through turbulent years and later holding significant government posts. He's best known today for major political works, but this set of letters on the Inquisition is a smaller, more specialized piece: a direct, personal argument aimed at correcting what he saw as one of history's most successful smear campaigns.

## The Setting: Why 1815?

The timing is everything. The Spanish Inquisition had just been:
1. **Suppressed** by the revolutionary Cortez (Spain's parliamentary assembly) a few years earlier, and then
2. **Reestablished** shortly before de Maistre wrote.

He addressed his letters to a Russian nobleman — a real person with the typical, deeply negative view of the Inquisition shared by much of educated Europe at the time. De Maistre's goal was to change that friend's mind.

## His Central Rhetorical Trick

De Maistre's most clever move is this: rather than relying only on friendly, pro-Inquisition sources, he leans heavily on the **official 1812 report of the very Cortez committee that abolished the Inquisition** — in other words, the words of its enemies. His logic: if even a hostile, motivated body admits certain facts favorable to the Inquisition, those admissions carry extra weight, since the committee had every reason to say the opposite.

## The Five Letters: What Each One Argues

### Letter One: Correcting the Basic Facts

De Maistre opens by attacking what he calls three "capital errors" the public believes:
- That the Inquisition was purely a **church** operation
- That **priests themselves** handed down death sentences
- That people were condemned merely for **private beliefs** they never acted on

His central claim across the letter: the Inquisition was fundamentally a **royal institution**. The Spanish king appointed the Inquisitor General, who then appointed the other inquisitors — a direct chain of monarchical control, not clerical independence.

He also debunks a popular myth that St. Dominic founded the Inquisition, pointing out it was established at the Council of Verona in 1184, decades before Dominic's followers even got involved (around 1233, after Dominic's death). Instead, he traces its origins to the 12th-century threat posed by the **Cathars/Albigensians**, a dualist religious movement he describes as rejecting core Christian doctrines and, in his telling, threatening both church authority and civil order — which led to the brutal Albigensian Crusade.

De Maistre also directly rebuts **Voltaire**, who had famously called the Inquisition a "bloody tribunal" and "frightful monument of monkish power." De Maistre insists its composition — a handful of clergy alongside a majority of secular officials — makes that image false, and grounds this in a core Catholic principle he cites in Latin: *Ecclesia abhorret a sanguine* ("the Church abhors blood"), the traditional idea that clergy are barred from involvement in bloodshed, even to the point of being excused from witnessing executions.

**His account of the actual process:**
1. The Inquisition tries the case and determines heresy.
2. If guilty, the person's property is confiscated and they're handed to the **secular** authorities (the Council of Castile).
3. Civil law — not church law — determines punishment, including any death sentence.
4. In milder cases, punishment was often just public recantation (with social consequences, but no execution).

### Letter Two: Was Anyone Punished for Private Belief Alone?

Here de Maistre goes after the specific, dramatic claim (which he attributes partly to Montesquieu) that someone — the famous example being a young Jewish woman — was burned simply for silently holding a different faith. He calls this a "national crime" of an accusation and insists it's fabricated.

His actual argument: people who kept their beliefs private and didn't publicly agitate were generally left alone. It was **public dissent and active proselytizing** that drew scrutiny — and even then, only under established law, not the whim of judges.

He also defends the use of **torture**, not by praising it, but by pointing out it was standard practice across virtually all European legal systems at the time (with roots going back to ancient Greece and Rome) — so singling out the Inquisition for using a universal legal tool of the era is unfair, in his view. He notes that even the decision to torture required approval from a Supreme Council, which he presents as evidence of procedural restraint.

*Contrast provided in the notes:* De Maistre includes a grim account of prison and interrogation conditions under Elizabeth I's English state — rat-infested cells, starvation, thumb-suspension torture — arguing England's own record was, if anything, worse.

### Letter Three: The Inquisition as a Court of Mercy

This letter reframes the Inquisition as something closer to a **court of equity** — a body meant to soften the rigid, "one-size-fits-all" nature of law with judgment and mercy. He offers two anecdotes:
- A Madrid case where a woman living a scandalous double life was handled quietly rather than through a dramatic public trial, to spare an unsuspecting bishop's reputation.
- The case of the **Cuestas brothers**, clergymen falsely accused through the machinations of a powerful court favorite, whom an inquisitor investigated and cleared — even against significant pressure.

### Letter Four: Did the Inquisition Keep Spain at Peace?

This is de Maistre's boldest and most counterintuitive argument. While the rest of 16th–17th century Europe tore itself apart in religious wars (the Reformation, the Thirty Years' War, the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre, multiple regicides), **Spain avoided that fate for nearly three centuries** — and de Maistre credits this directly to the Inquisition, which he says preserved religious and social unity by suppressing the sparks of unrest before they could spread.

He uses an analogy: if one country avoids a plague that ravaged its neighbors, and that country used a particular remedy no one else used, it's reasonable to credit the remedy — even if you can't fully prove the mechanism.

He also pushes back on the idea that Spain's national spirit — and its resistance to Napoleon — came from Enlightenment ideas rather than traditional faith, crediting the Inquisition-shaped Catholic culture instead.

### Letter Five: England's Glass House

The final letter turns the mirror around. De Maistre argues that **England has the least moral standing of any nation to criticize Spain**, laying out England's own penal laws against Catholics:
- Returning to Catholicism was legally **treason**
- Missing Protestant church services carried a prison sentence
- Sheltering or being a Catholic priest could mean execution by hanging, drawing, and quartering

He cites specific cases (like the Jesuit priest Edmund Campion) and statistics on Catholic deaths and persecutions under Elizabeth I, arguing that, by the numbers and by legal severity, England's persecution of Catholics was harsher and broader in scope than what the Inquisition is typically accused of. His closing line of argument: a nation whose own house is "made of glass" (quoting a maxim he attributes to Voltaire) shouldn't throw stones.

He also flips a common claim on its head — arguing that **persecution was historically a Protestant tendency**, not a Catholic one, pointing to Luther's own harsh rhetoric, various English persecutions of dissenters and Catholics across multiple reigns, and the 1560 Scottish Parliament's decree of death for practicing Catholics.

## The Supporting "Notes": Extra Ammunition

Scattered through the letters, de Maistre's notes add supporting detail:
- **Comparative severity:** He compares the Inquisition unfavorably-for-England against England's own **Court of High Commission** under Elizabeth I, which he describes as having sweeping, largely unchecked power to punish based on rumor or suspicion.
- **Numbers:** He cites a source claiming only around 19 executions (15 men, 4 women) over a long period under the Inquisition — a small number he still concedes is "too many," but frames as modest relative to its reputation.
- **Rome as the gentle counter-example:** He repeatedly points to the **Roman Inquisition** as unusually mild — citing testimony that it hadn't executed anyone in over a century and that ordinary, discreet people had nothing to fear from it.
- **Debunking a travel writer:** He spends real energy mocking a Reverend Mr. Townsend, an English travel writer whose vivid (and, in de Maistre's view, exaggerated and selectively outraged) accounts had shaped much of the English public's fearful image of the Inquisition.

## The Big Picture: What Kind of Argument Is This?

Stepping back, de Maistre's overall strategy has a consistent shape:
1. **Reframe authorship** — the Inquisition was the king's tool, not the church's.
2. **Reframe blame** — executions were carried out by civil courts, not clergy.
3. **Reframe context** — torture and harsh punishment were common across all of Europe, not unique cruelties.
4. **Reframe outcomes** — Spain's centuries of relative peace are presented as evidence the institution "worked."
5. **Reframe the accusers** — England and Protestant Europe, he argues, have no moral high ground given their own persecution records.

## A Word of Caution

This is important to keep in mind: this is **one deeply partisan perspective**, written explicitly to counter what its author saw as anti-Catholic prejudice, and published today as part of a series that self-consciously promotes "reactionary" thought — a tradition that looks back critically at modern liberal and Enlightenment ideas. De Maistre selects and frames his evidence to build a case, much like a lawyer would. Mainstream historians of the Inquisition today generally hold more complex views — acknowledging that procedures were often more restrained and bureaucratic than popular myth suggests, while also documenting real cruelty, fear, and injustice that de Maistre's advocacy-driven account tends to minimize.

The most useful way to read this text is as a rhetorically skilled **defense brief**, not a neutral history — valuable for understanding how such a defense could be constructed, but not a substitute for broader historical scholarship on the subject.
