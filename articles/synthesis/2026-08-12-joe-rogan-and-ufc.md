# The House Always Watches: How Legalized Betting Remade the UFC

In May 2018, the U.S. Supreme Court struck down the Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act (PASPA) in *Murphy v. NCAA*, ending the federal ban on state-authorized sports gambling outside Nevada. Within a few years, a sport that had spent two decades fighting for basic legitimacy — banned in New York until 2016, dismissed by John McCain as "human cockfighting" in the 1990s — found itself sitting inside the fastest-growing marketing category in American sports. The Ultimate Fighting Championship did not create the modern sports-betting boom. But few sports properties have folded betting as deeply into their on-screen product, sponsorship revenue, and even legal exposure as the UFC has over the last five years.

This piece separates out what is documented — corporate filings, court records, published contracts and statements — from what is credibly reported by journalists, and from what remains speculative or contested. Where the evidence runs out, that is stated plainly rather than filled in.

## Who actually runs the UFC

It's worth mapping the ownership structure before anything else, because "the UFC" is now several layers removed from a single decision-maker.

- **Zuffa, LLC** is the operating company that holds the UFC's promotional rights and contracts with fighters.
- **TKO Group Holdings, Inc.** is the publicly traded parent formed in September 2023 through a merger of UFC and WWE, engineered by Endeavor. Beyond UFC and WWE, TKO also owns IMG, On Location, and the Professional Bull Riders (PBR).
- **Endeavor Group Holdings** was UFC's majority owner since 2016, when a WME-IMG-led investor group (including Silver Lake Partners) bought the promotion for roughly $4 billion. In March 2025, Endeavor itself was taken private in a $25 billion deal with Silver Lake; the public-facing entertainment agency business was rebranded **WME Group**, while Endeavor Group Holdings continued on as the private parent that controls TKO. As of TKO's most recent public filings, <cite index="2-1">Endeavor Group Holdings retains a 63.9% controlling ownership stake of publicly traded TKO Group.</cite>

The individuals most associated with UFC's business direction:

- **Dana White** — UFC's president since 2001, and the public face of the promotion. He is not TKO's CEO; that role belongs to Emanuel. White retains "creative and operational control" over UFC's fight business, according to industry reporting, while sitting below the TKO corporate structure. He also serves on TKO's board.
- **Ari Emanuel** — Executive Chairman and CEO of TKO Group, and also Executive Chairman of WME Group. <cite index="6-1">His 2025 compensation from TKO totaled $67.4 million, up sharply from $18.1 million the year before, driven by a large stock grant — a package that included a $3 million salary, roughly an $11.9 million cash bonus, $43.9 million in stock awards, and $8.1 million in non-equity incentive pay.</cite>
- **Mark Shapiro** — TKO's President and COO since the company's 2023 formation, and now also President and Managing Partner of WME Group. <cite index="6-1">His 2025 TKO compensation rose to $42.6 million from $31.9 million the year before.</cite> Shapiro has a long broadcast pedigree, including a stint running programming and production at ESPN in the early 2000s, which is relevant later in this piece given ESPN's own betting pivots.
- **Joe Rogan** — UFC's longtime color commentator, not a corporate officer or owner. His role and its relationship to UFC's business are discussed in a dedicated section below.

Two structural points are worth flagging without over-reading them. First, TKO is a public company, so its incentives are now legible in SEC filings in ways Zuffa's were not as a private entity under Endeavor alone — investors get quarterly visibility into litigation reserves, betting-partner revenue disclosures, and executive pay that fans and journalists did not have a decade ago. Second, the same small circle of executives — Emanuel and Shapiro in particular — now sits atop UFC, WWE, a major talent agency, and (via Shapiro and Emanuel's separate, recently reported acquisition of a minority stake in the NFL's Las Vegas Raiders) an ownership position in another major sports league. That overlapping web of interests is a matter of public record; whether it creates any specific conflict in UFC's own dealings is not something the available reporting establishes one way or the other.

## From Nevada-only wagering to a betting-saturated broadcast

Before 2018, legal sports betting in the U.S. was effectively confined to Nevada sportsbooks and informal offshore or local markets everywhere else. PASPA's repeal let states legalize and regulate their own markets, and dozens moved quickly. Mixed martial arts — with its two-fighter, moneyline-driven structure, frequent live-odds swings, and heavy in-fight variance (a single strike can end a bout) — turned out to be an unusually good fit for a betting product built around real-time engagement rather than season-long narratives.

The UFC's first major, formalized betting partnership came in February 2021, when <cite index="17-1">the UFC named DraftKings as its first official sportsbook and daily fantasy partner in the U.S. and Canada, in a five-year deal reported to be worth $350 million including marketing and cash</cite>. <cite index="17-1">Dana White called it "the most important deal we've ever done to increase engagement with our fans and reach new ones."</cite> The integration was immediately visible on screen: <cite index="17-1">beginning at UFC 259, a DraftKings-branded clock appeared on the Octagon itself and on broadcast graphics, counting down time remaining in each round</cite>, with sportsbook odds and prop-style content layered into subsequent broadcasts.

That DraftKings deal ran until it was replaced. In March 2026, <cite index="13-1">bet365 became the UFC's new Official Sports Betting Partner across the U.S. and Canada in a multiyear agreement</cite>, timed to coincide with UFC's move to a new broadcast home. <cite index="20-1">Under the bet365 deal, betting integration appears across every UFC event during the partnership term — sportsbook tickers, fighter odds, "intro odds," and same-game parlay features woven into the broadcast to give fans real-time betting information tied directly to the fight.</cite> TKO's own language around the switch was candid about the strategic goal: <cite index="11-1">TKO's senior vice president of global partnerships described bet365 as understanding "fight fans and how they engage with the sport in real time," and said the partnership was meant to enhance "the viewing experience by providing fans with deeper insights, dynamic odds, and more ways to engage."</cite>

This dovetails with a broader reshuffling of UFC's media footprint. <cite index="11-1">UFC's live events moved to Paramount+ at the start of 2026, taking over broadcasting duties that had been held by ESPN, with UFC 326 becoming the first numbered UFC pay-per-view event broadcast on CBS.</cite> The betting-partner switch and the broadcast-rights switch happened in close succession, which is a useful reminder that UFC's betting relationships are negotiated as part of a broader media-rights ecosystem, not standalone sponsorships bolted onto a fixed broadcast.

### DraftKings, FanDuel, and ESPN Bet: the wider gambling-media landscape UFC sits inside

UFC's betting story doesn't happen in isolation — it's downstream of a much larger fight for market share among American sportsbooks and the media companies that carry their advertising.

- **DraftKings and FanDuel** are the two dominant operators in the U.S. market by handle, and both have spent heavily on media and league partnerships across the "big four" leagues as well as UFC.
- **ESPN Bet** was ESPN's branded betting product, launched via a partnership with Penn Entertainment. That arrangement did not last: <cite index="16-1">ESPN and Penn Entertainment ended their 10-year, roughly $2 billion contract for ESPN Bet after just two years, with Penn's app rebranding as theScore Bet.</cite> <cite index="16-1">In its place, ESPN entered a new multiyear partnership with DraftKings, described by ESPN's own betting VP as wanting "a partner that's scaled, that puts an emphasis on innovation, that has similar ways of operating as ESPN does."</cite> <cite index="12-1">The new ESPN–DraftKings agreement, effective December 1, 2025, names DraftKings the exclusive Official Sportsbook and Odds Provider of ESPN, integrating DraftKings' sportsbook, daily fantasy, and prediction-market products across ESPN's platforms.</cite>
- Reporting on the ESPN Bet failure noted that <cite index="16-1">Penn had projected ESPN Bet would reach 20% market share by 2027, but the product instead hovered around 5%</cite> — a useful data point on just how difficult it has been for even a marquee media brand to dislodge FanDuel and DraftKings' entrenched position.

The upshot: UFC's own betting-partner churn (DraftKings to bet365) mirrors an industry-wide pattern of media companies and leagues rotating sportsbook partners as the two market leaders consolidate share and challengers (Penn/ESPN Bet, and now bet365's U.S. push) try to buy their way into relevance through marquee sports content rights.

## Has betting changed the viewing experience?

Yes, in ways that are directly documented rather than speculative. Odds and lines are now built into broadcast graphics; a betting-company clock has literally sat on the Octagon; and prop-style "same-game parlay" content is marketed as part of the broadcast package rather than something a separate app layers on top. Commentary teams routinely reference betting lines as a shorthand for a fighter's perceived chances, a practice common across American sports broadcasting generally but one that is relatively new to MMA broadcasts specifically, since the sport's mainstream betting market is only a few years old in most states.

Whether this has changed fan *behavior* — not just broadcast aesthetics — is harder to state definitively from public reporting alone. Industry and trade coverage (GlobalData's sponsorship-sector analysis, for instance) treats gambling sponsorships as now among the largest single sponsorship categories across American sports leagues generally, UFC included, but granular audience-engagement data tying betting integration to viewership or PPV buy-rate changes specifically for UFC is not something available reporting substantiates with hard numbers.

## New incentives — and where they've collided with controversy

Betting integration does more than change graphics; it changes what UFC has to defend, and it has coincided with a run of integrity controversies that predate — but have intensified alongside — the betting boom.

**The James Krause scandal (2022–2025).** In November 2022, a UFC bout between Darrick Minner (coached by James Krause) and Shayilan Nuerdanbieke was flagged for suspicious betting activity — sportsbooks saw <cite index="58-1">unusual betting interest on Minner to lose in the first round and for the fight to last fewer than 2.5 rounds; Minner lost by TKO just over a minute into the first round.</cite> <cite index="58-1">New Jersey gambling authorities halted wagering on any events associated with Krause, and two Canadian jurisdictions temporarily suspended betting on UFC events altogether, while multiple sources told ESPN the FBI was collecting information about the fight.</cite> <cite index="50-1">Krause was separately reported by ESPN to have worked for years as an agent for an offshore sportsbook, ABCBetting.ag, according to multiple people who had wagered with him.</cite> The UFC banned any fighter who continued training with Krause from competing in the organization. <cite index="51-1">In March 2025, the Nevada Athletic Commission finalized multi-year suspensions for two fighters connected to Krause's gym — Darrick Minner (29 months) and Jeff Molina (36 months) — with a Nevada deputy attorney general stating Molina had direct knowledge of his teammate's pre-fight injury, failed to disclose it, and wagered on the fight using that insider information.</cite> Dana White's own public comments at the time were blunt: <cite index="56-1">he said that if fighters were found to have manipulated a fight for betting purposes, "they're going to get cut. They're going to go f---ing Federal prison," calling it "not enough money in it to ruin your life."</cite>

**The Dulgarian–del Valle fight (November 2025).** <cite index="47-1">At UFC Vegas 110 on November 1, 2025, featherweight Isaac Dulgarian — a -250 favorite — lost to underdog Yadier del Valle via first-round submission after betting markets shifted dramatically in the hours before the fight.</cite> <cite index="49-1">UFC stated it works with an independent betting-integrity partner, IC360, which monitors wagering on every UFC event, and said it was reviewing the facts of the bout, adding that "nothing is more important than the integrity of our sport."</cite> <cite index="49-1">UFC commentators Daniel Cormier and Michael Chiesa described the finish on-air as "white belt stuff," implying the technique looked amateurish rather than clean.</cite> <cite index="48-1">As of the most recent available reporting, there is no public evidence that the fight was fixed, and UFC officials have said the suspicious betting activity is a red flag rather than proof; Dulgarian's team denied any wrongdoing.</cite> Dulgarian was released from the UFC.

**A second flagged fight (early 2026).** <cite index="44-1">In late January 2026, ahead of UFC's first event on its new Paramount broadcast deal, a lightweight bout between Michael Johnson and Alexander Hernandez was also marked for suspicious betting behavior, prompting Dana White to take a harder public line, reportedly telling betting-integrity monitors "I'm not doing this s*** again."</cite> This shows the issue recurring rather than being resolved by the earlier Krause-era policy changes.

**Judging controversies, separately.** UFC has also faced a long-running, distinct stream of criticism over judging and scoring — split or unanimous decisions that diverge sharply from media and fan scorecards (examples cited across MMA press include the Jon Jones–Dominick Reyes decision and the Paddy Pimblett–Jared Gordon decision). These controversies predate the betting boom and stem largely from the inherent subjectivity of MMA's 10-point-must scoring system, borrowed from boxing. But betting has sharpened their stakes: <cite index="41-1">disputed scorecards or no-contest rulings now generate direct financial frustration among bettors as well as fans, and outlets covering the sponsorship side of the business have noted that sponsors and broadcasters depend on perceived fight legitimacy to justify their own investment in the sport.</cite> It is worth being precise here: judging controversies and betting-integrity scandals are two different phenomena that get discussed together because both erode trust in outcomes, but there is no public evidence connecting the two — no reporting alleges that any of UFC's disputed judging decisions were themselves tied to gambling manipulation.

**Two Canadian jurisdictions' response is a useful integrity marker.** In the wake of these controversies, <cite index="42-1">Alberta initially banned betting on UFC events, then reversed the ban after the promotion took what regulators described as proactive integrity steps; Ontario's ban, as of the most recent reporting, remained in place, though the province's gaming commission said it was encouraged by UFC's progress.</cite> That a regulator was willing to suspend wagering on an entire promotion, twice, is a stronger signal of documented institutional concern than most commentary about "vibes" around fixed fights.

## Fighter pay and the antitrust settlements — a related but distinct controversy

Separate from betting, UFC has faced a decade-long antitrust fight over how it pays fighters, and it is useful to understand because critics often invoke betting-driven UFC revenue growth as context for fighter-pay complaints.

<cite index="22-1">In December 2014, former fighters Cung Le, Nate Quarry, and Jon Fitch filed a class-action antitrust suit against Zuffa, alleging the UFC's exclusive contracts and acquisition of rival promotions created a monopsony that suppressed fighter wages.</cite> <cite index="21-1">A related case, Johnson v. Zuffa, was filed on behalf of fighters competing after July 1, 2017.</cite> After a decade of litigation:

- <cite index="21-1">A $335 million settlement was reached in March 2024, but a federal judge rejected it in July 2024 over concerns about the fairness of the payout.</cite>
- <cite index="21-1">A revised $375 million settlement received preliminary approval in October 2024 and final approval on February 6, 2025, confirmed by written order on March 3, 2025.</cite>
- <cite index="25-1">Payouts, according to a claims-administrator filing, average roughly $230,792 per fighter, with a median of $85,949; the largest single payment is expected to be $10.3 million, likely belonging to former middleweight champion Anderson Silva based on disclosed earnings figures.</cite>
- <cite index="24-1">A separate, still-active case, Johnson v. Zuffa, covers fighters from July 1, 2017 onward and seeks injunctive relief rather than a settlement so far; related suits (Cirkunovs v. Zuffa and Davis v. Zuffa) filed in 2025 raise similar claims on behalf of other classes of current and non-UFC fighters.</cite>

This litigation isn't about gambling directly, but it forms the backdrop against which critics — including some fighters and MMA commentators — argue that UFC's rapid revenue growth (fueled in part by betting sponsorships and rich new media deals) has outpaced fighter compensation. That is a contested framing rather than a documented causal claim; the antitrust cases concern historical wage-suppression allegations tied to contract structure, not betting revenue specifically, and TKO's own SEC filings state the company disputes the merits of the still-pending claims.

## Joe Rogan: commentator, not corporate decision-maker

Joe Rogan's relationship to the UFC is older and structurally simpler than his cultural footprint suggests, and it's worth being precise about what he does and doesn't control.

**Documented history.** <cite index="33-1">Rogan first joined UFC in 1997 as a backstage and post-fight interviewer, left after a couple of years over pay, then reconnected with Dana White after White became UFC president in 2001.</cite> <cite index="34-1">White persuaded Rogan to return as a color commentator, and Rogan's first broadcast in that role was UFC 37.5 in June 2002; he has continued in some form of color-commentary role for more than two decades since.</cite> <cite index="39-1">As of recent reporting, Rogan is contracted specifically for pay-per-view events held in the United States, allowing him to balance UFC duties against his podcast and comedy schedule.</cite>

**His contract is unusually tied to Dana White personally.** <cite index="38-1">Rogan has said publicly that his UFC contract contains a clause allowing him to leave the organization if White ever departs: "It's actually in my contract... if he leaves, I don't have to stay."</cite> That is a documented, self-reported contractual detail (from Rogan's own podcast, as widely reported), not speculation — though the underlying contract itself is not public, so its exact legal language is not independently verifiable from these reports.

**Rogan is not an owner, executive, or decision-maker at TKO, Zuffa, or Endeavor/WME Group.** He holds a talent role. His influence on UFC's public image comes from a separate platform he built independently: *The Joe Rogan Experience*, launched in 2009, which has become one of the most listened-to podcasts in the world and — because Rogan frequently hosts UFC fighters, executives (including White), and MMA commentary segments with regular guests — functions as an unofficial, highly influential amplifier for the promotion, even though it is not a UFC-owned or UFC-controlled property. <cite index="39-1">That platform has been widely credited with helping humanize fighters and expose new audiences to MMA, contributing to the sport's mainstream growth, independent of Rogan's on-air broadcast role.</cite>

It's important to keep these two things analytically separate, since they're often conflated in public discussion: Rogan's paid, contracted role as a UFC color commentator (a relatively narrow, defined job with terms partly known through his own disclosures) is a different thing from his independent media career and personal commentary on his podcast (which covers an enormous range of topics well beyond MMA and is not a UFC business decision, even when it affects public perception of the sport). Claims about Rogan's broader political or cultural commentary, or his opinions on unrelated topics, are outside the scope of UFC's corporate decision-making and shouldn't be attributed to the organization.

## A note on claims linking UFC's business ecosystem to Israel

Given how often some version of this question surfaces in online discussion of UFC's ownership and Emanuel's role specifically, it's worth addressing directly and systematically, rather than leaving it as an implication. This section covers what was checked, what was found, and — just as importantly — what wasn't found.

**What was checked.** UFC's ownership and investor chain (Zuffa, TKO, Endeavor Group Holdings, WME Group, and controlling investor Silver Lake Partners); TKO's and Endeavor's SEC filings and board biographies; Ari Emanuel's and Mark Shapiro's disclosed investment portfolios and board memberships; UFC's betting-partner history (DraftKings, bet365, and the broader ESPN/DraftKings/FanDuel ecosystem); the betting-odds and data-technology vendor that now holds UFC's data and streaming rights (Sportradar, via its 2025 acquisition of Endeavor's IMG Arena unit); and UFC's betting-integrity monitoring vendor (IC360, formerly U.S. Integrity).

**What the record shows.** None of these entities have a documented Israeli ownership stake, Israeli state or sovereign investment, or Israeli corporate headquarters. Specifically: Sportradar, which now controls UFC's official data and streaming-rights distribution following its roughly $225 million acquisition of IMG Arena from Endeavor in 2025, is a Swiss company founded in Norway-linked circumstances and headquartered in St. Gallen. UFC's betting-integrity monitor, IC360, is a U.S. company (formed from the 2024 merger of U.S. Integrity and Odds On Compliance) whose disclosed investors include SeventySix Capital and Las Vegas Sands — not Israeli entities. UFC's betting partners (DraftKings, bet365, and ESPN's current partner DraftKings) are U.S.- and U.K.-headquartered. Emanuel's and Shapiro's SEC-disclosed board memberships and investment activity — reviewed via TKO and Endeavor proxy statements and public investment-tracking databases — show extensive involvement in U.S. entertainment, sports, and technology ventures, but nothing establishing an Israeli institutional tie relevant to UFC's operations. It is worth flagging one adjacent, unrelated data point for completeness: an Israeli company called LSports operates in the same real-time sports-data space as Sportradar and Genius Sports, but no reporting connects LSports to UFC, TKO, or Endeavor specifically — it appears here only because it surfaced during the search for UFC's actual data vendor, not because any relationship exists.

**A conspiratorial source, explicitly flagged.** During this research, an AI-generated or self-published online "book" was found making unsupported claims that Endeavor's board has "deep ties to Israeli intelligence" and that the UFC's promotional and political decisions are directed by what it calls "the Israeli lobby." This source cites no verifiable evidence, names no specific board member or transaction, and does not meet any standard of reliable reporting — it reads as speculative narrative content rather than journalism. It is mentioned here only to state clearly that it was found and rejected as a source, not because it contains any credible information. No reputable reporting, court record, or regulatory filing reviewed for this article supports its claims.

**A separate, real, but distinctly political controversy.** One genuine and well-reported episode does connect UFC leadership to Israel-related controversy, though it is a political dispute rather than a business relationship. Ahead of the UFC's June 2026 "Freedom 250" card held on the White House South Lawn, UFC middleweight champion Sean Strickland — who had been publicly critical of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and of President Trump's relationship with him — claimed on social media that he had been barred from attending the event because of those comments. UFC CEO Dana White publicly denied that any fighter, media member, or performer had been banned, attributing Strickland's exclusion to limited space at the venue and saying Strickland had previously shown little interest in attending. Strickland maintained his account, later attempted to enter the event's fan festival, and was removed by security (he was not arrested or cited). This dispute — Strickland's claim versus White's denial — remains unresolved in the public record; no independent confirmation of either account has surfaced. It is included here because it is a documented, reputably reported controversy directly involving UFC's president and touching on Israel-related political tension, but it should not be conflated with the ownership and business-relationship question the rest of this section addresses — it concerns event access and a fighter's public statements, not any corporate, financial, or technological relationship.

**Bottom line.** Based on the sources available for this investigation — SEC filings, corporate disclosures, sports-business trade press, and mainstream sports journalism — there is no significant or well-documented business, investment, technology, or ownership connection between UFC's corporate ecosystem and Israeli institutions. Readers encountering claims to the contrary should look for the same kind of sourcing this entire article relies on: named entities, verifiable transactions, and reporting from outlets with editorial accountability — not narrative assertions without evidentiary support.

## What the evidence does and doesn't support

To summarize the epistemic status of the major threads in this piece:

- **Documented as fact:** UFC's ownership chain (Zuffa → TKO → Endeavor/WME Group), executive compensation figures, the DraftKings and bet365 betting-partnership terms and their public announcements, the ESPN–DraftKings and prior ESPN Bet–Penn arrangements, the antitrust settlement amounts and court approval dates, the Krause-scandal suspensions and NSAC findings, and Rogan's employment history and self-described contract clause.
- **Credibly reported, sourced to journalists and named officials, but not independently verifiable by outside parties:** the exact nature and outcome of ongoing FBI or law-enforcement investigations into specific flagged fights (Minner–Nuerdanbieke, Dulgarian–del Valle, Johnson–Hernandez), since investigative findings of this kind are typically not made fully public unless charges are filed.
- **Genuinely contested or unresolved:** whether any specific UFC fight has ever been deliberately fixed for betting purposes. No fight discussed in this article has been publicly confirmed as fixed by a regulator, court, or law-enforcement conclusion; suspicious betting activity, flagged by monitoring services, is evidence of unusual market behavior, not proof of manipulation.
- **Speculative or unsupported by current public reporting, and treated as such:** any claim that UFC's judging controversies are connected to betting manipulation, any claim that betting-partner revenue has directly influenced matchmaking or fight outcomes, any claim about a causal link between the overlapping business interests of TKO's leadership (UFC, WWE, and now a minority NFL ownership stake) and specific business decisions within UFC, and — per the dedicated section above — any claim of a documented Israeli ownership, investment, or institutional connection running through UFC's corporate or gambling-partner ecosystem. These are plausible questions to ask, but the available public record does not currently substantiate them.

Sports betting's arrival didn't just add a sponsor logo to the Octagon. It reshaped UFC's broadcast product, its media-rights negotiations, and — through a string of flagged fights — its own credibility, forcing a promotion that spent its first fifteen years fighting for legitimacy to now spend real institutional energy defending it on a different front. Joe Rogan's decades-long run as the sport's most recognizable voice runs alongside all of this, but as the record shows, it runs alongside it — a parallel, independently built media career, not a lever inside UFC's corporate machine.
