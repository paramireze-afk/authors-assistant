# The Next Bin Laden: How Gaza and Iran Are Reseeding the War on Terror

*Based on a conversation between Scott Horton, host of the libertarian-leaning Scott Horton Show, and Joe Kent — a former Army Ranger and Green Beret who went on to serve in the CIA's Special Activities Division and, most recently, as Director of the National Counterterrorism Center in the second Trump administration, a post he resigned from over the U.S. war with Iran.*

## The premise: terrorism as a policy feedback loop

Scott Horton opens with an idea he's been pushing for years: al-Qaeda's war against the United States was never a random eruption of religious hatred. It was, in Osama bin Laden's own framing, a strategy. The theory ran like this — Washington was propping up unpopular dictators across the Muslim world, and those regimes couldn't be toppled as long as American power stood behind them. So bin Laden's answer was to provoke the United States into overreacting, get it bogged down militarily (originally by luring it into Afghanistan, the way the Soviet Union had been), and let imperial overstretch do the rest.

That is history, not speculation — bin Laden said as much himself, and the "lure them into Afghanistan" strategy is well documented. Horton's broader argument, however, is his own analytical framework: that specific, identifiable U.S. and Israeli actions have functioned as recruiting propaganda for jihadist groups, a dynamic intelligence analysts call **blowback** — violence that flows back toward a country as an unintended consequence of its own foreign policy, as opposed to violence that springs from nowhere.

The centerpiece of Horton's case is the claim, sourced to the accounts of the September 11th hijackers' associates, that lead hijacker Mohamed Atta was radicalized specifically by the 1996 Israeli artillery strike on a UN compound in Qana, Lebanon, which killed over 100 sheltering civilians. Kent doesn't dispute this history; the conversation treats it as settled background rather than something in contention.

From there, Horton pivots to the present: he's assembled a stack of recent threat assessments — from Europol, terrorism analyst Ali Soufan's firm (Soufan was the former FBI right-hand man to counterterrorism chief John O'Neill), the *Sunday Times*, and the Institute for Economics and Peace's Global Terrorism Index — all pointing to the same theme. Al-Qaeda's propaganda arm has seized on the war in Gaza as its central recruiting narrative, and a new wave of "lone wolf" and small-cell jihadist violence is rippling across Europe and the United States as a result. Horton wants Kent, using his firsthand government experience, to sort out who is actually behind this — and how worried Americans should be.

## Sorting the threat: Sunni jihadists vs. Shiite militants

Kent's first move is to draw a distinction that he says gets collapsed too often in American political rhetoric: the difference between **Sunni jihadist groups** like al-Qaeda and ISIS, and **Shiite militant groups** aligned with Iran (sometimes called "Twelvers," after the dominant branch of Shia Islam).

The distinction matters, in Kent's professional assessment, because of intent and target selection. Sunni jihadist groups have historically wanted to strike the American homeland directly, or inspire people already living in the West to do so. Iran-aligned Shiite groups, by contrast, have traditionally confined their attacks to U.S. forces and interests in the Middle East itself rather than plotting attacks inside America. Kent notes that alleged Iranian assassination plots on U.S. soil do exist but calls many of them "debatable," pointing to what he describes as their sometimes murky FBI involvement — a skeptical aside rather than a settled claim.

Kent's genuinely new information — the kind only someone who recently ran the National Counterterrorism Center would have — is his prediction that this distinction may now be breaking down. He states plainly that he expects the U.S. strike on a girls' school in Iran (part of the recent U.S.-Iran war) to "flip that dynamic" and trigger a new category of blowback: Shiite-linked terrorism aimed at American soft targets at home, something that hasn't been a major pattern historically. This is Kent's forward-looking judgment, not a report of something that has already happened.

## AQAP: the group Kent says worries him most

Asked to rank the actual threats, Kent identifies **al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula (AQAP)** — al-Qaeda's Yemen-based branch — as the group he considers "ahead of the ball" versus ISIS when it comes to plotting against the American homeland.

Kent explains AQAP's founding logic: unlike some regional insurgencies that mostly fight local wars, AQAP's leadership has clustered in Yemen's remote al-Mahra province specifically to plan and inspire Western-focused attacks, while running just enough local insurgency to protect its own safe havens. He walks through the group's history of trying to hit the U.S. directly:

- The 2009 "underwear bomber," who tried to blow up a Detroit-bound flight on Christmas Day, was linked to **Anwar al-Awlaki**, an American-born AQAP cleric fluent in English who used that fluency to recruit Western Muslims directly, and **Samir Khan**, another American citizen who ran AQAP's English-language *Inspire* magazine.
- The same bombmaker behind the underwear bomb, Ibrahim al-Asiri, also built the 2010 printer-cartridge bombs intercepted en route to the U.S. and reportedly nearly killed Saudi Arabia's intelligence chief. AQAP and Saudi forces eventually killed him.
- After concluding that complex, remotely-directed plots kept getting intercepted, Kent says AQAP under Awlaki pivoted strategy — deliberately simplifying its message to Western sympathizers to something like "you don't need a bomb, just use a vehicle," anticipating the low-tech vehicle-ramming and knife attacks that ISIS would later popularize.

Kent connects this history directly to the present: he says the 2025 wave of U.S. attacks Horton catalogued — including the May 2025 Washington, D.C. shooting, cited by AQAP propaganda as a model attack, and separate incidents in Colorado and Manchester — were "basically all" tied by the perpetrators, in some form, to the war in Gaza. He specifically notes that the Washington attackers appeared to have no prior ties to Islam or organized jihadist networks; their radicalization, as best as investigators could tell, ran through social media content about Gaza rather than any formal recruitment pipeline. That is a striking claim to come from a former senior counterterrorism official, and it functions as Kent's central piece of evidence that Gaza — much like the 1996 Qana strike a generation earlier — is now operating as a mass radicalization engine independent of any single organization's control.

### Why AQAP might currently have room to operate

Kent adds a more speculative, forward-looking concern: with Saudi Arabia (and to a lesser extent the UAE) currently running an offensive against Yemen's Houthi movement, AQAP faces a choice — go quiet, or offer its fighters as informal allies to the anti-Houthi coalition through tribal back-channels. Kent says he doesn't know whether this is actually happening, but flags it as a realistic risk given how Yemeni tribal politics tends to work, and given that U.S. intelligence and military resources have been stretched thin by the parallel Iran war — leaving what he calls "a huge gap" in coverage of AQAP.

## The stranger paradox: when U.S. policy quietly protects al-Qaeda

This is where the conversation surfaces its most counterintuitive theme, one Horton returns to twice: the U.S. has, at different points, found itself effectively *shielding* al-Qaeda's Yemen branch rather than fighting it — a byproduct of choosing a bigger enemy.

Horton lays out a specific historical sequence, which he attributes to reporting in the *Wall Street Journal* and by journalist Barbara Slavin in *Al-Monitor*: in early 2015, U.S. Central Command was reportedly sharing intelligence with Yemen's Houthi movement to help it target al-Qaeda. But by March 2015, the Obama administration reversed course and backed a Saudi-and-UAE-led war *against* the Houthis instead — a war that, incidentally, put the U.S. on the same side as AQAP, since the Houthis are AQAP's enemy too. Horton frames this as a case where sectarian politics (Saudi and Israeli hostility toward Shiite groups) overrode the ostensible logic of the "war on terror" itself.

Both men agree this creates a paradox worth sitting with: **a group formally designated as America's enemy can rationally calculate that, for now, attacking America would be counterproductive**, because America is inadvertently doing useful work against that group's own regional rivals. Kent's answer, drawing on Ali Soufan's book *The Black Banners*, is that this has always been bin Laden's model — he cites an anecdote in which Chechen fighters allegedly asked bin Laden why he wanted to attack the U.S. given that Washington had supported Muslim causes in Afghanistan, Bosnia, and Chechnya; bin Laden's answer, in Kent's telling, was that he had a "larger vision" beyond any single regional fight. The strategic patience Kent attributes to today's AQAP leadership is presented as a continuation of that same doctrine: don't strike when it's tactically disadvantageous, keep the network intact, and wait.

## Syria: the ex-jihadist now running the government

The conversation's second major thread concerns **Ahmed al-Sharaa** (better known by his former nom de guerre, al-Jolani), the man who now leads Syria's post-Assad government. His biography, as both men lay it out, is genuinely remarkable and mostly a matter of public record: he fought American forces in Iraq as a member of al-Qaeda in Iraq, was later selected by al-Qaeda leader Ayman al-Zawahiri to run the group's Syrian branch (which became the al-Nusra Front), split from ISIS's Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi over strategy, eventually rebranded his faction again, and ultimately took power in Damascus with what amounts to U.S. and Turkish acquiescence.

Kent identifies the strategic disagreement that drove Sharaa away from Baghdadi and ISIS as a real and consequential split: Baghdadi wanted to fight the "near enemy" first — local groups regarded as apostates, including Shiites and Yazidis. Sharaa reportedly favored a longer-game "Muslim Brotherhood model" of actually seizing state power and governing, on the theory that controlling a government is a more effective platform from which to eventually confront the "far enemy" (the U.S. and, by extension, weaken support for Israel). This is presented as established history about the group's internal politics, not speculation.

What's speculative is Kent and Horton's shared prediction about where this leads. Kent's reasoning: Sharaa was installed with the backing of hardline Sunni factions inside his own coalition, so he cannot fully crack down on the al-Qaeda and ISIS veterans in his own ranks without risking assassination or being overthrown — "he can either betray us or be betrayed," as Horton puts it. Kent adds a detail from his own time running U.S. counterterrorism policy: he says he personally intervened to block a plan that would have handed roughly 5,000 of the most dangerous captured ISIS fighters over to Sharaa's government, arranging instead for Iraq to take custody and prosecute them in Baghdad. But two other detention sites holding ISIS-linked families — al-Hol and Roj camps, housing an estimated 20,000 people, many of them children who have known nothing but life under the caliphate — were, in Kent's account, effectively abandoned by the new Syrian government, which lacked the capacity to keep guarding them. Kent says those families have since dispersed into enclaves controlled by other foreign-fighter factions now folded into Syria's military — which he treats as a serious, ongoing intelligence blind spot, though not yet a full-blown crisis.

Journalist Lindsey Snell — whom Horton describes interviewing separately, and who was previously kidnapped by al-Qaeda while reporting from Syria during the rise of ISIS — is cited as agreeing with Horton's blunter prediction: that if Sharaa leans too far toward accommodating the West and Israel, one of his own fighters will eventually kill him and replace him with someone more hardline.

## ISIS-K: an origin story rooted in an earlier American war

The conversation's most historically dense digression concerns **ISIS-K** (ISIS-Khorasan, the Afghanistan/Pakistan-based ISIS affiliate), and it doubles as Horton's clearest illustration of blowback as a recurring pattern rather than a one-time event.

Horton's account — which he attributes to journalist Borhan Osman's reporting for the outlet Afghanistan Analysts Network, and to a claim from former CIA officer John Kiryakou — traces ISIS-K's roots to the Obama-era drone campaign against al-Qaeda inside Pakistan. Kiryakou's claim, per Horton, was that by 2009 there were only around 29 Arab al-Qaeda members left in Pakistan — meaning the drone war's actual targets had dwindled sharply, even as the campaign continued. As part of securing Pakistani government cooperation for that drone war, Horton says, the U.S. also backed the Pakistani government's own offensive against the Pakistani Taliban (the Tehrik-i-Taliban, distinct from the Afghan Taliban) in Pakistan's Swat Valley and tribal territories — a campaign Horton says killed roughly 80,000 people.

The survivors of that Pakistani Taliban crackdown, in this account, fled across the border into Afghanistan. There, Horton and Kent agree, Afghan intelligence — which both describe as essentially CIA-directed at the time — cultivated these refugee fighters and used them for retaliatory strikes against Pakistan, and hoped to also turn them against the Afghan Taliban. In 2013, this same refugee network pledged loyalty to Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi and the Islamic State, becoming ISIS-K. Kent, notably, doesn't dispute this lineage; he calls it emblematic of a broader pattern rather than a deliberate American scheme — "it hasn't been necessarily a scheme that we concocted," but rather the cumulative result of choices (the Iraq War, de-Baathification, the campaign against Assad) that, in his words, "went to create ISIS" as something that "became just a monster that we made ourselves."

On current ISIS-K activity, Kent notes what he frames as an unusual and unintentional bright spot: Afghanistan's Taliban government, despite being an adversary of the U.S. in its own right, views ISIS-K as a direct rival for power and has actively suppressed it — which Kent says has kept ISIS-K's ambitions comparatively contained, without this amounting to any kind of U.S.-Taliban cooperation.

## A speculative detour: jihadists, Chechnya, and Ukraine

Horton raises Russia's 2024 Crocus City Hall theater attack in Moscow, officially attributed to ISIS-K, and floats a theory: could Ukrainian intelligence have had some hand in enabling it, as a way of forcing Russia to divert resources? Kent is careful here — he stresses he wasn't in government at the time and doesn't know — but says the underlying logic wouldn't surprise him, comparing it to a basic principle of unconventional warfare: an enemy will look for cutouts capable of sabotage behind your lines. Horton adds a separate, better-documented piece of context — reported by journalist Vasco Cotovio for *The Intercept* and, separately, by the *New York Times* — that jihadist fighters did join Ukraine's Azov Battalion and fight on Kyiv's side following ISIS's territorial defeat in Syria. Both speakers are explicit that this is documented; whether any of those fighters were specifically deployed by Ukrainian intelligence for sabotage inside Russia is, they agree, unconfirmed speculation.

## Vetting the "Bin Ladenite" label — and a dissent from Tulsi Gabbard

Horton uses this section to push back on what he considers a category error common in Washington rhetoric — including, he says, in a past campaign ad from Tulsi Gabbard describing "hundreds of Bin Laden groups" worldwide that need to be defeated. Horton's distinction: there's a real difference between a local Sunni insurgency with no interest in attacking the U.S. (he cites al-Shabaab's fighters in Chad and Sierra Leone as an example) and groups genuinely committed to hitting the American homeland. Kent doesn't fully validate or dispute the Gabbard reference, but the exchange reinforces the episode's throughline: not every group waving a jihadist flag is an equivalent threat, and lumping them together, in Horton and Kent's shared view, obscures where the real danger sits.

On the fate of bin Laden's own inner circle, Kent's answers are appropriately hedged: Ayman al-Zawahiri, bin Laden's successor, was killed (Kent had confirmed this in an earlier appearance on the show). Whether bin Laden's son Hamza is dead is, per Kent, genuinely disputed within the intelligence community, with some assessments concluding he's dead and some speculation that Iran may be holding him — an open question, not a settled fact.

## Al-Qaeda in the Sahel: a rising but currently regional threat

Kent flags a further concern in the Maghreb and West Africa — a "very active" al-Qaeda affiliate expanding territorial control in Mali and neighboring areas. His assessment is measured: this group hasn't shown ambitions for attacks against the U.S. or Europe (in contrast to AQAP), but its expansion risks producing a Libya-style collapse that pushes large numbers of migrants into Europe, which he frames as a serious but different kind of crisis than direct terrorism.

## Iraq's militias: an uncomfortable dependency

Kent closes with a case that mirrors the AQAP paradox from a different angle. Discussing U.S. pressure on Iraq to disarm the **Popular Mobilization Forces (PMF)** — the mostly Shiite militia network, some elements of which are genuine Iranian proxies — Kent draws a distinction between factions like Kataib Hezbollah, which he says has attacked U.S. bases and should be disarmed, and the broader PMF, which he describes as an organic, homegrown force. His firsthand institutional memory: the PMF was originally stood up because the post-2003, U.S.-trained Iraqi army collapsed and fled in its first real test against ISIS, and a fatwa from Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani was needed to mobilize enough of an infantry force to fight back. Kent's concern is that indiscriminately disarming the PMF — done for reasons connected to the broader U.S.-Iran confrontation rather than any actual PMF threat to America — could leave Iraq without a sufficient force to prevent an ISIS resurgence, especially if Sharaa's government in Syria collapses and the "caliphate scenario" (a Sunni-jihadist reconstitution spilling from Syria into Sunni-majority western Iraq) reemerges, a scenario Kent says is genuinely plausible given lingering Sunni grievances in Iraq's contested provinces.

## The throughline: why Horton and Kent think another wave is coming

By the end, the episode's argument comes into focus as a layered claim rather than a single prediction:

1. **The mechanism is not new.** Blowback, in this framework, isn't a one-off explanation for 9/11 — it's a repeating cycle. The Qana massacre radicalized Atta; the Pakistani Taliban crackdown produced ISIS-K; the Iraq War and de-Baathification produced ISIS itself. Gaza, in Kent's assessment as a former senior counterterrorism official, is now functioning the same way for a new cohort with no formal ties to any organized group.
2. **Not all threats are equal, and Washington sometimes conflates them.** Kent's professional framework treats AQAP as the most acute homeland threat specifically because of its stated intent and English-language recruitment infrastructure — a different category from regionally-focused Sunni insurgencies or from Iran-aligned Shiite groups that have historically avoided attacking the U.S. homeland directly.
3. **U.S. policy can accidentally shield the very groups it names as enemies.** Whether it's tacit cooperation with AQAP against the Houthis, tolerance of ex-jihadist Sharaa in Damascus, or reliance on Iran-linked Iraqi militias to hold the line against ISIS, both speakers argue that sectarian and geopolitical priorities routinely override consistent counterterrorism logic — and that the resulting arrangements are fragile, dependent on personalities and alliances that could flip quickly.
4. **The Iran war may open a genuinely new front.** Kent's most forward-looking and least-verifiable claim is that the killing of Iranian civilians and officials — and especially the strike that killed nearly 200 schoolgirls — could, for the first time, generate the kind of anti-American blowback terrorism historically associated with Sunni jihadists, but now motivated by Shiite grievance instead. Neither speaker claims this has already happened at scale; it's presented explicitly as a forecast, buttressed by historical analogy (Norman Podhoretz's 2007 acknowledgment that bombing Iran would likely unleash "a wave of anti-American terrorism," and Michael Scheuer's — the CIA's former bin Laden unit chief — warnings about a "war against all of Islam" narrative).

Horton's closing aside is worth noting as a statement of method: he distances himself from conspiracy-driven explanations that attribute all terrorism to secret CIA machination, arguing that such theories are actually dangerous because they let real, policy-driven blowback go unexamined. "If the CIA is behind every last thing in the world, then you don't have to worry about anything that the CIA doesn't do to you," he says — the point being that when U.S. and allied actions plausibly and demonstrably generate retaliatory violence, that causal chain deserves scrutiny on its own terms, not dismissal as either paranoia or apologetics for the perpetrators.
