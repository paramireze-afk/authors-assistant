# Financing Multiple Sides of the Same Conflict

## The Straddle

In options trading, a **straddle** is a position built by buying a call *and* a put on the same asset at the same strike price. You are not betting the price will rise, and you are not betting it will fall — you are betting it will *move*, in either direction, and you will profit either way. The only outcome you actually lose money on is stillness.

**[Note — this is this document's own framing, not a term used by either source author]:** it's worth naming explicitly because it clarifies something the case studies below share that ordinary "war profiteering" doesn't. A profiteer picks a side and sells it weapons at a markup — he wants his side to win, or at least survive. The pattern documented in this page is structurally different: the financier is indifferent to *which* faction wins, because he is positioned on both, and the thing he is actually selling is the war itself, not victory for either combatant. The one outcome that hurts him is the one thing that ends the position early: a quick, decisive peace.

This page collects every instance across this knowledge base's two source books where a financier or financial network is documented — with varying degrees of evidentiary strength, noted case by case — funding two or more opposing factions in the same conflict, simultaneously or in close sequence.

---

## Why the Straddle Works: The Underlying Mechanism

Four structural conditions make this strategy viable, drawing together material from across both source books:

1. **Fiat or credit-based money removes the ceiling on what can be lent.** Griffin's Mandrake Mechanism and Elliott's "wealth is barren" critique both converge on the same enabling fact: if money can be created rather than earned, a lender is never actually choosing between funding side A *or* side B with a fixed pool of capital. He can fund both, because neither loan actually costs him anything to originate.
2. **Debt outlives the outcome of the war.** Whichever faction wins, it inherits a state, a treasury, and — per both books — a strong incentive to honor existing obligations to preserve its own future credit. The lender's claim survives regime change even when the regime doesn't.
3. **Intermediaries and cover stories launder the contradiction.** None of the case studies below involve a bank publicly announcing "we are funding both armies." Every one relies on a disguise — a humanitarian mission, a shell agent, a socially respectable non-Jewish frontman, a "Red Cross" label — that keeps the two funding streams from being visibly connected to the same source at the same time.
4. **A decisive, permanent peace is the only losing outcome.** This is the piece that turns "funding both sides" from a hedge into a *strategy*. A quick, clean victory ends the debt-generating conflict. The financier's actual interest, once positioned on both sides, shifts subtly from "which side wins" to "how long the war — and the debt it generates — can be sustained."

## Case Files

### 1. The Bolshevik Revolution — The Clearest Documented Case

The single most explicit example in either book. Per Griffin's account (see *[[The Bolshevik Revolution]]* for the full narrative), "Colonel" William Boyce Thompson, heading the American Red Cross Mission in revolutionary Russia, gave over two million roubles to **Alexander Kerensky's moderate provisional government** for propaganda purposes — and, together with **J.P. Morgan**, simultaneously transferred the rouble equivalent of one million dollars to fund **Bolshevik** propaganda in Germany and Austria. Griffin quotes historian Anthony Sutton's blunt assessment of the motive: "Thompson was willing to place seed money on any political power vehicle that would achieve his objective... Ideology could sway revolutionary operators like Kerensky, Trotsky, Lenin et al., but not financiers."

Griffin extends the straddle one step further back: Tsar **Nicholas II** himself had accepted loans from J.P. Morgan — the same financial house that, per Griffin's sourcing, "also funded the Mensheviks and the Bolsheviks" who would force his abdication and later execute him. The straddle here spans the entire cast: the monarchy being overthrown, the moderate government that replaced it, and the radical faction that overthrew *that* government in turn — three positions on the same board, financed in overlapping windows by an overlapping set of hands.

**The cover:** a humanitarian "Red Cross Mission," whose own personnel later admitted, per Griffin, that the Red Cross framing was "nothing but a mask."

### 2. Belgium, 1830 — The Sequenced Version

Not a simultaneous straddle but a close cousin: a *deferred* one. When Belgium's 1830 uprising against Dutch rule broke out, **Salomon Rothschild** explicitly declined to fund the rebel side, writing that his family's "goodwill does not yet extend to the point of putting clubs into the hands that would beat us... lending money to make war and ruin the credit that we sustain with all our efforts." Rather than betting on the outcome, the family waited. Once the revolution resolved through negotiation rather than a clean military victory, **James Rothschild** moved immediately to capture the resulting government's finances, writing that the goal was "to make ourselves absolute masters of that country's finances." Griffin calls this pattern — refusing to fund the volatile early phase, then rushing to capture whichever government survives it — "the strategy that has become the model of such operations ever since." **[Note:]** this is the straddle with the risk hedged out: rather than buying both the call and the put up front, wait for the coin to land, then buy whichever side is now winning at a discount.

### 3. The American Civil War — A Documented Dual Position

Detailed in *[[The Rothschild Family]]*: **August Belmont**, the Rothschild consortium's acknowledged American agent, is described by Griffin as buying up heavily depreciated **Confederate** state bonds at pennies on the dollar as the war turned toward the North — betting that a reunified United States would eventually be pressured to honor pre-war Southern debt obligations in full. In the same period, Belmont simultaneously served as the chief agent selling **Union** government bonds in England and France. One financial network, two governments at war with each other, both financed through the same hand.

### 4. Nicholas Biddle and Congress — The Domestic Straddle

A non-military variant, documented in *[[The First and Second Banks of the United States]]*: rather than opposing armies, Nicholas Biddle straddled *political factions*. Griffin describes Biddle "following the Rothschild Formula" by rewarding compliant Congressmen across party lines with business favors and financial retainers — Senator Daniel Webster's Bank of the United States legal retainer being the clearest documented example. The "conflict" here is legislative rather than military, but the underlying logic is identical: fund enough figures on every side of the fight that whoever wins the vote, the lender's interests are already represented in the winning coalition.

### 5. The Cold War — Funding the Enemy Rather Than the Opposition

A structurally different but related pattern, covered in *[[The Rothschild Formula]]*: rather than funding two combatants in the *same* war, Griffin (via historian Antony Sutton, from whom Chapter 14's title is drawn) documents American-subsidized Soviet industrial capacity supplying the weapons, trucks, and ammunition that killed American soldiers in Korea and Vietnam. This isn't the classic straddle — it's closer to *manufacturing the second position from scratch*, so that a conflict exists to be financed at all. Griffin extends this to the Gulf War, quoting CFR *Foreign Affairs* editor Fareed Zakaria's 1996 remark that "if Saddam Hussein did not exist, we would have to invent him," and Congressman James Traficant's floor speech describing American capital funding Russian missile production that was resold to China, which armed Iran, which then required more American troops to counter. **[Note:]** this variant deserves flagging as distinct — it's less "hedge your bet on who wins" and more "keep the game itself in permanent play" — but it belongs on this page because it's the same underlying incentive (a resolved conflict is a lost revenue stream) taken to its logical extension.
