# Joseph F. Sackett, MD (1940–2026): The Neuroradiologist Who Helped Build UW Radiology

## From Cleveland to New Orleans to Vietnam

Joseph Frederick Sackett was born on January 16, 1940, in Cleveland, Ohio. He earned a B.A. in biology from Dartmouth College in 1962 and an M.D. from Tulane University School of Medicine in 1966. After medical school he served in the Vietnam War, then began an academic career at the University of Wisconsin School of Medicine.

He arrived in Madison in 1974 and rose quickly, serving as Assistant Professor of Radiology from 1974 to 1978 and Associate Professor from 1978 to 1981, before becoming chair.

## The Problem He Helped Solve: Imaging the Spine

To understand his best-known paper, you need to know what spinal imaging looked like in the early 1970s. Before CT was widespread and long before MRI, doctors looked at the spinal cord and nerve roots with myelography, injecting a contrast agent into the fluid around the spinal cord and taking X-rays. The standard agents were oil-based. They didn't mix with spinal fluid, often had to be pulled back out with a needle, and could linger in the body for years, sometimes linked to chronic inflammation of the membranes around the spinal cord.

Metrizamide was a new kind of agent: water-soluble, non-ionic, and absorbed by the body on its own. Sackett was the first radiologist in North America to publish a pivotal study demonstrating its safety and efficacy for spine imaging, in 1977, a significant improvement over the oil-based agents. The paper, "Metrizamide: CSF Contrast Medium," appeared in *Radiology* and evaluated 215 patients.

He kept working on the problem. In 1983, he co-authored with his protégé Patrick Turski a double-blind comparison of iopamidol and metrizamide for myelography in 36 patients. Patients receiving metrizamide had sixteen adverse reactions among ten of them, versus six reactions in five patients receiving iopamidol, with no appreciable difference in image quality. In other words, he helped introduce one generation of contrast agent and then helped test its successor.

## Volunteering as a Test Subject

One of the more memorable stories from his career involves digital subtraction angiography (DSA). The idea behind DSA is simple: take an X-ray image before contrast is injected, take another afterward, and digitally subtract the first from the second, leaving a clean picture of just the blood vessels.

In the 1970s, Sackett assisted colleague Charles Mistretta, PhD, in developing DSA, even volunteering himself as a test subject. The procedure went smoothly, except that the contrast solution gave him hives. He shrugged it off years later:

> "It was not a major reaction."

DSA became a standard technique, and Sackett was part of its public debut. On May 20, 1981, he joined Thomas F. Meaney of Cleveland Clinic and M. Paul Capp of the University of Arizona on an American College of Radiology panel on breakthrough techniques in digital vascular imaging, which got wide media coverage. Later accounts credit him with a crucial role in the development of DSA.

## Chairman, 1981–1995

Sackett had been groomed for leadership. He served as vice chair under Francis Ruzicka, MD, who led the department from 1976 to 1981, and that partnership prepared him to take over. As he put it: "There weren't any major secrets."

His timing was notable. MRI was brand new when he took over. In 1984, the department began a collaborative research program with General Electric Medical Systems and installed the first MRI machine on campus; before that, researchers had to travel to GE's facility in Waukesha to use equipment. That relationship outlived his tenure by decades. According to the department, it has generated over 100 patents, and UW Radiology now has access to more than 20 MRI scanners for research.

He also reshaped the department itself. In 1981, it had five subdivisions: radiotherapy, medical physics, nuclear medicine, radiobiology and diagnosis. During his tenure, radiotherapy moved to Human Oncology, medical physics became its own department, and radiobiology shifted to the cancer center. Meanwhile imaging itself was splitting into subspecialties, and by 1995 the department had grown to ten major sections, most of which still exist in some form today. Departmental funding grew fourfold and annual scholarly output doubled on his watch.

Nationally, he was elected vice president of the American Society of Neuroradiology in 1987. Over his career he published 74 papers in refereed journals, served as a visiting professor for 32 semesters and wrote 11 textbook chapters.

## Parental Leave Before It Was Standard

This may be the most interesting part of his story, because it was about people rather than machines.

In the early 1980s, very few radiologists were women. Shortly after he became chair, a high-performing female resident asked for time off to manage morning sickness and stress during her pregnancy. The program had no pregnancy policy, only the Board of Radiology's six-week annual leave limit. Sackett and the faculty created the department's first pregnancy policy, granting up to four weeks of paid maternity leave.

Then the men asked too. Sackett recommended one week of paid paternity leave, as long as the service chief resident cleared it.

He wrote about all this in a 1987 *Investigative Radiology* piece with the wonderful title "Pregnant Pause." In it, he admitted that formal leave policies didn't solve the deeper problem of how a trainee could be a parent and still be academically productive, and he openly acknowledged that his stance went against some national radiology organizations. He also described a part-time training arrangement for a resident balancing child care. It lengthened her training, but she didn't have to choose between her career and motherhood.

## Mentorship and a Full-Circle Succession

One thread running through his career is Patrick Turski. Sackett introduced Turski to radiology and convinced him to make it his career. Turski joined the faculty in 1981, became chief of MRI in 1986 and chief of Neuroradiology in 1993, and eventually succeeded Sackett as department chair in 1995. The student he recruited became the person who replaced him.

## Later Years and Honors

He became Emeritus Professor on July 1, 1997, and the university established an endowed chair in his name in 1999. The Joseph F. Sackett Professorship in Radiology supports the chief of the Section of Neuroradiology. Holders include inaugural honoree Howard Rowley, MD, and current holder Tabassum (Tabby) Kennedy, MD.

In 2016, his alma mater honored him. He received the Tulane Medical Alumni Association's Lifetime Achievement Award on May 13, during his medical school class's 50-year reunion. His remarks were about loyalty to the school:

> "There is a special loyalty all Tulane Medical School classes have for their school."

His last public reflection came in a summer 2025 interview, published as the department approached its 2027 centennial. Asked what future generations could learn from earlier ones, he kept it short:

> "Just carry on and do as well as they have."

He was survived by his wife, Sheilah. A celebration of life is planned for May 30, 2027, which happens to fall in the department's centennial year.
