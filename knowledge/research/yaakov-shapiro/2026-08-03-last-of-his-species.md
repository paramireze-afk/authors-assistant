# "The Last of His Species": Rabbi Yaakov Shapiro on the Satmar Rebbe

## Introduction

Rabbi Yaakov Shapiro opens with thanks to his hosts — the rabbi who invited him, and the *mara d'asra* (the presiding rabbinic authority of the beis medrash, or study hall, where he's speaking). He calls the moment itself a *kiddush Hashem* — a sanctification of God's name — because, he says, that's what it is whenever people make a real effort to understand what a *gadol* (a Torah giant) truly is, "especially a *gadol ha-dor* like this one." Plenty of people, he says flatly, don't understand and don't want to understand. Breaking through that wall is itself the achievement.

He then poses the problem the whole talk is built around. It would be easy, he says, to eulogize the Rebbe with a list: he knew *Shas* (the Talmud) better than the next *gadol*, he built an enormous network of institutions, he saved Jews during the Holocaust. But he stops himself — that's not it. "He knew *Shas* better than the next *gadol*? There are plenty of people who knew *Shas*. That's a quantitative difference." His whole point, repeated almost like a refrain through the talk, is that describing the Rebbe by degree — more learned, more pious, more accomplished — misses him entirely. What he wants his audience to grasp is a *qualitative* difference: not a rabbi who was more of what other rabbis were, but someone from a different world altogether, glimpsed rather than fully knowable. He admits up front that in the time he has, he can only "scratch the surface."

## The Last of a Type

Rabbi Shapiro's central image comes from a teaching about why the patriarch Yaakov was brought back to this world after his death — so that later generations could see, with their own eyes, what an earlier and greater generation actually looked like. God doesn't do anything for nothing, he says; the point of bringing someone back was specifically to let people witness the real thing, because no amount of teaching or writing could have painted that picture. This, he argues, is why God sent the Satmar Rebbe into this particular generation: not to tell us about the earlier generations, but to *show* us.

He sharpens this with the story of Noach and the lion — Noach was late feeding the animals in the Ark, and the lion nearly killed him for it, a punishment that seems absurdly disproportionate for tardiness with a food bowl. The resolution given is that this wasn't an ordinary lion. It was the last lion left on earth, and an animal like that demands a different order of care entirely. "The Satmar Rebbe is not just an *erliche Yid*," Rabbi Shapiro says — not just a sincerely observant Jew, not just a *gadol* among other *gedolim*. "He's the last of his species on this planet, and there's nobody else like him." The reverence owed to a figure like that, he insists, is categorically different from the reverence owed to other great rabbis — not more of the same respect, but a different kind of respect.

To back this up, he brings testimony — and he's careful, throughout the talk, to flag what kind of testimony it is. Some of what follows, he says plainly, are stories: if you don't believe them, "you're not *apikorsus* [heretical] for doubting them, but you're missing something." Other material is not story but formal, recorded statement — eulogies and written testimony by named Torah authorities — and that, he says, carries different weight, because sometimes those statements are things "we know are true anyway."

He cites, from memory, a rabbi — associated with rescue efforts to help ransom the Rebbe out of Europe during the Holocaust — reportedly telling the Rebbe directly that in his whole life he had never met anyone with such pure faith. He also recounts, and this is a point he returns to more than once, an encounter with the Brisker Rav (Rabbi Yitzchok Zev Soloveitchik) — a scion of one of the most exacting and demanding rabbinic lineages in the Torah world, a man old enough as a small child to have witnessed the passing of the *Beis HaLevi* and, shortly before his own birth, of Rabbi Yisrael Salanter. According to Rabbi Shapiro, what began as a courtesy call — the Rebbe, still young and not yet well known, sending a donation ahead through an intermediary — turned into hours of private conversation once the two actually met. Afterward, in words Rabbi Shapiro says are recorded in the Brisker Rav's own published writings, the Brisker Rav said he had never in his life met a person as intellectually honest — as genuinely committed to pursuing truth for its own sake — as this man.

This is worth pausing on, and Rabbi Shapiro pauses on it too: the Brisker Rav, by that point in his life, had personally known nearly every major *gadol* of the previous century. For a man like that to say he'd never met anyone like the Satmar Rebbe is not a small compliment.

It also raises a question Rabbi Shapiro doesn't spell out immediately but that runs under the whole talk: why does he keep reaching for testimony from Lithuanian-style (*Litvish*) rabbis and other authorities well outside the Hungarian *chassidish* world the Rebbe came from, rather than resting on praise from within Satmar itself? The answer is implicit in how he uses these quotes: praise from your own community proves loyalty. Praise from a Brisker Rav, a Rav Moshe Feinstein, a Rav Shach — men from entirely different communities, different customs, sometimes different ideological camps — proves something else. It shows that recognition of this man crossed every line that normally divides the Torah world. That, for Rabbi Shapiro, is closer to real evidence.

He closes the section with one more written source — cautious, he notes, about comparing anyone in our era to a Biblical figure, and he keeps that caution — that says of the Rebbe: from ninety-two years of unbroken labor, "we don't know of a fighter of Hashem's wars like him," and that he was holy "from the womb." Rabbi Shapiro challenges the room to find that exact language applied to anyone else by an authority of that stature. He's not aware of one.

## Holiness, Labor, and Self-Formation

Here Rabbi Shapiro makes his theological turn. He brings a teaching about why, at the giving of the Torah, the Jewish people briefly considered receiving it through the angel Metatron rather than through Moshe Rabbeinu. Metatron, in this account, simply inherited his spiritual level as a gift. Moshe "received the baton" and never let go — his greatness was built, not given. The people ultimately chose Moshe precisely because what he *earned* was worth more than what Metatron was simply handed.

This is the lens, Rabbi Shapiro says, for reading everything about the Rebbe's childhood. Whatever he was born with amounted to almost nothing next to what he built through relentless, self-imposed labor from the earliest age — echoing a teaching that God "plants" great souls in every generation, but a planted seed still has to be watered, or nothing happens.

The childhood stories are the evidence:

- At five, his father hired him a tutor who taught him all day and much of the night, including Friday nights.
- At three or four, he was found crying on the floor doing *teshuvah* — because the night before, tired at his father's table, he'd fallen asleep and missed saying the bedtime Shema.
- On another occasion he was found crying because he'd seen someone violate Shabbos; his father had taught him that witnessing another person's failing reflects something lacking in the observer too.
- At seven, having learned that Torah study protects a person from the *yetzer hara* even between actual mitzvos, while performing a mitzvah protects only in the moment, he cited Rashi as his source when his father — testing him — asked where he'd found the distinction.
- His father set a rule limiting how late he could stay up learning. He was later found awake at 1 or 2 a.m. and, without guile, pointed out that he *had* gone to sleep, as instructed — and then simply woken up again.
- As a small child insisting on fasting despite the technical exemption for children, he argued the exemption is printed in small letters in the *siddur* (prayer book) — meaning it applies to those who can't manage it, not to those, like him, who could.
- Sickly as a boy, he was quietly giving away his own food to poorer classmates every day — discovered only when two of those classmates were caught fighting, each insisting the Rebbe had given the food to *him*.
- Around age seven, he organized the wealthier children of his community — what Rabbi Shapiro calls, half-joking, "the first appeal of his life" — lecturing them that money is worthless in the World to Come and only charity endures, and getting them to fund clothes and shoes for poor children.

By his teens, Rabbi Shapiro says, it was already known that "you couldn't catch him not knowing something" — vast stretches of Talmud and Midrash held cold, by heart.

## A Torah Mind

Much of the talk dwells on the sheer scope of the Rebbe's command of Torah material, and again Rabbi Shapiro keeps flagging where each piece comes from.

He mentions a report — secondhand, as he presents it — that a leading rabbi once said he knew of no one alive who matched the Rebbe's command of *Chazal* (statements of the Sages). He then offers something closer to his own direct experience: he says he found several places (he counts four) in the Rebbe's writings citing a *Chazal* he could not locate anywhere, despite extensive searching — "I decided to do something better than a computer" — and wrote to a scholar renowned for an exceptional memory of the entire Rabbinic corpus, asking for the source. He still has the reply. It was one word, essentially: "I don't remember" — which he calls probably the only time in that scholar's life he ever said those words. Rabbi Shapiro reads this as evidence, not embarrassment: if even that memory couldn't place the citation, the Rebbe was drawing on material simply out of reach of everyone else.

More testimony, of varying kinds, follows the same shape — difficult, long-unresolved questions met with instant, unprepared answers:

- A story, heard rather than witnessed, of a questioner arriving with a problem that had stumped scholars for months, receiving an answer on the spot with eyes closed, and walking away singing.
- **Rav Moshe Feinstein**, in a letter Rabbi Shapiro says he has seen written for a memorial volume, describing how in the 1950s he would send an emissary — Rabbi Greenblatt — with hard questions from his own lectures, and would always accept what came back, delivered instantly, "as if he was learning the topic right now." Rabbi Shapiro adds that he personally phoned Rabbi Greenblatt afterward to ask two things: did Rav Moshe ever send questions to anyone else this way (no), and did he always accept the Rebbe's answers (yes, always).
- A rabbi who mocked the idea of the Rebbe receiving ordination, pointing to how long — he claimed two and a half hours — the Rebbe took just to recite the bedtime Shema, implying there was no time left to actually learn. The reported answer: once tested on the whole of *Shas*, the Rebbe knew it backward and forward, "and I see he's a *yerei Shamayim* [God-fearing man] as well."
- **Rav Shach**, quoted as saying the Rebbe learned, in raw hours, *less* than many ordinary scholars, given everything else on his plate — yet achieved more, because an innate capacity, sharpened since childhood, let him absorb in minutes what took others months.

Rabbi Shapiro folds in here the point the Brisker Rav made earlier about intellectual honesty — a thread he'll return to when he gets to the Rebbe's political judgment: a mind this capable was also, by every account he brings, relentlessly honest, willing to tell people the truth even when it cost him.

## Prayer and Spiritual Discipline

The talk turns to the Rebbe's *avodah* — his service of God in prayer — and the physical intensity behind it. As a child he would shake and flush red with emotion while praying, weeping with such visible feeling that people said simply watching his face during prayer was itself spiritually moving. Rabbi Shapiro says this never let up with age.

He describes — and here the source seems to be the Rebbetzin herself, relayed to him directly, so this is testimony he heard firsthand from a named witness — how the Rebbe's prayers, particularly in his later years, could run so long that meals prepared for him hours earlier would go completely untouched; the Rebbetzin would show Rabbi Shapiro the leftovers reassembled almost like a puzzle, uneaten, and say simply: he didn't eat a thing. Not once, she told him — repeatedly. Asked why, the Rebbe reportedly said: from a young age, "you have to get used to not eating."

One story, drawn from a written account by fellow survivors — three men, one of whom apparently became a writer — describes the Rebbe in Bergen-Belsen fasting several times a week and praying the full liturgy from memory even there. When guards lined prisoners up to forcibly shave their beards, the Rebbe handed clippers to the man standing beside him (named as Yosef Ashkenazi) and asked him to cut his beard himself rather than let a guard touch it. In the confusion the clippers were lost. When the guard reached him, he simply jabbed a pencil into the beard, declared it "clean," and moved on — the only man in that line whose beard went untouched. Rabbi Shapiro calls this *siyata dishmaya* — Divine assistance — following him even there.

He ties this directly back to the previous section: the instant, unprepared Torah answers, he argues, weren't pure intellect. They were the fruit of exactly this kind of self-denial. One story underscores how fragile the balance could look from outside: a doctor examining the Rebbe late in life was reportedly astonished he could still blow the shofar — physically demanding even standing, let alone seated, which the Rebbe insisted on doing. And yet, Rabbi Shapiro notes, it apparently wasn't the fasting or the sleepless nights that ever visibly wore him down; he's quoted as saying he never once felt weak from hunger. What did visibly shake him, according to one account, was being shown a difficult text touching on Divine suffering — that alone reportedly caused a collapse taking weeks to recover from.

## Compassion Without Self-Interest

Rabbi Shapiro shifts to stories about a compassion he frames as categorically different from ordinary decency — rooted, in his argument, specifically in deep Torah learning rather than in temperament.

He recalls, as something he personally experienced, speaking at a bar mitzvah where the family asked him beforehand to be careful, since some relatives present were intermarried and a previous speaker had apparently made pointed remarks. Rather than soften his talk, he used it to make an argument: there's a real difference, he told the room, between the ethics of a "nice guy" and the ethics Torah study can produce in a person — heights "you can't even imagine if you never learned it." To prove it, he posed the room a real story about the Rebbe and asked them to guess the ending.

A man had come to the Rebbe with an elaborate hardship story — dying parents, children needing to marry, mounting debts — and received a large sum in charity. The Rebbe's people later discovered the whole story was fabricated. What was his reaction? Rabbi Shapiro collected guesses from his audience — forgiveness, resignation — before giving the real answer: relief. "He's a faker? Thank God. I thought this man was suffering. I'm so happy he doesn't have real troubles." No resentment at being deceived, because his concern from the start had only ever been the man's suffering — and learning there wasn't any was, to him, simply good news. Rabbi Shapiro says he's tried this same exercise on many audiences since, and no one ever guesses it. That's the whole point.

He adds a line attributed directly to the Rebbe: that he had never in his life knowingly hurt so much as a fly, and that whatever suffering came to him from opponents was only because he insisted on speaking the truth openly.

## Leadership and Practical Judgment

This section turns to what looks, at first, like an unlikely pairing — a man raised deliberately shielded from the outside world showing sharp, worldly political judgment — but Rabbi Shapiro treats it as one more example of his larger theme: real greatness isn't confined to one lane.

He describes the Rebbe as someone who never read a newspaper and never learned to write in Latin characters — his parents taught him only to sign his name, for travel documents, and nothing more; his father held that children should be shielded even from things merely unsuitable, not only forbidden. Against that backdrop, Rabbi Shapiro tells of the Rebbe declining to co-sign a petition against missionary activity that several major rabbis wanted his name on, predicting — correctly, according to the story — that the Israeli Prime Minister was about to visit the Pope and would use precisely such a document as evidence that even his religious opponents backed the government's broader posture. The others signed without him. Events, Rabbi Shapiro says, played out exactly as predicted, prompting one of the signers to remark afterward that the Rebbe "sees further than all of us."

The fullest illustration is the **Six-Day War of 1967**, and Rabbi Shapiro is careful here to correct what he calls a common misconception: that the Rebbe attributed Israel's swift victory to open miracles. Not so, he insists — there are, by his count, six places in the Rebbe's writings on the war, and in every one the Rebbe explicitly distinguishes this outcome from an overt, Sea-of-Reeds-style miracle. His actual position, as Rabbi Shapiro presents it, was that Israel's military position was simply strong, that outside analysts already knew it, and that the triumphant public talk of open miracles actually worried him — because if this wasn't a miracle and people called it one, what would they do when a real one came? Rabbi Shapiro recalls hearing, as a child, that before the war the Rebbe had said — with no access to any intelligence briefing, no English, never having read a paper in his life — that if Israel's ambassador asked President Johnson for help and was told American assessments already predicted a decisive Israeli win even if Israel struck first, that alone told the whole story. Rabbi Shapiro adds, as his own aside to the audience, that this reading lines up with material now public — CIA assessments from the period, and Israeli military histories — though he offers this as his own supporting point for the Rebbe's judgment, not as the substance of the Rebbe's claim itself. What stays with him, he says, is simpler: a man with none of that information reasoning his way to the same place the analysts eventually confirmed. Asked once, personally, how he understood politics so well, the Rebbe told him: "A lot of people know more than me — but politics, I know."

The same practical shrewdness, in Rabbi Shapiro's account, showed up in smaller, warmer ways too — a philanthropist caught between two communities each suspicious of his ties to the other, resolved with a single letter and a laugh. Rabbi Shapiro notes, almost as an aside, that a secular Israeli magazine, obligated after the Rebbe's passing to publish something despite clear disapproval of his positions, filled its tribute mostly with a list of his jokes — proof, to Rabbi Shapiro, of a real sense of humor sitting alongside everything else.

## Building an Independent Religious World

The talk shifts from anecdote to institutional philosophy, which Rabbi Shapiro treats as one of the Rebbe's most significant and least appreciated achievements.

He opens with a teaching about the midwives Shifra and Puah: as major figures in their own right (identified in tradition with greater Biblical women), why does caring for infants earn them such distinction — surely they had bigger things to do? The answer: their greatness was caring as much about the smallest, least powerful people as about the large affairs they were equally capable of. Rabbi Shapiro applies this to the Rebbe's institution-building — the same man engaged with global politics and mass rescue treated each individual community member's concern with the same seriousness.

He recalls, from personal experience, being asked at a major yeshiva why the Rebbe reportedly disapproved of how quickly students there moved through the Talmud. He turned the question back on the room: how long had they taken to finish their current tractate? About three weeks, they said. He then asked a basic practical question about that same material — no one could answer it. That, he told them, was the concern: speed without depth produces knowledge you can't actually use.

He also recalls being present, alongside another rabbi, when the Rebbe was asked which method of study he personally preferred — Lithuanian or Hungarian. The Rebbe answered that the Hungarian and Polish approaches were essentially the same, and the Lithuanian method was "the outlier" — but that whichever approach suited a given person was the one that person should follow, since every authentic path leads to the same place. Not indifference to method, Rabbi Shapiro stresses, but recognition that different people genuinely need different roads.

That pluralism about method, though, sat beside an absolute insistence on one unmoving standard. Every institution faces real pressure — schools built around their students' backgrounds, congregations shaped by their donors, communities that can only be spoken to in the terms they'll actually hear. Rabbi Shapiro cites, from a memorial volume of writings attributed to a Telshe Rosh Yeshiva, a case in point: students there covered comparatively little Talmud by volume because they also studied secular subjects all afternoon toward professional careers, and needed real intellectual satisfaction in their limited learning time simply to stay engaged at all — a legitimate accommodation, not a failure.

But then the warning, built on an image worth sitting with: picture crossing a lake. The direct route is a bridge, straight across. But if someone won't take the bridge, you send him around instead — north, then south, then up a tree and around. Multiply that across a whole community, everyone circling for good reasons, and — as Rabbi Shapiro puts it — "sooner or later you forget where the ultimate direction is." People come to believe the winding path *is* the destination, simply because it's the path their own teacher handed them, without ever realizing the teacher only gave them that path because it was the most that person, at that moment, could manage.

## The Clock on the Tower

Rabbi Shapiro closes this section with an explanation he attributes directly to the Rebbe: why public clocks sit high on towers rather than at street level, where they'd be easier to read. If the clock were at street level, the Rebbe reportedly explained, then every time your watch disagreed with it, you'd simply walk over and reset the clock to match your watch — and the next person would do the same to match *his* watch — until the public clock meant nothing at all. Mounted high on the tower, out of casual reach, it works the other way: everyone adjusts their own watch to match the clock, because the clock itself can't be casually touched.

He treats this as close to the thesis of everything he's just said: the Torah's real standard has to stay "up on the tower" — visible, authoritative, out of reach of convenience — precisely so that when people or communities need to accommodate their own circumstances, and they will, it's *they* who do the adjusting, never the standard. There will always be people who need to observe things in modified ways for good reason; that was never the question. The question was making sure no one ever loses sight of what the untouched original actually looks like.

## A Living Sefer Torah

Rabbi Shapiro returns, near the end, to the puzzle he opened with: how do you describe someone who seems to hold together things that don't normally sit in one person — secluded, all-consuming scholarship *and* worldly political sharpness; total physical self-denial *and* apparently undiminished strength; an iron commitment to one authentic path *and* genuine patience for other people's different paths?

His answer draws on a teaching that no character trait is inherently good or bad — even apparent opposites, like strictness and mercy, are simply tools, right in one moment and wrong in another. A person of real completeness isn't locked into a single disposition; he reaches for whatever a given moment calls for, because the Torah itself contains all these seemingly contradictory qualities as one whole. That, Rabbi Shapiro says, is the difference between an ordinary righteous person, who may excel in one dimension, and what tradition calls a "living *Sefer Torah*" (Torah scroll) — someone in whom the Torah's full range, contradictions included, is simultaneously present. It's the only way, he argues, to make sense of a man who ran an entire network of institutions, personally tested every student in his yeshiva each week, gave the sermons, led the *tisch* (the Rebbe's communal table), answered impossible questions on the spot, and tracked global politics — all without reading a newspaper or, by his own account, ever once feeling weak from hunger.

## Conclusion

Rabbi Shapiro ends where he began: God doesn't send a soul like this for nothing. The Satmar Rebbe, in his framing, was sent to show a generation that had never seen it what the earlier generations of Torah greatness actually looked like — not described, but demonstrated, in a living person. He notes that the Rebbe passed away decades before this talk, but says his teachings are still fully alive and available to anyone willing to look for them. It's not too late, he tells the room, for someone who never met him to still draw close, learn from him, and let him serve — even now — as an advocate and a model for the generation that follows.
