# The Secret Elite: World War I Through the Lens of Hidden History

## Introduction

Ask most people why World War I started and you will hear some version of the same story. A Serbian nationalist shot an Austrian archduke in Sarajevo, the alliance system kicked in like a row of falling dominoes, and Germany, aggressive and militaristic, plunged Europe into catastrophe. That last part was written into the peace treaty itself. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles placed responsibility for the war on Germany and its allies, and that verdict has shaped how the war has been taught ever since.

In 2013, two Scottish writers, Gerry Docherty, a retired teacher of economics and modern studies, and Jim Macgregor, published a book that turns this story upside down. *Hidden History: The Secret Origins of the First World War* argues that the war was not an accident and not primarily Germany's doing. Instead, they claim, it was the product of a deliberate, decades-long effort by a small, secretive network of powerful men based in Britain, men who saw Germany as the greatest threat to British world dominance and who worked patiently to surround it, isolate it, and finally destroy it.

They call this network the Secret Elite. Its founders included the diamond magnate Cecil Rhodes, and its long-time leader was a figure most people have never heard of: Alfred Milner. This article walks through their argument, explains who these men were and what the authors say they did, and connects it to the economic rivalry and the Berlin–Baghdad Railway. Then it brings in a much older and far more controversial reading of the same war: that of the British writer Nesta Webster, who in the 1920s argued that World War I was one act in a centuries-long drama staged by secret societies. Putting the two side by side shows how writers working decades apart, each convinced the official account left something out, looked at the same war and found hidden hands at work, where their accounts agree, and where they point in completely different directions. Finally, the article looks at what the documentary record actually shows, including about the Rothschild banking family, and at why most professional historians reject both theories. You can make up your own mind.

## Part One: The Professor Who Found the Network

Docherty and Macgregor did not invent the idea of a secret British network out of thin air. Their starting point was the work of Carroll Quigley, a respected historian at Georgetown University's School of Foreign Service, who taught generations of American diplomats. Bill Clinton, one of his former students, cited him by name in his speech accepting the Democratic nomination in 1992.

In 1949, Quigley finished a manuscript called *The Anglo-American Establishment*. It was not published until 1981, after his death. In it, and later in his giant 1966 history *Tragedy and Hope*, Quigley described a secret society founded by Cecil Rhodes in the 1890s, which over the following decades grew into an influential network that Quigley called the Milner Group. According to Quigley, this group shaped British imperial policy, controlled important newspapers, dominated key Oxford colleges, and had a strong hand in foreign affairs from the Boer War through the 1930s.

What makes Quigley especially interesting is that he was not hostile to the group. He said he largely agreed with its goals and objected mainly to its secrecy. That gives his account an unusual credibility in the eyes of writers like Docherty and Macgregor: this was not an enemy making accusations but an insider-sympathetic scholar describing what he had found.

Quigley's own work focused on the group's influence over imperial policy, the press, and academia. The leap to claiming that this network deliberately engineered World War I is Docherty and Macgregor's.

## Part Two: Cecil Rhodes and the Dream of a British World

Cecil Rhodes was one of the richest men on earth. He made his fortune in the diamond fields of South Africa, consolidating the mines into the De Beers company with the backing of the Rothschild banking house in London, and later expanded into gold. He became Prime Minister of the Cape Colony, and a whole region of Africa was named after him: Rhodesia, today Zambia and Zimbabwe.

Rhodes was also a man with a grand and openly stated ideology. As a young man at Oxford, he had been inspired by the art critic John Ruskin, whose famous inaugural lecture in 1870 called on young Englishmen to carry their nation's rule and values across the world. In 1877, Rhodes wrote a document he called his "Confession of Faith," in which he argued that the English-speaking peoples were the finest race in the world and that the more of the world they ruled, the better it would be for humanity. He dreamed of bringing the whole uncivilized world under British rule, of a railway running from the Cape to Cairo, and even of eventually bringing the United States back into the British fold.

To pursue that dream, Rhodes believed he needed something like a secular religious order, a secret society of dedicated men who would work across generations. He wrote this intention into a series of wills. The famous Rhodes Scholarships, which still bring students from around the world to Oxford, came out of the last of them.

According to Quigley, and following him Docherty and Macgregor, the secret society was formally founded in London in February 1891. They name five founding figures:

- **Cecil Rhodes**, the money and the vision.
- **William T. Stead**, the most famous crusading journalist of his day, editor of the *Pall Mall Gazette* and later the *Review of Reviews*.
- **Reginald Brett, later Lord Esher**, a behind-the-scenes courtier who would become the closest adviser to King Edward VII.
- **Nathaniel Rothschild**, head of the British branch of the Rothschild bank and the financier behind Rhodes's diamond empire.
- **Alfred Milner**, a brilliant Oxford-trained administrator who would become the society's leader after Rhodes died.

In Quigley's description, the society had an inner core who knew its full aims and an outer ring of supporters who were drawn into its work without necessarily knowing that an organized group stood behind it.

## Part Three: Alfred Milner, the Man History Forgot

If there is a central character in *Hidden History*, it is Alfred Milner. Docherty and Macgregor argue that he was one of the most powerful men in the British Empire for a quarter of a century and that he has been almost entirely left out of the popular story of the war. The authors point out that Milner's early biographies were written by people within his own circle, and they claim that a large amount of his correspondence was destroyed or kept from the public.

Milner was born in 1854, educated at Balliol College, Oxford, and worked as a journalist under Stead at the *Pall Mall Gazette* before moving into government service in Egypt and at the Treasury. He described himself as a British race patriot whose loyalty was to the empire as a whole, not merely to the British Isles.

### The Boer War as a dress rehearsal

In 1897, Milner was appointed High Commissioner for Southern Africa. The region was then divided between British colonies and two independent Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State. The Transvaal sat on top of the largest gold deposits in the world, the Witwatersrand.

Rhodes had already tried to grab the Transvaal once. In late 1895, his associate Leander Starr Jameson led an armed raid into the republic, hoping to spark an uprising among foreign miners. It failed humiliatingly, and Rhodes was forced to resign as Prime Minister of the Cape.

Docherty and Macgregor argue that Milner then accomplished by diplomacy what Rhodes had failed to do by force. Through a series of demands over the voting rights of British miners in the Transvaal, Milner pushed negotiations to a point the Boers could not accept. War broke out in 1899. It lasted until 1902, cost tens of thousands of lives, and became notorious for the British concentration camps in which large numbers of Boer women and children, and Black Africans, died of disease and neglect. At the end of it, the gold fields were under British control.

For the authors, the Boer War is the template for everything that follows: a war deliberately provoked by a small group of men in pursuit of imperial and financial goals, sold to the public as a defense of British rights and honor.

### The Kindergarten and the Round Table

While in South Africa, Milner gathered around him a team of bright young Oxford graduates who became known as "Milner's Kindergarten." Several went on to powerful careers. Lionel Curtis became a leading theorist of imperial federation. Philip Kerr became private secretary to Prime Minister Lloyd George and later ambassador to the United States. Robert Brand became a prominent banker. Geoffrey Dawson became editor of *The Times*, Britain's most influential newspaper.

After returning to Britain, Milner and his circle founded the Round Table movement around 1909–1910, with groups across the empire and a journal of the same name. In Quigley's account and that of Docherty and Macgregor, the network was now positioned in politics, the civil service, the press, finance, and the Oxford colleges of Balliol, New College, and All Souls, which in turn shaped how British history was written.

## Part Four: The German Problem

By the early 1900s, according to *Hidden History*, the Secret Elite had identified their great enemy. It was not France, Britain's historic rival, nor Russia, which had been threatening British India for decades. It was Germany.

The economic facts behind this fear are real and widely accepted. Germany had unified only in 1871, but within a generation it had overtaken Britain in steel production and was leading the world in chemicals, electrical engineering, and scientific research. German manufactured goods were beating British ones in markets around the world. The German merchant marine was expanding. German banks were reaching into Latin America, Africa, and the Ottoman Empire. And from 1898, Germany was building a battle fleet.

Above all, Germany was reaching toward the Middle East. The Berlin–Baghdad Railway, a German-financed line that would run from central Europe across the Ottoman Empire to Baghdad and on toward the Persian Gulf, threatened to give Germany an overland trade route that the Royal Navy could not blockade. It would open the oil-rich lands of Mesopotamia to German companies and bring German influence close to the approaches to British India. As the Royal Navy switched from coal to oil in the years before the war, the Persian Gulf became even more vital.

In the logic of the Secret Elite as Docherty and Macgregor describe it, all of this pointed to one conclusion. If Germany kept growing, the British Empire would be overtaken, not in a sudden defeat but in a slow commercial decline. Germany could not be outcompeted. It would have to be crushed. And since Britain could not do that alone, it would need allies on the continent who could put millions of soldiers in the field.

## Part Five: Building the Trap

The heart of *Hidden History* is the claim that between 1901 and 1914, the Secret Elite systematically rearranged Europe's alliances and Britain's military commitments to prepare for that war, often without the knowledge of Parliament, much of the Cabinet, or the British public.

### King Edward VII

The authors give a central role to King Edward VII, who came to the throne in 1901. Edward was the uncle of the German Kaiser, Wilhelm II, and the two disliked each other intensely. Lord Esher, one of the society's founders, was the king's closest adviser. Edward was a skilled and charming diplomat, and he spent much of his reign travelling to foreign capitals.

In 1903, Edward made a highly successful state visit to Paris that helped turn French public opinion toward Britain. The following year, Britain and France signed the Entente Cordiale, settling their colonial disputes. France recognized British control of Egypt, and Britain gave France a free hand in Morocco. In 1908, Edward met Tsar Nicholas II at Reval, now Tallinn, in the Baltic, cementing the new relationship with Russia. To many in Germany, this looked like a policy of deliberate encirclement, and Germans used exactly that word, *Einkreisung*, to describe it.

### The Liberal Imperialists

Docherty and Macgregor argue that the Secret Elite did not depend on any one party. When the Liberal Party came to power in 1905, the key foreign and defense posts went to three men the authors identify as Milner's chosen men: Edward Grey as Foreign Secretary, Richard Haldane as Secretary for War, and Herbert Asquith, first as Chancellor of the Exchequer and from 1908 as Prime Minister. These three were leaders of the "Liberal Imperialist" wing of the party, and earlier in 1905 they had agreed among themselves, in what became known as the Relugas Compact, to work together to secure these offices.

### Secret military conversations

Here the authors stand on some of their firmest ground. In early 1906, Grey authorized secret talks between the British and French general staffs about how British troops would fight alongside the French army in a war against Germany. Most of the Cabinet was not told. The conversations continued for years, and detailed plans were drawn up for sending a British Expeditionary Force to France. The full Cabinet only learned the extent of these arrangements in 1911.

In 1912, Britain and France went further. Under a naval arrangement, France concentrated its fleet in the Mediterranean while Britain concentrated its fleet in the North Sea, which in practice meant Britain was protecting France's northern coast. An exchange of letters between Grey and the French ambassador, Paul Cambon, stated that neither side was bound to help the other. But Docherty and Macgregor argue that this was a fiction designed to reassure Parliament: France had stripped its Channel defenses on the assumption that Britain would protect them, and Britain was morally committed whatever the letters said.

Throughout this period, Grey repeatedly told Parliament that Britain had no secret obligations that would bind it to go to war. The authors see this as deliberate deception.

### Belgium

The authors also point to staff conversations between British and Belgian military officers in 1906 and again in 1912, which discussed how British forces might land and operate in Belgium in the event of a German invasion. Docherty and Macgregor argue that these talks compromised Belgium's official neutrality and that Belgium's role in 1914 was not that of an innocent bystander. In their telling, the German invasion of Belgium, which became Britain's public reason for going to war, was something the planners had anticipated for years and were prepared to use.

### Russia

The other half of the trap was Russia. In 1907, Britain and Russia signed the Anglo-Russian Convention, dividing Persia into spheres of influence and settling disputes over Afghanistan and Tibet. Britain, which had treated Russia as its main imperial threat for most of the nineteenth century, suddenly became its partner. Meanwhile, French banks lent huge sums to the Russian government, some of it tied to building strategic railways that would let Russia move troops quickly to the German frontier.

## Part Six: The Partners Abroad

*Hidden History* argues that the Secret Elite had willing partners in Paris and St. Petersburg.

Two figures stand out. The first is Alexander Izvolsky, Russian Foreign Minister from 1906 to 1910 and then ambassador in Paris until 1917. Izvolsky was humiliated in the Bosnian crisis of 1908, when Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia and Herzegovina after what he believed was a deal to support Russian access to the Turkish Straits, a deal that never materialized. The authors argue that from then on he worked tirelessly for war with Germany and Austria. When war came, he is reported to have boasted that it was his war, though the anecdote comes through later accounts.

The second is Raymond Poincaré, who became French Prime Minister in 1912 and President in 1913. Poincaré came from Lorraine, part of which France had lost to Germany in 1871, and the authors portray him as determined to win it back. He strengthened the military alliance with Russia and encouraged Russian rearmament. In late July 1914, at the very height of the crisis, he was on a state visit to St. Petersburg, and the authors argue that he used it to stiffen Russian resolve.

## Part Seven: Sarajevo

The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand on June 28, 1914, was carried out by young Bosnian Serbs armed and helped by a Serbian secret society, the Black Hand, led by Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, who was also the head of Serbian military intelligence. That much is accepted by historians.

Docherty and Macgregor go further, arguing that the plot had support from Russian officials. They point to claims, made by Apis himself during his later trial in 1917, that the Russian military attaché in Belgrade, Viktor Artamonov, knew of the plan and had promised Russian backing. These claims are disputed, and Artamonov denied them. In the authors' view, though, Sarajevo was the spark the war planners needed, a crisis in the Balkans that would draw in Austria-Hungary, then Russia, then Germany, then France, with Britain entering at the moment of its choosing.

## Part Eight: July 1914

The authors read the July Crisis as the trap being sprung. In their account, Grey kept Germany guessing about Britain's intentions, never clearly warning Berlin that Britain would fight, which encouraged Germany to believe Britain might stay neutral. At the same time, France and Russia had been privately reassured. Russia ordered general mobilization on July 30, a step that, under the military plans of the day, made a German response almost automatic.

Germany's war plan, the Schlieffen Plan, required a rapid attack on France through Belgium. When German troops crossed the Belgian border on August 4, Britain declared war, presenting itself to the world as the defender of a small neutral nation. Two members of the Cabinet, John Morley and John Burns, resigned rather than support the war. In the authors' view, the "defense of Belgium" was the public face of a decision made years earlier.

## Part Nine: The War, the Peace, and the Cover-Up

Docherty and Macgregor carried the story forward in a second book, *Prolonging the Agony* (2018). There they argue that once the war was underway, the same network deliberately kept it going for years longer than necessary. They point to evidence that the British naval blockade of Germany was porous in practice, with supplies reaching Germany through neutral countries such as the Netherlands and Scandinavia, and they argue this helped Germany keep fighting while bankers and industrialists profited. They also argue that peace opportunities were rejected.

Milner himself returned to the center of power. In December 1916, when Lloyd George became Prime Minister, Milner joined the small War Cabinet that ran the British war effort, and later served as Secretary of State for War and then Colonial Secretary. Members of his circle filled key advisory roles. The authors and Quigley both note that Milner had a hand in the 1917 Balfour Declaration, which promised British support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine.

The peace settlement, in the authors' view, delivered exactly what the network had wanted. Germany lost its navy, its colonies, and its overseas investments. It was saddled with the war guilt clause and massive reparations. In the Middle East, the Ottoman Empire was carved up, and Britain took control of Mesopotamia, now Iraq, including the oil of Mosul. The railway that had once promised German access to the Persian Gulf now ran into British-controlled territory.

The network also institutionalized itself. Members of the Milner circle were central to founding the Royal Institute of International Affairs, known as Chatham House, in London in 1920, and Quigley linked its American counterpart, the Council on Foreign Relations, founded in 1921, to the same movement. Finally, the authors argue that the official story of the war was shaped by historians and archivists sympathetic to the British establishment, and that inconvenient documents were destroyed or withheld, which is why, in their words, this history remained hidden.

## Part Ten: Nesta Webster's Arguments About the War

Decades before Quigley's account of the Milner Group appeared, Nesta Webster argued in *World Revolution* (1921) and *Secret Societies and Subversive Movements* (1924) that World War I had been shaped by hidden forces. Her reading of the war is very different from the one in *Hidden History*, and several of its threads touch on documented events that the standard story tends to pass over quickly.

### The framework: one hidden current behind every upheaval

Webster's starting point was the Bavarian Illuminati, a real secret society founded in 1776 by the law professor Adam Weishaupt and suppressed by the Bavarian government in the 1780s. Following the writers Augustin Barruel and John Robison, she argued that its revolutionary program survived inside certain branches of Freemasonry and passed from the French Revolution through the revolutions of 1848, socialism, anarchism, and finally Bolshevism. In her view, the major upheavals of modern history were stages in a single long campaign against monarchy, religion, property, and the nation.

She did not see that campaign as one organization with one command. In *Secret Societies and Subversive Movements*, she named several forces at work: revolutionary Freemasonry of the French Grand Orient type, Theosophy and other occult movements, Pan-Germanism, international finance, and social revolution. These, she argued, sometimes competed and sometimes cooperated, but together they pushed toward the destruction of the old order. World War I was where several of them converged.

### Claim one: Pan-Germanism used revolution as a weapon

The heart of Webster's interpretation is that German imperial ambition and international revolution worked together. Unlike *Hidden History*, she placed the aggressive drive on the German side, but she argued that Germany pursued its aims not only with armies but by setting off revolutions inside its enemies' empires.

This is the part of her argument with the strongest support in the documentary record, much of which became fully available only after her books were written. Germany did pursue a deliberate wartime strategy, sometimes called "revolutionizing," of stirring up unrest behind enemy lines:

- **Russia.** In 1917, the German government arranged for Lenin and other exiled revolutionaries to cross Germany from Switzerland in a sealed train, and German funds reached the Bolsheviks through intermediaries such as Alexander Helphand, known as Parvus, a revolutionary-turned-financier who had pitched the plan to Berlin. The aim was to knock Russia out of the war, and after the Bolshevik seizure of power, Russia signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in 1918, handing Germany vast territory.
- **The Islamic world.** In November 1914, the Ottoman Sultan, now Germany's ally, proclaimed a holy war against Britain, France, and Russia. The idea had been promoted in Berlin, notably by the orientalist Max von Oppenheim, who argued that Muslim subjects of the British and Russian empires could be incited to rise. German missions went to Afghanistan and Persia to try to spark revolts aimed at British India.
- **India.** Germany backed Indian revolutionaries abroad in what became known as the Hindu–German Conspiracy, including plans to ship arms to India for an uprising.
- **Ireland.** Germany shipped arms toward Ireland for the 1916 Easter Rising, and the Irish nationalist Roger Casement worked with the German government before being captured and executed.

This is also where Webster's reading connects to the Berlin–Baghdad Railway. The railway was meant to carry German goods and influence through the Ottoman Empire toward the Persian Gulf, the edge of British India. In wartime, the same route became the corridor for German officers, agents, and the holy-war campaign aimed at Britain's Muslim subjects. Sean McMeekin's *The Berlin-Baghdad Express* tells this story in detail. For a writer like Webster, it illustrated her point: German power and the politics of revolution and subversion traveled down the same tracks.

### Claim two: revolutionary Freemasonry and the Balkans

Webster drew a sharp line between British Freemasonry, which she generally regarded as harmless and patriotic, and the Grand Orient tradition of continental Europe, which she saw as political and revolutionary. That distinction led her and writers in her tradition to look closely at the Masonic connections of revolutionary movements in the years before the war.

Two episodes get the most attention:

- **The Young Turks.** The Committee of Union and Progress, the movement that took power in the Ottoman Empire after the 1908 revolution and later brought it into the war on Germany's side, organized in its early years in Salonika, partly through Masonic lodges chartered under Italian and French obediences, which offered meeting places protected from the Sultan's police. Historians accept that this link existed. They disagree on how much it mattered: the lodges were a useful cover for a nationalist officers' movement, not evidence that Masonry directed it.
- **Sarajevo.** At the October 1914 trial of the assassins, one of the conspirators, Nedeljko Čabrinović, testified that Milan Ciganović, the man who supplied the weapons in Belgrade, was a Freemason, and suggested that Freemasons had condemned the Archduke to death. Writers in Webster's tradition seized on this testimony as evidence of a Masonic hand in the assassination. Historians of Sarajevo have found no evidence supporting it. The documented chain runs through the Black Hand and Serbian military intelligence, and the defendant's testimony at trial was inconsistent.

### Claim three: the war's real purpose was revolution

Webster's broadest argument concerned the outcome. By 1918, the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman empires had all collapsed. The Romanov, Habsburg, and Hohenzollern dynasties had fallen. Bolsheviks ruled Russia and briefly took power in Hungary and Bavaria, and revolutionary uprisings broke out in Berlin. To Webster, a result this sweeping, and this favorable to the revolutionary cause, could not be a coincidence. The war had done in four years what generations of revolutionaries had been unable to do: tear down the Christian monarchies of Europe. She argued that the hidden revolutionary current she traced back to the Illuminati was the real beneficiary.

Historians answer that revolution followed the war because the war broke the old states, not because the war was planned to produce it. The monarchies that fell were, after all, the ones that had chosen war in 1914, and the ones that lost it.

### Claim four: international finance and the Jewish question

The most controversial thread of Webster's argument is her claim that behind or alongside these forces stood an international Jewish power, working through finance and through revolutionary movements. Her main evidence was twofold.

The first was the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, which purported to be the secret program of Jewish leaders for world domination and which the London *Morning Post* promoted in 1920. In August 1921, *The Times* published Philip Graves's discovery that large passages had been plagiarized from an 1864 French satire by Maurice Joly aimed at Napoleon III, which has no connection to Jews. The text had been fabricated in the Russian Empire around 1900. Webster continued to argue that the document described something real even if its origin was false.

The second was the prominence of revolutionaries of Jewish background among the Bolshevik leadership, such as Leon Trotsky, Grigory Zinoviev, and Lev Kamenev. The record shows these men had broken with Judaism and saw themselves as internationalist revolutionaries; most Russian Jews were not Bolsheviks, and the leading Jewish political parties were rivals of the Bolsheviks. Jewish communities were also among the worst victims of the Russian Civil War, with tens of thousands killed in pogroms, mostly by anti-Bolshevik forces. Critics have long argued that this thread of her case treats the actions of particular individuals as the responsibility of an entire people.

## Part Eleven: Where the Two Theories Meet and Collide

Set *Hidden History* beside Webster's work and the similarities jump out. Both believe that the official story of World War I is a cover. Both believe that small, secretive groups shaped events from behind the scenes over many decades. Both believe the historical profession helped hide the truth: Docherty and Macgregor point to Milner's circle in the Oxford colleges, and Webster complained in her autobiography that her books were deliberately boycotted by those who wanted her findings suppressed. And both read the war's dramatic outcomes, the fall of empires and the redrawing of the map, as evidence of design.

But the two theories point in opposite directions. For Docherty and Macgregor, the villains sat at the very top of the British Empire: Rhodes, Milner, Grey, Asquith, and the men around King Edward VII. For Webster, the British Empire was the victim. She wrote as a loyal imperialist, and her heroes were the very establishment that *Hidden History* indicts. Her enemies were revolutionaries, occultists, Pan-Germans, and, in her view, Jews. Webster died before Quigley's account of the Milner Group was published, and her books built their case around entirely different villains.

The one place where the two stories can blur together is the word "finance." Both theories name international finance as a hidden force, and Quigley's list of the Rhodes society's founders includes Nathaniel Rothschild. This is exactly the seam where later writers have stitched the two traditions together, taking the documented, specific claims about Rhodes and Milner and fusing them with Webster-style claims about a Jewish conspiracy. That combination is common online today. It is worth recognizing, because it turns on the same question Webster's critics raised about her own work: whether the actions of particular people are being attributed to an entire people.

## Part Twelve: The Rothschilds in 1914: What the Record Shows

Since the Rothschild name comes up in both traditions, it is worth looking at what the family actually did. The Rothschilds were a powerful banking family that happened to be Jewish, and their real history is in some ways more surprising than the myths.

By the late nineteenth century, the family operated major banking houses in London, Paris, and Vienna. In London, Nathaniel "Natty" Rothschild, the first Lord Rothschild, was one of the most important financiers in the empire, and his bank did help Cecil Rhodes consolidate the diamond mines into De Beers. That connection is real and is part of why Quigley placed him among Rhodes's circle.

But by 1914, the Rothschilds were no longer the dominant force in world finance they had been a century earlier. Joint-stock banks, Deutsche Bank in Germany, and the American house of J.P. Morgan had grown enormously. When war came, it was J.P. Morgan, not the Rothschilds, that became the Allies' purchasing agent and banker in the United States.

More striking still, the evidence shows the London Rothschilds tried to keep Britain out of the war. As the historian Niall Ferguson documents in his history of the family, in the final days of July 1914 Lord Rothschild urged *The Times* to soften its hostile line toward Germany and support neutrality. The paper's foreign editor, Henry Wickham Steed, angrily rejected the approach, regarding it as a German-Jewish financial attempt to bully the paper, and *The Times* became even more hawkish. So in July 1914, the leading British Rothschild was on the side of peace, and he was rebuffed partly because of prejudice about who he was.

The war itself split the family the way it split Europe. The Paris and London houses were on the Allied side and the Vienna house on the side of the Central Powers. Natty Rothschild died in 1915. His relative, Evelyn de Rothschild, a British army officer, died of wounds suffered fighting the Ottomans in Palestine in 1917. Whatever one thinks about the power of great banking dynasties, and that is a fair subject for scrutiny, the record does not show a family pulling the strings of a war that cost it its sons and set its branches against one another.

## Part Thirteen: The Case Against Both Theories

*Hidden History* has a large following among readers skeptical of official narratives, and it formed the basis of James Corbett's 2018 documentary *The WWI Conspiracy*. But it has had very little acceptance among professional historians of the war, and it is important to understand why.

**Quigley does not say what the authors say.** Quigley described an influential network, but his work focuses on imperial policy, the press, and academic influence. He did not argue that the group planned World War I. Critics also point out that Quigley's evidence for how organized and secret the group really was is thinner than his confident tone suggests. Many historians see the Milner circle as an influential clique of like-minded imperialists, not a disciplined conspiracy steering world events.

**Influence is not control.** It is true that the men in this story knew each other, went to the same colleges, wrote for the same papers, and shared a worldview. That was true of the whole British ruling class of the time. Showing that powerful people were connected and held similar views is not the same as showing that they coordinated a plan for war over two decades.

**Decisions in Vienna and Berlin.** The documentary record of July 1914 shows that Austria-Hungary chose to use the assassination to crush Serbia, and that Germany's leaders, in the so-called blank check of July 5–6, chose to back Austria knowing that this risked a war with Russia and France. Historians like Fritz Fischer, drawing on German archives, showed that some German leaders saw a war as an opportunity and drew up ambitious war aims shortly after it began. The Hidden History account tends to treat German and Austrian leaders as reacting to a trap rather than making their own choices.

**The Cabinet nearly split.** If a small group controlled British policy, critics ask, why was the Liberal Cabinet so divided in the first days of August 1914, with a majority initially reluctant to fight and two ministers resigning? Britain's entry was a near-run thing, decided largely by the invasion of Belgium.

**Contingency plans are not conspiracies.** Military staffs plan for many possible wars. The British-French and British-Belgian conversations were real, and Grey's lack of candor with Parliament is a genuine criticism that mainstream historians also make. But planning for a German attack on France through Belgium is different from causing one.

**The challenge to Webster's sources.** Historians regard Webster's world-revolution theory as resting on even weaker evidence than *Hidden History*. Its central documentary pillar, the *Protocols*, was proven to be a plagiarized forgery within a year of her campaign around it. Her claim that the Illuminati survived for more than a century as a hidden directing force rests on Barruel and Robison, writers whose evidence historians have long found unconvincing. And her method, treating any dramatic outcome as proof of hidden design and any revolutionary of Jewish background as evidence of collective Jewish guilt, can, critics argue, be used to prove almost anything. Historians such as Norman Cohn, in *Warrant for Genocide*, traced how this style of thinking, of which Webster was a leading English-language voice, helped pave the way for the Nazi genocide. For these reasons, most historians today study her work as a chapter in the history of ideas about secret societies and antisemitism, rather than as a guide to the origins of the war.

**Keep individuals and peoples separate.** The most useful rule for exploring any of this material is to stick to the documented actions of specific people. It is legitimate to ask what Milner did in South Africa, what Grey told Parliament, or what Natty Rothschild urged *The Times* to print. It stops being history when the actions of individuals become the supposed guilt of a group.

## Conclusion

Strip away the most dramatic claims, and much of what *Hidden History* describes is real. There was a circle of ambitious, imperial-minded men around Rhodes and Milner who held influential positions in politics, finance, the press, and Oxford. The Boer War was pushed by Milner. Germany's economic rise frightened Britain's elites. The Berlin–Baghdad Railway and Middle Eastern oil were genuine strategic stakes. Grey did make secret military commitments to France that he concealed from Parliament. And the peace of 1919 did hand Britain and France the colonies, oil, and territory that Germany had once reached for.

Where Docherty and Macgregor part company with mainstream historians is the claim that these facts add up to a single, deliberate plan, carried out over more than two decades by a secret society, to bring about the deadliest war the world had yet known. Whether you find that persuasive depends on how you weigh the evidence of intent against the evidence of chaos, miscalculation, and the choices made in Vienna, Berlin, and St. Petersburg.

Nesta Webster's version is a different kind of story. She drew on real events: the German government's sponsorship of Lenin, the collapse of four empires, the growth of revolutionary movements across Europe. She wove them into a single theory of hidden design. Its key document, the *Protocols*, was shown in 1921 to be a forgery, and her critics argue that her theory placed blame on a whole people. For those reasons her work remains among the most debated in this field. Comparing her with *Hidden History* shows how the same instinct, the suspicion that great events have hidden authors, can lead in very different directions depending on what evidence is accepted and where the blame is placed.

Either way, the question at the heart of both theories is worth asking about any war: who made the decisions, who kept them secret, and who stood to gain. The answers hold up best when they are about specific people, specific choices, and specific documents.
