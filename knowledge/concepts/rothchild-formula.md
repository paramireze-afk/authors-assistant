# The Rothschild Formula

## What It Is, Up Front

The Rothschild Formula is Griffin's name for a **hypothetical five-point strategy** he constructs in Chapter 11 to explain a pattern he believes runs through the last three centuries of central banking and warfare: that the ability to create fiat money gives a small class of financiers both the *means* and the *motive* to keep the world in a perpetual cycle of war and government debt, because war is what makes debt permanent and profitable on a scale peacetime taxation never could achieve.

Griffin is explicit that this is a theoretical construct, not a documented conspiracy with minutes and signatures. He builds it inductively — from the personality profile and documented behavior of the House of Rothschild and their financial successors — and then tests it against the historical record from the founding of the Bank of England in 1694 through the Gulf War. His conclusion is that whether or not anyone ever consciously wrote the Formula down, history behaves as though someone had.

---

## Origins: The House of Rothschild

Griffin grounds the Formula in the documented history of the Rothschild banking dynasty, founded by Mayer Amschel Rothschild (born Bauer), the son of a Frankfurt goldsmith. Mayer's fortune began, in Griffin's account, with his adoption of fractional-reserve banking, but it was multiplied by his five sons, each of whom established the family in a different European financial capital. By the first half of the nineteenth century, the Rothschild brothers were conducting major transactions for the governments of England, France, Prussia, Austria, Belgium, Spain, Naples, Portugal, Brazil, and the German states — effectively serving as personal bankers to much of the crowned heads of Europe.

Griffin draws two traits out of this history that become the psychological foundation of the Formula:

- **Secrecy as strategy.** The Rothschilds rarely sought public office or engaged in open political debate, yet — through the granting or withholding of funds, the provisioning of statesmen with an informal diplomatic service, and daily contact with decision-makers — they shaped the major events of their era from behind the scenes. Griffin notes this technique "has been practiced by financial manipulators ever since, and it is fully utilized by those who operate the Federal Reserve System today."
- **Profit from both sides of conflict.** Continual European war created opportunities for smuggling scarce goods past military blockades; because the Rothschilds often financed both sides of a given conflict and carried real political influence, the sight of their red-shield insignia was frequently enough to guarantee safe passage through checkpoints on either side of a war zone.

## The Foundational Case Study: Napoleon vs. the Bankers

Griffin presents the economic war waged against Napoleon Bonaparte as the clearest illustration of the pattern the Formula would later describe.

### Napoleon's refusal to borrow

Napoleon, having restored order to post-revolutionary France, was determined to keep his government out of debt and out of bankers' control. He forbade loans for current government expenditure and stated his reasoning plainly: a government dependent on bankers for money is controlled by them, not the reverse — "the hand that gives is above the hand that takes." He distrusted financiers openly, declaring that "money has no motherland; financiers are without patriotism and without decency: their sole object is gain."

Griffin notes the financiers' calculation in response: without resorting to debt, they believed Napoleon could not sustain a large-scale war, and so could be more easily toppled and replaced with a monarch receptive to their influence.

### The Louisiana Purchase as an escape hatch

Facing a treasury too depleted to arm an army against a coalition of England, Austria, Prussia, Russia, and Spain, Napoleon solved his funding problem without borrowing — by selling a "vast swamp called Louisiana" to the Americans for roughly £3,000,000.

### A plot to destroy the United States

Griffin recounts a lesser-known episode: when Napoleon threatened to annex Holland (which owed substantial debts to English bankers), the English banker Sir Francis Baring and his Dutch counterpart negotiated a peace proposal in response — but the French financier Ouvrard revised it into something far more ambitious: a joint English-French military conquest and division of the newly independent United States, with the northern states joined to Canada under England and the southern states falling to France, and Napoleon himself tempted with the title "King of America." The plan collapsed only because Napoleon discovered it, arresting Ouvrard and exiling the French police chief Fouché who had approved it.

### Funding Wellington, and Waterloo

With Napoleon denied access to loans, the Bank of England instead created vast new sums of fiat money to fund the coalition armies of Russia, Prussia, and Austria against him. When the Duke of Wellington struggled to pay for his campaign in Portugal and Spain with discounted British promissory notes, Nathan Rothschild personally organized a smuggling operation — routing British gold through France itself, using his sons James (posing as a supporter of Napoleon's government) and Kalmann (coordinating along the Pyrenees) to convert gold into Spanish banknotes and deliver Wellington's funding literally under Napoleon's nose.

Griffin recounts the famous — and, per a 1915 English court ruling he cites, factually vindicated — story of Nathan Rothschild's private courier network beating Wellington's own messenger to London with news of the victory at Waterloo by nearly a full day. Rothschild reportedly stood at his customary pillar in the London Stock Exchange and began *selling* British government bonds, letting the market assume he had received news of a British defeat. As panic selling crashed the price, he reversed and bought the entire market — acquiring, in Griffin's words, "the dominant holding of England's entire debt at but a tiny fraction of its worth."

### The character behind the Formula

Griffin uses Benjamin Disraeli's 1844 novel *Coningsby* — whose financier character Sidonia was, by Disraeli's own admission, modeled directly on Rothschild — to capture the psychological profile he believes underlies the Formula: a man "without affections," to whom "woman was... a toy, man a machine," lending simultaneously to France, Austria, Prussia, and Russia, becoming "lord and master of the money market of the world." Griffin pairs this with a chilling attributed quote: "When the streets of Paris are running with blood, I buy." His summary judgment: "This analytical indifference to human suffering" — cold objectivity, immunity to patriotism, indifference to the human condition — is what makes the Formula's logic *executable* by real people, regardless of whether any of them ever consciously articulated it as a strategy.

## The Formula Itself

Griffin introduces the Formula with a hypothetical figure: a man "totally pragmatic," smarter and more cunning than most, incapable of patriotism, who has observed that kings and politicians are always fighting over something and has concluded that war is both inevitable and profitable. If such a man surveyed history, Griffin argues, he would arrive at five operating principles:

| # | Principle |
|---|---|
| 1 | **War is the ultimate discipline to any government.** If it survives the test of war, it survives; if not, it perishes. All else — the sanctity of its laws, the prosperity of its citizens, the solvency of its treasury — will be sacrificed by any government in its primal act of self-survival. |
| 2 | **War guarantees debt.** All that is necessary to ensure a government maintains or expands its debt is to involve it in war or the threat of war. The greater the threat and the more destructive the war, the greater the need for debt. |
| 3 | **Enemies must exist — and can be manufactured.** To involve a country in war, it needs enemies with credible military strength. If such enemies exist, so much the better. If they exist but lack strength, they must be financed into strength. If no enemy exists at all, a hostile regime must be financed into being. |
| 4 | **Uncooperative governments must be replaced.** The ultimate obstacle is a government that declines to finance its wars through debt. When this happens, internal opposition, insurrection, or revolution must be encouraged to replace it with a more compliant government — assassination of heads of state included, if necessary. |
| 5 | **No side may win decisively — unless the enemy must be destroyed.** No nation can be allowed to remain militarily stronger than its adversaries, since that could lead to peace and a reduction of debt. This may require financing both sides of a conflict, so that neither achieves a decisive victory or defeat. While peace must always be publicly proclaimed as the goal, the unspoken objective is perpetual war.

## Griffin's Explicit Caveat: A Theory, Not a Confession

Griffin is unusually careful, immediately after stating the Formula, to qualify exactly what kind of claim he is making:

> "Whether anyone actually put this strategy into words or passed it along from generation to generation is not important. In fact, it is doubtful it has ever worked that way."

His argument is not that a literal document exists, or that each generation of financiers consciously briefed the next. Rather, he proposes that **shared incentives and a shared personality type** — cold objectivity, immunity to patriotism, indifference to the human condition — are sufficient to produce the same pattern of behavior generation after generation, "whether it is the product of conscious planning or merely the consequence of men responding to the profit opportunities inherent in fiat money." His summary formulation, restated near the end of the chapter: the mechanism of central banking creates "an irresistible temptation to convert debt into perpetual war and war into perpetual debt." This distinction — pattern over proof, incentive over intent — is central to how Griffin uses the Formula throughout the rest of the book: as an interpretive lens applied to documented events, not as an accusation requiring a smoking-gun document.

## Corroborating the Pattern: England's Wars, 1689–1815

Griffin's first test of the Formula is a simple chronology. Counting forward from the founding of the Bank of England (created explicitly to finance a war):

| Years | Conflict |
|---|---|
| 1689–1697 | War of the League of Augsburg |
| 1702–1713 | War of Spanish Succession |
| 1739–1742 | War of Jenkin's Ear |
| 1744–1748 | War of Austrian Succession |
| 1754–1763 | French and Indian War |
| 1793–1801 | War against Revolutionary France |
| 1803–1815 | Napoleonic Wars |

England also fought two wars against America in this period (the War for Independence and the War of 1812). Griffin's tally: in the 126 years between 1689 and 1815, England was at war for 63 of them — "one out of every two years in combat. The others were spent preparing for combat." He concludes: "The mark of the Rothschild Formula is unmistakable in these conflicts. The monetary scientists often were seen financing both sides."

## Case Study: Belgium, 1830

Griffin illustrates that financing is selective and self-interested, not indiscriminate. When Dutch-ruled Belgium faced a revolutionary uprising in 1830, both the ruling government and the revolutionaries sought Rothschild financing. Salomon Rothschild declined to meaningfully fund the rebels, writing: "Our goodwill does not yet extend to the point of putting clubs into the hands that would beat us... lending money to make war and ruin the credit that we sustain with all our efforts." When the revolution was instead settled by negotiation, James Rothschild moved immediately to capture the new Belgian government's finances, writing that the goal was "to make ourselves absolute masters of that country's finances" by cultivating intimacy with its new Finance Minister and acquiring all the treasury bonds he could offer. Griffin calls this "the strategy that has become the model of such operations ever since."

## Case Study: World War I and the Sinking of the *Lusitania*

Griffin devotes an entire chapter (12) to applying the Formula to America's entry into World War I.

**The setup.** When British and French financing needs exceeded what their own central and merchant banks could supply, they turned to the House of Morgan — acting, in Griffin's account, as partners of the Rothschilds — to sell their war bonds in America. Morgan collected commissions on both ends of the transaction: once when the money was borrowed, and again when it was spent on war material Morgan himself often had a financial stake in producing. Total purchases eventually reached roughly $3 billion.

**The souring.** As German U-boats began sinking Allied shipping faster than it could be replaced, Britain came close to being starved into surrender, and Morgan could no longer find American buyers willing to fund new Allied bonds — or to refinance the old ones coming due. A Senate investigation later raised the possibility, which Griffin treats as highly plausible, that the Wilson administration entered the war in part because American bankers needed to protect their Allied loans from default.

**The dispensable ship.** Griffin notes that Morgan had built an international shipping trust that competed directly with Cunard, the British line that owned the *Lusitania* — making the ship, from Morgan's perspective, "quite dispensable." The *Lusitania* had in fact been built to British Admiralty military specifications, capable of conversion to an armed cruiser, and by the time of her final voyage she had been reclassified in the Admiralty fleet register not as a passenger liner but as an armed auxiliary cruiser, her lower decks converted to carry munitions rather than passenger cabins. On May 7, 1915, she was sunk by a German U-boat off the coast of Ireland, killing 1,195 people, 195 of them American. Griffin presents this event as the single moment that turned American public opinion from opposition to acceptance of war — an outcome that happened to align precisely with the financial interests of the bankers whose loans were at risk.

## Case Study: The Russian Revolution

In Chapters 13–14 ("Masquerade in Moscow" and "The Best Enemy Money Can Buy"), Griffin extends the Formula to the Bolshevik Revolution, arguing that New York financiers — operating under cover of a Red Cross mission — provided support to the Bolshevik faction. He supplies a strikingly literal example of Formula point 5 (financing both sides): Tsar Nicholas II accepted loans from J.P. Morgan even as, per Griffin, "Morgan also funded the Mensheviks and the Bolsheviks." The Mensheviks subsequently forced the Tsar's abdication, and the Bolsheviks later executed him — the same financial house, by Griffin's account, having backed both the regime and the revolutionaries who destroyed it.

## Case Study: The Cold War — "The Best Enemy Money Can Buy"

Griffin argues the Formula continued operating well into the twentieth century, citing historian Antony Sutton's research (which gives Chapter 14 its title) that American-subsidized Soviet industry supplied the weapons, ammunition, and transport that killed American soldiers in Korea and Vietnam — including trucks manufactured in American-built plants running the Ho Chi Minh Trail. He quotes Navy Secretary John Lehman telling Annapolis graduates in 1983 that some of the most advanced American military technology they would encounter would be on Soviet ships.

### Saddam Hussein as "the best enemy money can buy"

Griffin extends the pattern to the Gulf War, noting that Saddam Hussein's regime was deliberately contained rather than destroyed — his biological-weapons facilities largely spared, his helicopter gunships left intact. He cites CFR *Foreign Affairs* managing editor Fareed Zakaria's 1996 rationalization as, in his words, "about as clear a statement of the Rothschild Formula as one is apt to find":

> "If Saddam Hussein did not exist, we would have to invent him... Nothing destroys an alliance like the disappearance of the enemy."

Griffin also quotes Congressman James Traficant's 1997 floor speech describing American money funding Russian missile production, which was then sold to China, which sold the missiles to Iran, which then required more American troops and dollars to counter the resulting threat — Traficant calling it "not foreign policy... foreign stupidity." Griffin's response: "Traficant is on target with his analysis of the problem, but he missed the bull's eye regarding the cause. American leaders are not stupid. They merely are implementing the Rothschild Formula."

## Beyond War: The Formula Applied to Domestic Politics

Griffin does not confine the Formula strictly to international war financing — he applies its underlying logic to domestic political manipulation as well.

- **Nicholas Biddle and the Second Bank of the United States.** Griffin writes that, "following the Rothschild Formula," Biddle secured control over Congress by rewarding compliant politicians with financial favors — even Senator Daniel Webster reportedly kept the Bank's retainer as legal counsel while serving in the Senate. Here the Formula's logic (buy loyalty through financial dependency) operates without any war at all, applied instead to capturing legislative behavior. (See "The Federal Reserve System" for the fuller Biddle-Jackson narrative.)
- **Salmon Chase and the Civil War greenbacks.** Griffin directly invokes Formula point 1 — that "the sanctity of its laws, the prosperity of its citizens, and the solvency of its treasury will be quickly sacrificed by any government in its primal act of self-survival" — to explain Treasury Secretary Salmon Chase's about-face on fiat greenbacks: he championed them as an "indispensable necessity" during the Civil War, then declared them unconstitutional eight years later as Chief Justice, once the nation was again at peace.

## How the Formula Connects to Griffin's Other Core Concepts

| Concept | Its relationship to the Rothschild Formula |
|---|---|
| **Fiat Money** | The Formula only functions where governments can create money without direct taxation; fiat money is the fuel that makes perpetual war financially survivable. |
| **The Mandrake Mechanism** | The specific technical process — buying government debt with newly created money — by which the Formula's war debt is actually manufactured. |
| **The Bank of England** | Griffin treats the Bank's 1694 founding as the starting point from which the modern era of Formula-style war financing becomes possible; see the related note for the full lineage. |
| **The Federal Reserve System** | The twentieth-century American vehicle through which Griffin argues the Formula has continued to operate — funding World War I, World War II, Korea, and Vietnam. |
| **Government Debt** | The Formula's central objective: not war for its own sake, but war as the most reliable generator of permanent, compounding government debt. |
| **Usury** | The profit motive underlying the Formula — interest collected on money created for war that was never earned, saved, or actually possessed by the lender. |
| **War Finance** | The Formula is, in essence, Griffin's general theory of why and how wars get financed once central banking exists as an option. |
| **Political Power / the CFR** | Griffin connects the Formula's modern application (Saddam Hussein, the Cold War) directly to the Council on Foreign Relations network, framing perpetual controlled conflict as a tool for building the "New World Order" discussed elsewhere in the book. |

## Griffin's Conclusion

Griffin does not claim to have proven the Rothschild Formula as documented policy — he is explicit that it is a hypothesis built from observed patterns, personality traits, and structural incentives rather than a discovered manifesto. But he argues the historical record, from the Bank of England's founding wars through the Gulf War, behaves exactly as the Formula predicts it would: credible enemies are financed into existence when none exist, governments that resist debt-financed war are pressured or replaced, no conflict is permitted to end in decisive victory, and the same financial houses profit whichever side prevails. His closing formulation for the chapter distills the entire theory into a single reversible phrase: the temptation, wherever central banking exists, is to convert **debt into perpetual war, and war into perpetual debt.**
