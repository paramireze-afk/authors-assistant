# Israel Is Not Judaism

### A written adaptation of the *Voice of Rabbis* podcast, Episode 1, with Mosha Silverstein and Rabbi Yaakov Shapiro

---

## 1. Introduction: What Voice of Rabbis Is, and Why This Episode Exists

The episode opens with host Mosha Silverstein introducing a new organization, Voice of Rabbis, Inc., and its companion website. He explains that Voice of Rabbis is best known for an X (Twitter) account with close to 300,000 followers, active since 2022, which has recently been acquired by an extended Satmar family — Satmar being a Hasidic movement founded in the tradition of Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum. Silverstein frames the whole project as a vehicle for continuing Rabbi Teitelbaum's teaching.

He addresses a controversy directly and briefly: reports that the X account is partly run by workers based in the Philippines. His explanation is that the account's owners employ Philippines-based staff to help with the volume of day-to-day writing and activity, which is why some posts show Philippine IP addresses — a mundane staffing explanation, offered to close the subject before moving on.

Silverstein then states the occasion for launching this podcast now: the sharp rise in antisemitism since the war in Gaza began, which he describes as the worst in generations. Voice of Rabbis, he says, was created in response to this emergency. His first guest is Rabbi Yaakov Shapiro, whom he introduces as the author of *The Empty Wagon*, a major work of Orthodox anti-Zionist argument.

Before the interview proper begins, Shapiro gently corrects one detail in Silverstein's introduction. Silverstein had recalled Rabbi Teitelbaum using the phrase *mesirat nefesh* — literally "handing over the soul," commonly translated as self-sacrifice — to describe the obligation to publicly separate Judaism from Zionism, and had wondered aloud whether the Rebbe meant this literally, as a matter one should be willing to die for. Shapiro doubts that reading: in his understanding, the phrase more likely conveyed the seriousness of the effort required — comparable to how English speakers use "self-sacrifice" to mean strenuous devotion, not literal martyrdom — rather than a formal religious ruling that a Jew is obligated to give his life for the cause. This small correction sets the tone for the rest of the conversation: strong claims delivered with real care about precision.

## 2. Defining Zionism: The Argument's Starting Point

Everything Shapiro says in this episode flows from a single definitional claim, which he states early and returns to repeatedly: **Zionism is the belief that the Jewish people constitute a political nationality — comparable to French, Japanese, or Italian nationality — and that the State of Israel is the nation-state of that nationality.**

He is careful to distinguish this from a much softer, more familiar idea: that Israel is simply a state whose population happens to be majority Jewish, the way France is majority French. That familiar idea, he argues, is not actually what Zionism claims, and it is not what Israeli law actually says. Zionism's real claim, in his account, is stronger and stranger: that *all* Jews everywhere — whether or not they are Israeli citizens, whether or not they have ever set foot in Israel, whether or not they want anything to do with it — belong to this Jewish nationality, and that Israel is *their* country, in the same structural sense that France is the country of a French citizen.

To make this vivid, Shapiro draws a repeated comparison. Every other country in the world, he says, represents only its own citizens or "alumni." A French person who becomes an American citizen owes no ongoing political loyalty to France; France makes no claim on them. But Israel, on Shapiro's reading of Zionist ideology and Israeli law, is different: it claims a Jew born in Poland, raised in America, who has never been to Israel and wants nothing to do with it, is nonetheless *nationally* connected to Israel — that Israel is, in some real sense, "his country" too, whether he agrees or not.

This is the seed from which the rest of the episode grows: if that claim is true, Shapiro argues, then enormous and largely unnoticed consequences follow for how Jews everywhere are perceived and treated. If it is false — if it is a modern ideological invention rather than a description of what Judaism actually is — then a great deal of what passes for "supporting Israel" or "criticizing Israel" among Jews rests on a category error that Zionism itself created.

## 3. Judaism as Covenant, Not Nationality

Before Shapiro can argue that Zionism misrepresents Judaism, he lays out what he holds Judaism actually to be: not a nationality, but a religion built on covenant.

He offers a plain analogy using a real-world puzzle: if Judaism is simply a religion, why do we speak of "non-religious Jews" — people who don't believe or practice, yet are still considered fully Jewish? In Christianity, he notes, a person who doesn't believe in Christianity is not a Christian. In Islam, a person who doesn't believe in Islam is not a Muslim. So why is a secular, nonobservant person still unambiguously a Jew?

Shapiro's answer rests on a distinction between two covenants in Jewish tradition:

- **The Noahide covenant**, given (per tradition) to Noah and, through him, to all of humanity. It consists of seven commandments — prohibitions on murder, theft, idolatry, and so on, along with a requirement to establish courts of justice — binding on everyone, Jew and non-Jew alike.
- **The Mosaic covenant**, given specifically at Mount Sinai, some 800 years later by Shapiro's telling, to those who were present and their descendants. This covenant consists of the Torah's 613 commandments and applies only to Jews.

A "non-religious Jew," in this framework, is not someone who has opted out of a religion the way a lapsed Christian might. It's someone who remains *obligated* under the Mosaic covenant — bound to it by birth and history — whether or not they personally choose to fulfill that obligation. Shapiro introduces the term *kofer* (plural *kofrim*) for such a person within this framework, and notes, almost in passing, that Islam has a linguistically related term, *kafir* — offered as an observation about shared vocabulary, not a claim that the concepts work identically in both traditions.

He pushes the point further with what he clearly considers a provocative but sincere theological claim: a non-Jew who keeps the seven Noahide laws is, in his assessment as a rabbi, closer to God than a Jew who has abandoned belief and practice entirely — closer, he says pointedly, "than a godless heretic like Theodor Herzl." Judaism, he says, is a meritocracy of the soul: a Jew can be born with what tradition calls a holy soul, like Moses, or can, through choices and beliefs, take on what tradition describes as a demonic one. What a person is born as, in other words, does not settle what they become.

From here Shapiro moves to a linguistic argument meant to head off an obvious Zionist counter-claim: doesn't the Bible itself call the Jews a nation — an *am* — which is exactly the word for a political people? Shapiro says this equivocates. The Hebrew word *am*, he argues, doesn't carry the modern, specifically political sense of "nation-state" that Zionists want it to carry. He offers two illustrations from Jewish sources: the Talmud describes women collectively as "their own" group using similar collective language, and the Book of Joel describes a swarm of locusts as a "nation" — obviously not a political entity, but a unified mass. *Am*, on his reading, means a unified group in the way "the elderly" or "a crowd" is a group — not a nation-state in the nineteenth- and twentieth-century political sense. There was, he adds, no such concept as a political nation-state at the time the Torah was given; Jews at that point were "subjects of kings," not citizens of anything resembling a modern polity.

**A note on where this section sits methodologically:** everything above is Shapiro's theology, offered as his sincere religious position, not a claim meant to be checked against outside sources the way a court case would be.

## 4. Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, the Six-Day War, and the Origin of Satmar's Public Campaign

With the theological foundation laid, Silverstein returns to the story that opened the episode: Rabbi Teitelbaum's reaction to the Six-Day War in June 1967.

As Silverstein tells it, just hours after Israel's victory — while much of the Jewish world was celebrating — Rabbi Teitelbaum was, by contrast, in tears. Speaking at a *shalashudes* (the third Sabbath meal, a traditional setting for Hasidic teaching, held as the Sabbath draws to a close), he reportedly told his followers they had no idea what terrible consequences this victory would bring — not only for the wider world, but specifically for the Jewish people. Shapiro affirms this account and adds a striking detail: everything that has followed — every political and military entanglement Israel has faced involving Gaza and the West Bank — flows, in his and Silverstein's shared reading, from that war. Had Israel not attacked Egypt in 1967, they argue, Israel, the Jewish people, and arguably the whole world would be better off today.

Out of that moment, according to Silverstein, came a specific instruction from Rabbi Teitelbaum: that someone should speak *in English* at Satmar's protests, so that the wider world — not just the Yiddish-speaking Satmar community — could understand the core message: **that Israel does not represent the Jews.** Satmar is, as both speakers acknowledge, an unusually insular community that does not typically permit English-language speeches even internally (Shapiro notes with some humor that he is not even allowed to address his own congregation in English, despite it being his first language — Yiddish, learned formally only in the tenth grade, functions as something closer to his third language). The decision to make an exception for anti-Zionist protest messaging shows how important Rabbi Teitelbaum considered this specific communication to be.

This is also where Shapiro clarifies something that matters a great deal to how the rest of the episode should be understood: **Satmar's anti-Zionist protests are not the same thing as pro-Palestinian protests**, even when the two sometimes happen near each other geographically. Satmar's protest is not "Israel is committing genocide" or "Israel is an apartheid state" — those are political-military judgments Shapiro says he has no more personal obligation to render than he does about Ukraine, Russia, or China. The Satmar protest is narrower and, in the speakers' view, prior to any of that: it is the claim that **Israel does not speak for Judaism, and being Jewish does not make a person responsible for what Israel does.**

He illustrates the distinction with a rule he says he applies personally: if a stranger asks him what he thinks about the war in Ukraine, he might have an opinion, offered as an ordinary citizen with a view — the same way anyone might have an opinion about Russia or North Korea. But if a journalist asks him, specifically *because he is Jewish*, to weigh in on Israeli government policy, he refuses on principle — not because he lacks an opinion, but because answering would concede the very premise he is trying to dismantle: that being Jewish creates an inherent connection to Israel's conduct. "Am I Israeli?" he says he asks such reporters. "No." "Am I Palestinian?" "No." "Then why are you asking me?"

He recalls the Satmar Rebbe himself making the same point about the underlying land dispute, asked once who rightfully owned the disputed territory, Israel or the Palestinians: "I have as much to do with that as who owns the English Channel, England or France." The comparison is not a judgment on the merits of the conflict — it is a statement that, as a Jew observing Jewish law, the dispute is simply not his to adjudicate.

## 5. How Zionism Reinterpreted Jewish History and Identity

Having distinguished Judaism-as-covenant from Zionism-as-nationality, Shapiro turns to intellectual history: how, concretely, did this reinterpretation happen?

He points first to the nineteenth-century historian **Heinrich Graetz**, author of a monumental history of the Jewish people. Reading from *The Making of Modern Zionism*, a scholarly study by the Israeli political scientist **Shlomo Avineri**, Shapiro presents Graetz — a figure not usually thought of as a political Zionist — as one of the movement's real intellectual originators. The passage he reads describes Graetz's "revolutionary" contribution: treating the Jews as "a people, a nation, not just a community of faith," with a "political content" bound up in Jewish messianism and tied to the land of Palestine. Graetz's historical writing, according to the passage, took biblical figures who had functioned in Jewish memory as religious heroes and "revived" them through a process of secularization and romanticization — turning them, in effect, into national heroes instead.

Shapiro's own gloss on this is blunt: the Zionists, he says, didn't just add a political layer on top of Judaism — they "tore out the guts of Judaism" and "stuffed" a hollowed-out religious shell with nationalist content instead. Wherever Judaism had a sacred concept, Zionism substituted a secular-national one: the Holy Land became a political homeland; the Holy Language (Hebrew, reserved traditionally for prayer and study) became an everyday national vernacular; and the age-old liturgical yearning "next year in Jerusalem" — traditionally understood as a prayer for the messianic future, when a resurrected King David would rule in an age of universal peace — became, in Zionist hands, a call for ordinary political self-determination in the present.

From Graetz, Shapiro moves to **Theodor Herzl**, whose 1896 pamphlet *The Jewish State* he credits with a specific and, in his telling, telling legal move: Herzl claimed the Zionist movement had something like a *legal* right to speak on behalf of Jews everywhere, based on an old Roman-law concept Shapiro calls *negotiorum gestio* — the principle that someone may lawfully manage an absent or unrepresented party's affairs without being asked. His illustration: if you find an unattended, endangered piece of livestock on a ship, you may step in and act as its manager on the absent owner's behalf. Since the Jewish people, in Herzl's framing, had no one looking after their collective interests, the Zionists appointed themselves as *gestors* — caretakers — with the standing to act and speak for world Jewry. Shapiro traces a direct line from this posture through Ben-Gurion, Moshe Dayan, and Golda Meir, up to Benjamin Netanyahu — noting, for instance, that when Netanyahu addressed the U.S. Congress during the Obama administration, he explicitly said he was speaking not only for Israelis who voted for him but "for all the Jews all over the world."

Then comes **Vladimir (Ze'ev) Jabotinsky**, founder of Revisionist Zionism — the direct ideological ancestor, Shapiro notes, of the political tradition that produced today's Likud, and personally significant to Netanyahu, whose father Benzion served as Jabotinsky's secretary and protégé. Jabotinsky is, for Shapiro, the figure who most starkly reveals what the Zionist project was actually trying to build, because Jabotinsky said it openly. In a 1905 tribute written on Herzl's death, Jabotinsky set out to describe the ideal "Hebrew" — the new man Zionism aimed to produce — by taking the *Yid* (a Yiddish/Russian term for "Jew," often used pejoratively) and imagining his precise opposite. Shapiro reads the passage at length: because the *Yid* is "ugly, sickly, and lacks handsomeness," the Hebrew ideal would be endowed with "masculine beauty, stature, massive shoulders, vigorous movements." Where the *Yid* is "frightened and downtrodden," the Hebrew "ought to be proud and independent." Where the *Yid* "has accepted submission," the Hebrew "ought to know how to command." Where the *Yid* hides from strangers' eyes, the Hebrew should look the world "straight and deep into their eyes" and declare his identity with pride.

Shapiro's point in quoting this at length is not merely rhetorical. He treats it as proof, in the founders' own words, that Zionism was never simply a plan to give persecuted Jews a state — it was, from very early on, an explicit project to *change what a Jew was*, psychologically and even physically, because the architects of Zionism themselves regarded the traditional Jewish character as something shameful that needed replacing. He connects this directly to a claim he attributes to early Zionist writers (naming, as one instance, the phrase he associates with "Greenberg"): that the reason people hate Jews is, in effect, that Jews deserve it as currently constituted — and that fixing antisemitism therefore meant *changing the Jews*, not defending them as they were.

Finally, Shapiro brings in **Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook**, first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandatory Palestine, as the figure who broke what had been near-total religious resistance to this nationalist reframing. Even the religious-Zionist Mizrachi movement, Shapiro says — Orthodox Jews willing to cooperate practically with the Zionist project — had originally rejected the underlying theological premise that Jewish nationhood was a legitimate category, regarding it as a form of idolatry. It was Rabbi Kook's theological reworking, according to Shapiro, that opened the door to full religious Zionism, effectively normalizing within observant circles an idea the tradition had, in Shapiro's account, previously and near-universally rejected.

## 6. Israeli Law: How the State Defines Itself, in Its Own Words

At this point in the conversation, Shapiro shifts from history and theology to law — and insists that what he's about to say isn't his own interpretation of Zionism, but Zionism's interpretation of itself, stated in Israel's own statutes and court rulings.

He starts with the **2018 Nation-State Law** (formally, Basic Law: Israel — The Nation-State of the Jewish People). Its content, as Shapiro summarizes it, is that Israel is "the nation state of the Jews," meaning specifically Jews — not Israelis generally. Under this law, Israeli citizenship is one thing; "nationality" is a separate legal category, and only Jews hold the Jewish nationality that carries the constitutionally protected right to self-determination in Israel. A Christian, Muslim, or Hindu Israeli citizen has full individual civil rights, Shapiro allows, but does not have national self-determination rights in the country the way a Jewish citizen does.

This produces a strange bureaucratic fact Shapiro dwells on: Israel divides its population by three separate categories — religion, citizenship, and something called *le'om* (nationality) — and there is no "Israeli" nationality category at all. There are, he says, over a hundred recognized nationalities registered in Israel: Jewish, Arab, Druze, and many others, but not "Israeli." He notes that Israeli passports do list "Israel" under nationality for international purposes, purely for practical reasons — showing "Jewish" on a passport at a foreign airport would only cause confusion — but internally, within the state's own population registry, there is no such thing as an Israeli national. Only a Jewish one, an Arab one, and so on.

He quotes directly from a 2014 article on the Israeli Ministry of Foreign Affairs website, written by **Avigdor Lieberman**, then serving in a senior government role: "The state of Israel is the national state of the Jewish people. It has equal individual rights for every citizen... But only the Jewish people have national rights" — a flag, an anthem, the right of every Jew worldwide to immigrate, and other national symbols, which the article states are granted only "to our people in its one and only state." Lieberman's own formula, as Shapiro quotes it, mirrors his own analogy precisely: "What Japan is to the Japanese... and France is to the French, Israel is to the Jews." Shapiro notes that Danny Ayalon and Benjamin Netanyahu have made essentially the same argument in their own writing — Netanyahu explicitly framing anti-Zionism as antisemitism on the logic that just as one cannot claim to support French identity while opposing France's existence, one cannot support Jewish identity while opposing Israel's.

Shapiro then turns to a court case that, in his telling, settles the matter beyond argument: a group of Israeli citizens — the majority of them Jewish — sued for the right to be registered under an "Israeli" nationality rather than their existing ethnic/religious categories. The case went to the Israeli Supreme Court, decided by a three-judge panel. The court rejected the appeal. Shapiro reads directly from the ruling: the appellants had argued that treating "world Jewry" as belonging to a single Jewish political nation was incoherent, since Jews everywhere already hold the nationality of whatever country they're citizens of. The court disagreed, and the presiding judge's language is, for Shapiro, the crucial sentence: "the conception that Judaism is not only a religious affiliation but also a national affiliation is the foundation stone of Zionism." The court went further, reasoning that if Israeli citizens were permitted to register as "Israeli" rather than "Jewish," this could fragment the unity of the Jewish nation as a whole and undermine the very basis on which Zionism claims a right to national self-determination in the first place — because, as the presiding judge put it, that right belongs to *nations*, not to fragments of nations.

Shapiro draws out the implication with evident relish: this means, according to Israel's own highest court, a person cannot simultaneously be a national of Israel *and* a national of another country — you are either Jewish by nationality, in which case Israel is your country, or you are American (or French, or whatever) by nationality, in which case it is not. He turns this into a direct question aimed at Diaspora Jews: are you American by nationality, or Jewish by nationality? If the latter — which is exactly what Zionism, by its own legal logic, requires you to be — then America, according to that same logic, is not really your country at all.

He caps this section with the **1952 Status Law**, which formalized the relationship between the State of Israel and the World Zionist Organization and Jewish Agency. He reads (from memory, he notes, rather than a document in hand at that moment) what he describes as Article One: the state "regards itself as the creation of the entire Jewish people" — not merely of the Zionists who built it. And Article Five, he says, commits Israel and the Zionist movement to the "ingathering of the exiles" — meaning the immigration of all Jews to Israel — as "the central task... in our days," requiring "constant effort... by the Jewish people in the diaspora," meaning, he stresses, all of us, wherever we live. The law, on his reading, expects cooperation from all Jews worldwide "as individuals and groups" in this project by law, not merely as a matter of political sentiment.

He adds one more historical citation, attributed to Ben-Gurion in 1951: that Zionist organizations bear a "collective duty" to assist the State of Israel "in all conditions and under all circumstances," and — in the phrase Shapiro treats as the crux — "whether the government to which the Jews in question owe allegiance desire it or not." In other words, Diaspora Jews' obligation to Israel, on this account, does not yield even to the wishes of their own home governments. Shapiro contrasts this with a historical example of legitimate dissent he clearly admires: British Jews who, in the 1930s, openly and lawfully opposed their own government's anti-Zionist "White Paper" policy restricting Jewish immigration to Palestine. Those Jews, he notes, were ordinary English citizens exercising ordinary political dissent within their own polity — nobody accused them of disloyalty to Britain for it, because their disagreement was a normal act of citizenship, not an act of allegiance to a foreign power. What Ben-Gurion's formula describes, by contrast, is something categorically different: an expectation of obligation to Israel that holds regardless of, and potentially in conflict with, the will of one's own country.

"There's one word for that," Silverstein says. Shapiro agrees, and names it directly: this is dual loyalty. And dual loyalty, when demanded of an entire people rather than chosen individually, is — in the framework the two of them build across the rest of the episode — a textbook definition of antisemitism.

## 7. Dual Loyalty, Antisemitism, and What Happens to Diaspora Jews

This is where the legal argument turns into the episode's central political claim, and Shapiro walks through it as a chain of reasoning.

Start with an ordinary case, he says. If the United States bombs Afghanistan, angry Afghans direct their anger at Americans — not, typically, at every person of American descent living anywhere in the world regardless of citizenship. If Japan bombs the United States, Americans are angry at Japan, not at every ethnically Japanese person globally. Even in uglier, criminal cases — people assaulting Chinese-Americans over COVID-19's origins in China, which Shapiro condemns explicitly as criminal — the underlying logic of blame still runs along lines of citizenship and nationality, however wrongly it's sometimes misapplied to innocent individuals.

Israel, Shapiro argues, breaks this pattern — not because of anything Gazans or the wider world do wrong on their own initiative, but because Israel itself insists, legally and rhetorically, that it represents not just its own citizens but Jews everywhere. So when Israel bombs Gaza, the people angry at that action are, per Israel's own self-definition, not just angry at Israelis — they are, by the logic Israel itself has built, entitled to be angry at Jews as such. "They should be angry at the Israelis," Shapiro says, "but they won't. They'll be angry at the Jews." The distinction that would normally protect ordinary diaspora members of a nation from blame for that nation's specific state actions simply doesn't hold here, because Israel has structurally erased the distinction between "Israeli" and "Jew" that every other country maintains between its citizens and unrelated members of a shared ethnicity or religion abroad.

From here, Shapiro reframes the familiar antisemitic trope of "dual loyalty" — the accusation that Jews are secretly more loyal to a foreign power (historically leveled against Jews with no basis at all) — with an uncomfortable twist: in the case of Zionism specifically, he argues, the dual-loyalty claim isn't a paranoid fantasy invented by antisemites. It is, he says, *literally Zionism's own stated legal and ideological position* — as demonstrated by everything just covered in Section 6. This produces what Shapiro treats as one of the strangest and most under-examined features of the whole situation: Zionists and antisemites, from radically different motives, end up agreeing on the same underlying premise — that Jews are collectively responsible for and connected to Israel. He offers a striking illustration: the white-nationalist figure Richard Spencer once described himself, in an Israeli television interview, as a "white Zionist," telling Jewish Israelis he respected their ethnostate and wanted the same exclusivity for white Americans. Antisemites, Shapiro says, *love* Zionists for exactly this reason — Zionism confirms, in the antisemite's own terms, that Jews really do belong to a separate nation with a separate homeland, and really are collectively answerable for what that nation-state does.

He extends this into a broader, more provocative claim about the psychology of manipulation and contempt: people who go along uncritically with what they're told — his examples are people who supported COVID-19 vaccination under public pressure, or who supported Trump financially and politically without scrutiny — are not respected by the very people manipulating them, but quietly despised for their compliance. He applies the same logic to Jews who unconditionally support Israel: he suggests such unconditional support isn't rewarded with respect by those orchestrating it, historical or political, but is, in his view, met with contempt for its ease.

The practical consequence Shapiro keeps returning to is reputational and material, not merely rhetorical: American Jews, he argues, are perceived as *less* fully American than their neighbors — Christian, Muslim, Buddhist, whatever — specifically because of the widespread assumption, encouraged by Zionist rhetoric and institutions (he mentions synagogues that sell Israeli land, institutions flying Israeli flags, neighborhoods hanging Israeli flags publicly), that Jews are nationally tied to a foreign country. He cites, without providing the underlying figures himself, a general pattern tracked by Tel Aviv University's Kantor Center for the Study of Contemporary European Jewry (referred to in the episode simply as the "Kantor Center for Antisemitism"): that antisemitic incidents rise measurably whenever Israel takes actions perceived unfavorably abroad — which he treats as direct, empirical confirmation of the causal chain he's describing.

## 8. Traditional Jewish Virtue Versus the "New Jew"

Returning to the cultural argument opened in Section 5, Shapiro develops the Jabotinsky material into a fuller contrast between what he holds up as classical rabbinic ideals and what Zionism, in his account, deliberately replaced them with.

He names three traditional aspirational Jewish character traits, rendered in Hebrew: **rachmanim** (merciful, compassionate), **bayshanim** (roughly, capable of feeling shame — the opposite of shameless), and a third built around the idea of *nadiv*, generous giving tempered by kindness. These, Shapiro says, are what the tradition asks a Jew to aspire toward.

He sets this against the **Betar anthem**, written by Jabotinsky for his Revisionist Zionist youth movement, which Shapiro partially recites: the movement rising "from a pit of dust and decay," through "blood and sweat," into a "new race" that is — and Shapiro lands hard on this specific word — "proud, generous, and *cruel*." The word for cruel in the original, he notes, is the direct antonym of the traditional value of mercy. This is not incidental wordplay, in his reading, but the point: Jabotinsky knew traditional rabbinic vocabulary well enough to invert it deliberately, term by term, producing an anthem whose ideal Jewish youth is explicitly and proudly the opposite of what the tradition asks for.

To make the contrast concrete, Shapiro turns to King David — a figure both Zionists and traditional Jews claim as their own, but read very differently. He cites Psalm 27, in which David, a king with an army and every reason to ask God for victory, treasure, or the heads of his enemies, instead asks for exactly one thing: to dwell in God's house, in the study hall, all the days of his life, and to bask in God's presence. This, Shapiro says, is the actual theme of Judaism — a request nearly every Jewish child and adult knows, set to a half-dozen familiar melodies. The Zionist youth movement, by contrast, has its own songs about conquest and crowns and dying-or-conquering the mountain. "This," Shapiro says, "is the difference between Jewish youth and Zionist youth."

He connects this directly to a live, painful contemporary issue: the resistance among ultra-Orthodox ("Haredi" or, in the episode's terms, "Karedi") communities in Israel to serving in the Israeli military. The objection, per Shapiro, isn't primarily about physical danger or religious exemption for its own sake — it's that the army functions, deliberately, as an instrument for reshaping Jewish character along exactly the Jabotinsky-style lines just described: teaching people to want to fight, to want to win, to become, in effect, "tough guys," which Shapiro frames as tantamount to a kind of forced religious conversion. He references, with visible discomfort, a real incident: a 14-year-old boy killed by a bus during a protest against military conscription, whose father said publicly that he would rather his son had died that way than gone into the army. Shapiro doesn't offer this as something to be celebrated, but as evidence of how seriously this community takes the stakes of the conscription fight — as, in their eyes, nothing less than an attempt at forced religious and cultural conversion.

## 9. Orthodox Rabbinic Disagreement: Strategy After 1948

Shapiro is careful throughout to distinguish two different, sometimes conflated questions: whether Zionism is theologically legitimate (on which, he argues, virtually all pre-state Orthodox authorities agreed — it was not) and, once the State of Israel existed as a fact on the ground, how anti-Zionist communities should relate to it politically and practically. On that second question, real disagreement opened up among people who agreed entirely on the first.

He walks through the internal Orthodox debate over whether anti-Zionist parties could participate in the Israeli Knesset (parliament) without thereby legitimizing Zionism. One rabbinic position — which Shapiro attributes to a Rabbi Grozovski — held that a parliament, unlike the Zionist movement itself, is not an inherently ideological institution: it represents the actual population of a country, including people who reject the state's founding ideology outright, the same way Arab members sit in the Knesset today without anyone mistaking their presence for an endorsement of Zionism. Some anti-Zionist Orthodox factions accepted this reasoning and entered practical politics, subject to conditions — chief among them, an ongoing public obligation to keep declaring their anti-Zionism even while sitting in the government's institutions.

Rabbi Teitelbaum rejected this path, for two stated reasons Shapiro reconstructs carefully. First, on pure experience: conditions attached to this kind of permission tend to survive as formal justifications long after the underlying commitments quietly disappear — and indeed, Shapiro notes, that's roughly what happened. Second, and more fundamentally: in a democracy, Teitelbaum argued, the government is not separate from the people the way it is under a monarchy or dictatorship — the population *is*, in an important sense, part of the government, since elected officials function as the people's agents. If the state's existence is itself prohibited, participating in its governing institutions cannot be a neutral act, regardless of one's stated intentions. Shapiro brings in a Jewish legal analogy to sharpen the point: if three witnesses testify falsely to convict someone, all three bear responsibility equally, even if one could argue "he would have been convicted anyway without me" — complicity isn't diluted by redundancy. On this reasoning, joining the Knesset is prohibited under Jewish law regardless of whether it's also, in Teitelbaum's separate assessment, strategically counterproductive (which he believed it was).

Shapiro is emphatic that this was a genuine, respectful disagreement about strategy, not a dispute about the underlying anti-Zionist conviction itself — he stresses that the position of parties like Agudath Israel, who ultimately did not join the government along these lines, explicitly denied being "any less anti-Zionist" than Satmar or Neturei Karta, comparing themselves again to the Arab Knesset members whose participation doesn't imply ideological agreement.

He situates this whole debate against the broader historical backdrop that virtually the entire pre-state Orthodox rabbinic world — with only a small number of exceptions — regarded the core Zionist idea (that Jews constitute a political nationality) as, in his words, heresy and idolatry. Even the religious-Zionist Mizrachi movement, before Rabbi Kook's reworking of the theology, held this same objection, agreeing to practical cooperation with the Zionist project while still formally rejecting its underlying ideological premise.

---

## 10. Anti-Zionism and the "Fringe" Accusation

Both speakers push back, at some length, against a framing they say gets applied to them by Zionists and antisemites alike: that anti-Zionist Orthodox Jews represent a tiny, marginal fringe.

Shapiro's argument for scale rests on institutional size rather than headcount surveys: he describes the largest Jewish educational institution in the world, and what he calls (borrowing loosely from Christian vocabulary, for the benefit of listeners unfamiliar with the term "congregation" in this context) the single largest Jewish congregation in the world, as both strongly anti-Zionist — and notes that this same institution recently prohibited its own leadership from joining any Zionist organization, "regardless of the benefits," on the grounds that doing so is a serious violation of Jewish law. He extends this to Agudath Israel's own rabbinic council, which likewise prohibits its members from involvement with Zionist organizations specifically, even while it does not go as far as Satmar in prohibiting all forms of state engagement.

He recounts, as a personal anecdote, a recent conversation (or "debate," as he describes it) with an AI chatbot — the transcript names "Grok" — in which the system initially characterized anti-Zionist Jews as a fringe position. When Shapiro pressed it and demonstrated this wasn't accurate, the system reportedly acknowledged that its answer simply reflected whatever content was most prevalent online, regardless of whether that content was true — which Shapiro treats as a small but telling illustration of how the "fringe" narrative gets manufactured and repeated without real scrutiny.

He also recalls, more personally, having led anti-Zionist protests in front of the Israeli consulate in New York for roughly thirty years, joking that there's footage of him at the same spot decades ago, beard fully black, not yet gray. The point of the anecdote is persistence: this is not a new or opportunistic position adopted in response to recent events, but a decades-long, continuous public stance.

---

## 11. Hasbara and the Strategy of Redirection

Shapiro introduces a term — **hasbara**, Hebrew for "explanation," used to describe organized pro-Israel public advocacy — and describes what he calls its central tactical rule: never defend Israel's specific conduct point by point; instead, redirect any criticism onto the critic. Why are you criticizing Israel specifically, when so many other countries do worse? Why are you so obsessed with such a small country? The implication offered to the critic, per Shapiro, is that scrutiny itself is suspect — evidence of bias or antisemitism — rather than a subject requiring a substantive answer.

He connects this to the "three D's" test associated with Natan Sharansky (rendered in the episode by his Soviet-era name, Anatoly Sharansky): the idea that criticism of Israel becomes antisemitic when it demonizes, applies a double standard to, or delegitimizes the Jewish state. Shapiro doesn't dispute that such a test could, in principle, be applied fairly — but he treats it, in practice, as a mechanism reliably deployed to reclassify inconvenient questions as bigotry rather than answer them.

He offers military language to describe the underlying strategy: Israel, he says, cannot afford to fight its literal wars on its own small territory — hence wars are fought in Gaza, Lebanon, and Iran rather than inside Israel itself. The rhetorical "war of words" about Israel's own character and conduct works the same way, in his account: never let the argument be fought on Israel's home ground (whether Israel's actions are just), because that ground is where Israel is most exposed. Instead, always move the battlefield onto the critic — is *he* an antisemite, is *she* consistent, does the critic single Israel out unfairly. He states plainly that this strategy is formally taught, citing a specific document as evidence.

That document is the ***Hasbara Handbook***, published by the World Union of Jewish Students around 2002 (roughly a quarter-century before this recording, as Shapiro notes). He reads a passage he considers remarkably candid for an advocacy publication: it attributes younger Jewish students' declining unconditional support for Israel to "the gradual shift in our generation towards attempting to find personal meaning in Judaism." Shapiro's reading of this line is pointed — he takes it as an admission, from inside the pro-Israel advocacy world itself, that Zionist identity has functioned as a substitute for genuine religious engagement, and that the more people actually connect with Judaism as a religion, the less they need Israel to supply their sense of Jewish identity. "If you want to weaken Zionism," he concludes, borrowing the document's own logic against it, "strengthen real Judaism."

---

## 12. The Religious-Liberty Commission Episode

Shapiro then shares a recent, still-unfolding story that he treats as a live demonstration of everything just described.

A few weeks before this recording (he says late January), Shapiro was contacted by a woman named Carrie — a commissioner, he learned, on a Trump administration commission concerning religious liberty, meeting monthly on different topics with roughly a dozen commissioners who call witnesses, gather testimony, and prepare recommendations for the president. February's topic was antisemitism. Carrie wanted Shapiro to testify.

Shapiro's first reaction, relayed to her directly, was skepticism about what "antisemitism" would actually mean in this context: not, he suspected, the ordinary street-level antisemitism of someone knocking a yarmulke off a passerby's head (an experience Shapiro says he's personally had, along with having a penny thrown at his feet once in Penn Station) — but rather a narrower complaint about insufficient support for Israel on college campuses and elsewhere. He agreed to participate anyway, proposing what he considered the truthful core of the issue: that American Jews are perceived as less fully American because of a foreign country, thousands of miles away, that legally and rhetorically claims to represent them — and that this perception carries real reputational, material, and social costs, a pattern he says is empirically confirmed by the Tel Aviv University Kantor Center's tracking of antisemitic incidents rising in step with unpopular Israeli actions.

Scheduling ultimately excluded Shapiro from testifying directly — he was told, apologetically, that there were no more open witness slots. The day before the hearing, Carrie asked whether she could read a roughly 30-second prepared statement from him into the record instead. He agreed. At the hearing, alongside a panel of witnesses Shapiro describes as largely Zionist advocates, Carrie read his statement and then asked the other witnesses directly whether what he'd said — that many Jews object to being presumed connected to a foreign country — was itself antisemitic.

According to Shapiro, no witness would give a clean answer. One offered a conditional: it would be antisemitic only if applied as a "double standard" — without clarifying what that meant in this context. Another invoked the Sharansky three-D's framework without actually answering the question posed. When Carrie pressed again later in the hearing, one witness dismissed the underlying point entirely, saying it didn't matter whether the position was antisemitic or not, since he didn't personally care about antisemitism as long as people obeyed the law — and then attacked Shapiro's credibility directly, claiming (per Shapiro) that Shapiro had deliberately mistranslated Jabotinsky's writing. Shapiro rejects this flatly and somewhat wryly offers to personally tutor his accuser in reading Hebrew.

Only one commissioner, Shapiro says, genuinely engaged with the substantive question: Ben Carson, who affirmed that it would indeed be wrong for Jews to be perceived as outsiders in America, and invoked the historical example of Haym Salomon, whose financial support helped fund the American Revolution, as evidence of how deeply embedded Jewish contribution is in the American story. Nobody built on Carson's point; the subject was changed, and the hearing moved on.

In the aftermath, Shapiro says, Carrie was removed from — or, by her own account, disputed her removal from — the commission, on the stated grounds that she had used her position to advance a personal political agenda; she maintains she was appointed by Trump directly and therefore cannot be dismissed by the commission's chairman. National press coverage followed — Shapiro names the *Wall Street Journal*, the *New York Times*, and *The Atlantic* — but, in his account, every piece of coverage he saw framed the story as a single, narrow question: was Carrie herself an antisemite for raising this? None of the coverage he encountered engaged with the substantive question that had prompted her to raise it in the first place — whether American Jews are, in fact, treated as less fully American because of assumed loyalty to Israel. To Shapiro, this is not incidental; it is the hasbara strategy from Section 11, playing out in real time, exactly as described: never defend or examine the substance, always redirect scrutiny onto whoever raised the question.

## 13. The Six-Day War, Clausewitz, and Why Israel Fights the Way It Does

In the episode's closing stretch, the conversation turns to military history, and Shapiro brings out a specific source: a West Point military-history volume on the Arab-Israeli wars, part of a series that also covers the Chinese Civil War and the Korean War, which he bought years ago when Amazon still primarily sold books.

He reads from the volume's own concluding assessment of Israel's military record: an "excellent model" of how an outnumbered nation can fight and win, built on universal conscription, a highly competent standing force, and central geographic position — but the same passage raises a pointed, unresolved criticism. The text invokes the Prussian military theorist **Carl von Clausewitz**, whose core principle Shapiro explains for listeners unfamiliar with it: a war is only worth fighting if winning it leaves you in a *better political position* than before the war started; military victory that doesn't translate into improved political standing isn't actually a strategic success.

Applied to Israel, the West Point text argues this principle "has somehow eluded Israel's leaders." The 1956 Sinai Campaign's military success didn't secure Israel's position. The 1967 victory brought new territory but, per the text, "severely strained" Israel's military and economic resources, deepened its international isolation, and damaged perceptions of its cause's legitimacy — while, strikingly, it was the *military setback* of the 1973 Yom Kippur War that created the political conditions for Israel's first peace treaty with an Arab state, Egypt. Continued military responses to Palestinian resistance, the text concludes, eroded international support and strengthened Palestinian resolve rather than weakening it.

The West Point analysis, as Shapiro reads it, poses this as a genuine puzzle it doesn't resolve: why would a state with such evident military competence keep making choices that, by ordinary Clausewitzian logic, backfire politically? Shapiro's answer is the one this entire episode has been building toward: **Clausewitz's rule simply doesn't apply to Zionism, because political advantage was never actually the goal.** The goal, in his reading, is to demonstrate strength for its own sake — to no longer be, in the loaded phrase both speakers use repeatedly, "the weak Jew." Being a Jew with half a foot always mentally in Auschwitz, unable to trust the world to intervene the way it failed to intervene during the Holocaust, means — on this account — that winning itself, and being seen to win, matters more than whether winning actually improves the country's long-term position. "If we had this army under Hitler," Shapiro says, "there wouldn't have been a Holocaust." The lesson drawn from the Holocaust, on this account, is not "seek peace" but "never again be defenseless" — and that lesson, pursued to its logical end, can override ordinary strategic calculation entirely.

Shapiro backs this with specific examples of Israeli leaders explicitly rejecting caution as a form of shameful "exile mentality": Netanyahu, responding to U.S. criticism during the late Obama years (after the U.S. abstained rather than vetoed a UN resolution on settlements), declaring an end to "this exile mentality" and old, accommodating Jewish habits of mind. Naftali Bennett making a similar statement more recently, about no longer seeking to be loved by the world, only feared. Jonathan Pollard, in a video Shapiro references, criticizing Netanyahu himself as insufficiently aggressive precisely because Pollard considers him still psychologically an "exile Jew." Even in 1967, Shapiro recalls, there were Israeli officials (he names Levi Eshkol) reluctant to attack Egypt preemptively, since Egypt had not yet actually attacked and Israel already had access to whatever military resources it needed on its own timeline — and those officials were mocked by their colleagues as weak, negotiating, exilic.

The pattern culminates, for Shapiro, in a well-known Israeli children's song whose lyrics he summarizes rather than quotes in full: the whole world is against us, we don't care about them, they don't care about us — a message, he notes with visible discomfort, sung by children's choirs. Being feared, not being loved, is the explicit goal — a fist, not a negotiation, is "the only thing we have to save ourself."

Silverstein closes this section by drawing out the human cost of this framework as Shapiro sees it, on ordinary Jews who want no part of it: people, Shapiro included, who simply want to live quietly, spend time with family, walk along the water, and be left alone — which, he notes with an edge of bitter irony, is itself treated by Zionist logic as a kind of betrayal, since wanting to be left alone runs directly against the whole project of demonstrated strength the "New Jew" was built to pursue.

## 14. Synthesis: The Argument as a Whole

Put together, the episode makes a single sustained argument with several interlocking parts.

Judaism, as the speakers define it, is a covenantal religious relationship: obligations flowing from Sinai, binding on Jews regardless of personal belief, structurally distinct from the seven universal Noahide laws that bind everyone else. It has never, in their account, been a political nationality in the modern sense.

Zionism, beginning intellectually with figures like Graetz in the nineteenth century and taking its most explicit and revealing form in Jabotinsky's early-twentieth-century writing, reconstructed Jewish identity as exactly that: a political nationality, requiring a state, a flag, a revived vernacular language, and — most tellingly, in the speakers' reading — a deliberately re-engineered national character built as the point-by-point opposite of the traditional Jewish ideal. This reconstruction is not, in the speakers' presentation, a matter of interpretation or inference on their part; it is written directly into Israel's own governing law (the 2018 Nation-State Law), its founding institutional agreements (the 1952 Status Law), and its highest court's own reasoning (the *Ornan* nationality case) — all of which affirm, in the state's own words, that Jewish national self-determination belongs to Jews as a global category, not to Israeli citizens as such.

Because Israel legally and rhetorically claims to represent Jews everywhere, the ordinary mechanism by which anger at a state's conduct stays confined to that state's own citizens breaks down: Israel's actions generate hostility that lands on Jews as a category, including those with no connection to Israel at all. This is not, in the speakers' account, an unfortunate accident of antisemitic overreach — it is the predictable, structural consequence of a "dual loyalty" framework that Zionism itself insists on, rather than one antisemites invented independently.

The speakers' proposed response is neither political engagement with the Israeli-Palestinian conflict (which they treat as outside their concern as Jews per se) nor quiet accommodation, but public, deliberate separation: a decades-long protest tradition, rooted in Rabbi Teitelbaum's post-1967 call, insisting — loudly, and now in English as well as Yiddish — that Israel does not speak for Judaism, alongside institutional non-participation in Zionist organizations, continued rabbinic debate over the narrower and more practical question of political engagement with the Israeli state itself, and a broader argument that the antidote to Zionism's hold on Jewish identity is not confrontation with Israel but a return to the religious substance — the covenant, the tradition, the psalms — that Zionism, on their reading, was built to replace.

## 15. Glossary

| Term | Meaning as used in the episode |
|---|---|
| **Zionism** (per Shapiro's definition) | The belief that Jews constitute a political nationality and that Israel is the nation-state of that nationality, entitled to represent and speak for Jews worldwide. |
| **Am** (עַם) | Hebrew for "people" or "nation" in a general, non-political sense; Shapiro argues its biblical usage does not carry the modern meaning of political nationality. |
| **Noahide covenant** | The seven commandments traditionally understood as binding on all humanity, given through Noah. |
| **Mosaic covenant** | The 613 commandments of the Torah, given at Sinai, binding specifically on Jews. |
| **Kofer / Kofrim** | Term used for a nonbelieving or nonobservant Jew who remains covenantally obligated regardless. |
| **Mesirat nefesh** | Literally "handing over the soul"; devoted self-sacrifice, the phrase Silverstein attributes to Rabbi Teitelbaum. |
| **Shalashudes** | The third Sabbath meal, a traditional Hasidic teaching occasion, and the setting of Teitelbaum's 1967 remarks. |
| **Hasbara** | Hebrew for "explanation"; organized pro-Israel public advocacy and messaging strategy. |
| **Negotiorum gestio** | Roman-law doctrine permitting someone to manage an absent party's affairs without prior authorization; cited as Herzl's implicit basis for Zionist representation of world Jewry. |
| **Le'om** | Hebrew for "nationality," the Israeli legal category distinct from citizenship and religion. |
| **Rachmanim, Bayshanim** | Traditional Jewish character ideals: merciful/compassionate, and capable of shame (as opposed to shameless). |
| **Betar** | Jabotinsky's Revisionist Zionist youth movement, and the name of its anthem. |
| **"New Jew" / "New Hebrew"** | The Zionist cultural ideal of a proud, physically strong, assertive identity, explicitly constructed as the opposite of a perceived passive "exile" Jewish stereotype. |
| **Exile mentality** | A term used pejoratively by Zionist leaders (per the episode) for caution, negotiation, or restraint, treated as a shameful holdover from Diaspora psychology. |
| **The Three D's** | Natan Sharansky's framework (demonization, double standards, delegitimization) for distinguishing antisemitic criticism of Israel from legitimate criticism. |
| **Karedi (Haredi)** | Ultra-Orthodox Jews; in this episode, the community most associated with resistance to Israeli military conscription. |
| **Aguda / Agudath Israel** | Haredi organization historically opposed to secular Zionism, though later willing to engage practically with the Israeli state through Knesset participation. |
| **Neturei Karta** | A distinct anti-Zionist Haredi movement, ideologically aligned with but institutionally separate from Satmar. |
