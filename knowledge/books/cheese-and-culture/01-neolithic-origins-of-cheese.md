# Sour Milk and the Birth of Cheese: The Neolithic Origins

Picture a hillside somewhere in what is now southeastern Turkey, northern Iraq, or western Iran, about ten thousand years ago. A small group of people is watching over a flock of wild-looking sheep and goats. These animals are not quite the fluffy, docile creatures we know today. They are leaner, wilder, and only recently under human control. And the people watching them have no idea that they are standing at the beginning of a story that will eventually lead to Parmesan, Brie, Cheddar, and the block of cheese sitting in your refrigerator right now.

That is where Paul Kindstedt begins his book, *Cheese and Culture*. Kindstedt is a food scientist at the University of Vermont who has spent his career studying how cheese works at the chemical level. What makes his book unusual is that he brings that scientific lens to history. He keeps asking a simple question: given what we know about milk, bacteria, salt, and climate, how would people in a particular time and place actually have made cheese? Today we start at the very beginning.

## The Herders of the Fertile Crescent

The Fertile Crescent is the arc of land that curves from the eastern Mediterranean coast up through southern Turkey and down along the Tigris and Euphrates rivers into Iraq and western Iran. It is one of the places where people first moved from hunting and gathering to farming, and it is where wheat, barley, lentils, and several important animals were first domesticated.

Archaeologists generally date the domestication of sheep and goats to roughly eleven thousand to ten thousand years ago, in the hills and mountain foothills of this region. Cattle came a little later, tamed from a massive wild ox called the aurochs, a beast so large and fierce that taming it is honestly a little hard to imagine.

Here is something that surprises many people. At first, these animals were almost certainly kept for meat, hides, and bone, not milk. Milking a wild or semi-wild goat is not easy, and early domestic animals likely produced only small amounts of milk, mainly enough for their young. So when did milking begin?

## Detectives in the Pottery Shards

For a long time, historians had to guess. In the early nineteen-eighties, the archaeologist Andrew Sherratt proposed an influential idea he called the "secondary products revolution." He argued that people first kept animals purely for meat, and only thousands of years later, around the fourth millennium BC, began systematically using what he called secondary products: milk, wool, and animal pulling power for plows and carts.

Then the detective work got more sophisticated, and the timeline was pushed back dramatically.

One clue came from bones. When archaeologists study the ages at which ancient herds were slaughtered, they can see patterns. If a herder wants meat, it makes sense to let animals grow to full size before slaughter. But if a herder wants milk, the smart move is to slaughter most of the young males early, so the mothers' milk goes to people instead of to calves, kids, and lambs. Some very early sites show exactly this pattern.

An even more striking clue came from pottery. Clay pots are porous, and fats from whatever was cooked or stored in them can soak into the walls and survive for thousands of years. Chemists can extract those ancient fats and tell whether they came from meat or from milk. In 2008, a team led by the chemist Richard Evershed published evidence of milk fats in pottery from northwestern Anatolia, in modern Turkey, dating to around the seventh millennium BC. That is more than eight thousand years ago, far earlier than the secondary products model predicted.

In fairness to Sherratt, his broad point still has some force. Milking may have been practiced early, but large-scale, intensive dairying, along with wool sheep and plow oxen, does seem to have expanded later. Historians still debate how gradual or sudden that shift really was.

## The Problem with Milk

So people were milking animals very early. But here is the twist that makes this whole story fascinating: most of those people probably could not comfortably drink the milk.

Milk contains a sugar called lactose. To digest it, our bodies need an enzyme called lactase. Every human baby makes lactase, because babies live on milk. But in most mammals, including most humans throughout history, the body stops producing much lactase after weaning. An adult without enough lactase who drinks a large glass of milk can end up with bloating, cramps, gas, and diarrhea, because the undigested sugar passes into the gut, where bacteria ferment it.

Even today, the majority of adults worldwide have reduced lactase activity. The ability to digest milk into adulthood, called lactase persistence, is the result of genetic mutations that became common in only certain populations, especially in northern Europe, and separately in some groups in Africa and the Middle East. Ancient DNA studies show that the earliest farmers who brought agriculture into Europe almost all lacked the European version of this mutation. Geneticists estimate it arose and began spreading roughly seven to eight thousand years ago, after dairying was already underway.

I want to flag something honestly here. Since Kindstedt's book came out in 2012, the genetic picture has gotten more complicated. A large study published in 2022 suggested that the mutation remained rare for thousands of years even while people were using milk widely, and only spread rapidly much later, possibly during times of famine and disease when the ability to digest milk could mean the difference between life and death. So the old idea that drinking milk simply gave people an advantage that spread the gene is now under debate. That newer research came after the book, so you won't find it in Kindstedt's account.

Either way, the puzzle is clear. Early herders were milking animals, yet most adults among them could not easily digest fresh milk. So what were they doing with it?

## Let the Bacteria Do the Work

This is where Kindstedt's scientific perspective really shines, and the answer is wonderfully simple: they let the milk go sour.

Think about the climate. The Fertile Crescent is warm, and in summer it is hot. Fresh milk left out in that heat does not stay fresh for long. Milk naturally carries lactic acid bacteria, tiny microbes that feed on lactose and turn it into lactic acid. In warm conditions they multiply quickly, and within hours the milk begins to sour.

As the acid builds up, something dramatic happens to the milk's proteins. Most of the protein in milk is a family called casein, which floats around in tiny clusters. Normally these clusters carry an electric charge that keeps them apart, suspended in the liquid. But as the milk becomes more acidic, that charge fades away. At a certain point the clusters stop repelling each other and begin sticking together. The milk thickens and then separates into soft, white curds and a thin, yellowish liquid called whey.

Now comes the key insight. When you drain away the whey, most of the lactose goes with it, because lactose dissolves in the watery part of milk. And the bacteria have already eaten some of the lactose that remains. The result is a soft, tangy, fresh cheese, something like a simple cottage cheese, that is far lower in lactose than the milk it came from. A person who could not drink a cup of milk could often eat this cheese without trouble. The same is true of yogurt-like fermented milks.

In other words, the first cheesemakers did not need to invent anything elaborate. Nature and climate did much of the work. Kindstedt suggests that the earliest cheese was very likely this kind of acid-curdled, fresh cheese, and that its low lactose content may help explain why dairying took hold among people who could not drink milk.

## What About Rennet?

Most cheeses today are made with rennet, an enzyme found in the stomach of young calves, lambs, and kids. Rennet makes milk set into a firm, smooth curd that can be pressed and aged into hard cheeses.

There is a famous old story about how rennet was discovered. A traveler carries milk in a pouch made from an animal's stomach, and after a long, hot journey he opens it to find curds and whey. It is a charming tale, and it is perfectly plausible that something like it happened, because people did use animal organs as containers. But honestly, nobody knows. It is a story, not a recorded event, and it has been retold so often that it gets presented as fact far more confidently than the evidence allows. Many scholars think rennet came into use later than simple acid curdling, but the exact timing is uncertain.

## Salt and Seasons

There was another big problem early herders faced, and it may be the most important reason cheese mattered. Milk is seasonal. Sheep and goats give birth in late winter and spring, and their milk is plentiful for a few months, then dwindles. Fresh milk spoils in hours. Even fresh cheese lasts only days in a hot climate.

So the challenge was preservation: how do you take a burst of spring abundance and stretch it into the lean months?

Salt is a big part of the answer. Salt pulls moisture out of cheese, and drier cheese gives spoilage microbes less to work with. Salt also directly slows the growth of many harmful bacteria while allowing the hardier lactic acid bacteria to carry on. Pressing out more whey, drying cheese in the sun or wind, or storing it in salty brine, much like modern feta, could turn a perishable food into one that lasted for weeks or months.

Exactly when and how the earliest herders began salting and drying their cheese is not something we can pin down precisely, but the logic of the climate makes it very likely that preservation was a pressing concern from early on.

## The Oldest Cheese Strainers

If you want a satisfying piece of physical evidence, here is one. Archaeologists have long found strange clay pots peppered with small holes, which look a lot like modern cheese strainers. For years their purpose was debated. Maybe they were for honey, or beer, or something else entirely.

In 2013, the year after Kindstedt's book appeared, researchers analyzed residues in perforated pots from Poland dating to roughly seven thousand years ago and found milk fats inside them. That makes them some of the strongest direct evidence of cheesemaking yet found. This discovery was in central Europe, not the Fertile Crescent, which tells us that by then cheesemaking had already traveled a long way with farming communities. Kindstedt wrote before those results were published, so they are an addition to his story rather than part of it.

## Why It Was Such a Breakthrough

So let's step back and see why this matters. Turning milk into cheese solved three problems at once. It made milk digestible for adults who lacked lactase. It concentrated nutrition, packing protein and fat into a smaller, portable package. And it preserved a seasonal, fragile food so that it could feed people long after the animals stopped giving milk.

That combination changed what it meant to keep animals. A flock was no longer only a supply of meat that disappeared when an animal was slaughtered. It became a living, renewable source of food, harvested day after day. And cheese, as we'll see in the next part of this series, soon became something more than food. It became wealth, tribute, trade goods, and even an offering to the gods.
