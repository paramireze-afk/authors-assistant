# The Bitcoin Dollar, Five Years On

## Stablecoins, debt sinks, and the tools that use us

In a long-form return episode of the *Unlimited Hangout* podcast, investigative journalists **Whitney Webb** and **Mark Goodwin** revisit the central thesis of Goodwin's book *The Bitcoin Dollar*: that Bitcoin and dollar-backed stablecoins are being positioned as the successor to the petrodollar system, and that this arrangement serves the interests of the US government and a small circle of Silicon Valley and Wall Street power brokers rather than ordinary holders.

The episode lands roughly five years after Goodwin first published the idea in September 2021. Along the way, the hosts set out where they stand on AI and on Bitcoin, respond to critics who call them Bitcoin promoters, and walk through what they see as the policy and corporate architecture now taking shape around digital dollars.

---

## Part 1: Why they won't use AI, but will discuss Bitcoin

The episode opens with a pair of recent opinion pieces the two wrote about artificial intelligence, an unusual format for an outlet that typically publishes very long investigative work.

Webb's piece focused on **Pangram**, an AI-writing detector. Her argument is that such tools don't actually detect whether AI was used; they flag writing that resembles patterns common in current AI output. Because many of those patterns (em dashes, words like "delve") also occur in human writing, she says journalists are now self-censoring their style to avoid being falsely flagged. In her view, detectors are trying to insert themselves as a trust intermediary between writers and readers, when authors should build that trust directly.

Both hosts say they refuse to use AI in their work. Webb frames AI as a tool of **cognitive diminishment** (a term she later traces to Henry Kissinger and Eric Schmidt's 2021 book on AI) and of **homogenization**, pointing to how brands can now instantly generate near-identical content for every viral moment.

Goodwin offers a personal account. In his final period as editor-in-chief of *Bitcoin Magazine*, which he left in 2024, he says the outlet increasingly ran press material through ChatGPT. Although he was opposed to AI and never used it himself, he noticed his own willingness to hand tedious rewrites to colleagues who would. That experience of creeping laziness, he says, is part of why he left to return to writing very long investigative pieces.

### The distinction they draw

The hosts anticipate the objection: how can they reject AI as a tool while discussing Bitcoin? Their answer rests on **frequency and dependence**:

- **AI** requires constant, ongoing interaction: prompting, conversing, delegating. The hosts argue that repeated reliance is precisely what erodes skill and independent thought over time.
- **Bitcoin**, by contrast, can be acquired with a handful of actions and then left untouched for years. As a form of money rather than a generative tool, it doesn't replace the user's thinking.

Webb also notes that money isn't generative output in the way AI content is, so the "use it or lose it" problem applies very differently.

### Responding to the "Bitcoin shill" charge

Goodwin pushes back on commenters who call him a Bitcoin cheerleader because of his former role and his book. He points out that his core thesis has always been skeptical: that Bitcoin was plausibly launched with US government or US-affiliated involvement, timed to the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, as a release valve for the inflation that would follow years of quantitative easing.

Webb adds that she spoke at the 2023 Bitcoin Conference largely to criticize the direction of the community, urging attendees to stick to values such as separating money from the state, privacy, and use as a medium of exchange rather than an asset held by institutions like BlackRock. Both say they became unwelcome in much of the Bitcoin world around 2024, as the industry aligned with the Trump campaign and focused on price.

They stress they are not financial advisers, have no sponsors, and are funded only by subscribers and donations.

## Part 2: The Bitcoin Dollar thesis

### From gold to petrodollars

Goodwin begins with history. Under the Bretton Woods system, dollars were redeemable for gold. Strains appeared in the late 1960s, including France under Charles de Gaulle repatriating gold and the collapse of the London Gold Pool, and in 1971 President Nixon closed the gold window. A period of high inflation followed.

What emerged next, Goodwin explains, was the **petrodollar system**: whether formalized or a handshake understanding, Gulf states led by Saudi Arabia priced oil in dollars and recycled their earnings into US Treasuries and equities. Because nearly every industrializing country needed oil, they first needed dollars. That gave the US a durable source of demand for a currency it could create at low cost, and a ready buyer for its debt.

Counterintuitively, he notes, leaving gold didn't end dollar dominance; the world became *more* dollarized. Rising oil prices, such as the spike after the 1973 Yom Kippur War, actually increased demand for dollars.

### Replacing oil with Bitcoin

The Bitcoin Dollar thesis proposes that the same structure is being rebuilt with two substitutions:

| Petrodollar system | Bitcoin Dollar system |
|---|---|
| Oil as the energy commodity | Bitcoin, which requires energy to mine |
| Dollars needed to buy oil | Stablecoins needed to buy Bitcoin |
| Gulf states recycle into Treasuries | Stablecoin issuers hold Treasuries as reserves |

Goodwin emphasizes that the overwhelming share of Bitcoin trading, futures, spot buying, and now ETF activity is denominated in dollars. Webb adds a key difference from oil: most of the world's oil sits outside the US, while a large share of Bitcoin is held in the US, much of it with **Coinbase Custody**.

### Why March 2020 matters

Bitcoin's supply schedule cuts new issuance in half roughly every four years and eventually goes to zero. Goodwin highlights the May 2020 halving as notable because it was the first time Bitcoin's annual issuance rate fell below both the Fed's roughly 2% inflation target and the growth rate of the gold supply. That coincided with pandemic lockdowns and massive money printing, during which Bitcoin rose from around $3,000 to nearly $70,000 within about a year.

### From inflation to deflation

Webb raises a longer-term angle: after years of inflation that has transferred wealth upward, those who end up owning most assets would benefit from a shift to a deflationary system. Goodwin agrees, with a caveat: only if they hold large amounts of the new deflationary reserve asset.

## Part 3: The network behind it

A large portion of the episode traces relationships the hosts have documented in their ongoing series *The Chain*, which they say they plan to publish as a book, likely next year. Key points they raise include:

- **Xapo and Coinbase Custody.** Coinbase's custody operation acquired technology developed by Xapo, founded by Wences Casares, whom the hosts describe as the person credited with introducing Bitcoin to figures such as Peter Thiel and Reid Hoffman. Goodwin describes elaborate security measures, such as splitting private keys across locations including satellites and Swiss vaults, and argues such protections are built for institutions, not ordinary users.
- **PayPal and Idealab.** The hosts argue Bitcoin may have emerged from the broader PayPal orbit. They describe how Max Levchin, after being told he couldn't work on cryptography for the NSA as a non-citizen, went to Silicon Valley, stayed with Idealab's Scott Banister, and co-founded PayPal with Peter Thiel. They link Idealab co-founder Bill Elkus to Jeffrey Epstein.
- **Tether's origins.** They cite released Epstein emails from around 2013 in which Idealab's Bill Gross discusses a digital currency idea and Epstein suggests he speak to Brock Pierce. Pierce, William Quigley, and Craig Sellars were among those involved in Tether's creation.
- **Other connections.** They mention their previous reporting on Satellogic, a Tether-backed satellite firm, and their argument that SpaceX may have roots in an Idealab space venture involving Peter Diamandis.

### PayPal as the first digital dollarization project

Goodwin returns repeatedly to an anecdote from an early PayPal insider's memoir, *The PayPal Wars*, describing Thiel telling staff that PayPal would give people worldwide access to dollars and free them from their own central banks. Goodwin's objection is that the dollar is itself a central bank product; spreading it doesn't free anyone from central banking, it expands the reach of one particular central bank and undermines other countries' capital controls. He argues the same marketing language now appears in stablecoin promotion.

Webb adds that many Thiel-adjacent figures present themselves as opposed to central banks while, in her view, supporting private banks, which she says is what the Federal Reserve fundamentally represents. Stablecoins, she argues, let private institutions bypass the Fed as middleman entirely.

## Part 4: Why the hosts say CBDCs were a "red herring"

Goodwin's 2022 article *The Stablecoin Monster* argued that fears about a US central bank digital currency were misplaced. Both hosts say the documentation never suggested the Fed intended to issue a retail dollar CBDC. Instead, they expected private stablecoins to fill that role.

They argue this allowed the administration to look pro-freedom by banning a US CBDC by executive order, while simultaneously passing the **GENIUS Act**, which establishes rules for stablecoins backed by US Treasuries.

Their account of how the legislation took shape:

1. The collapse of **Terra/Luna**, an algorithmic stablecoin, in 2022 was used to justify banning algorithmic designs.
2. The resulting Lummis–Gillibrand framework, which became the basis for the GENIUS Act, required stablecoins to be backed by short-term US government debt.
3. The hosts suggest competing stablecoin designs were effectively sidelined, leaving Treasury-backed issuers such as Tether and Circle in a dominant position.

They note the later **CLARITY Act** failed, adding that Bitcoin doesn't need it, since it is already treated as a commodity rather than a security.

### Digital ID as the other half

Webb ties digital money to digital identity. She argues that policy documents consistently link digital ID to financial transactions through approved wallets, and that the realized system is likely to look like a "mosaic" of private-sector wallet and ID providers sharing common standards, so that data can still be centralized. She cites the World Bank's ID4D initiative and the BIS as likely hubs, and notes recent federal digital modernization efforts led by Airbnb co-founder Joe Gebbia.

## Part 5: Stablecoins as instruments of state power

The hosts point to recent public statements from Treasury Secretary **Scott Bessent** describing dollar stablecoins, including Tether's USDT, as tools for US financial power and sanctions. They connect this to a WikiLeaks-published US military manual describing the IMF and World Bank as financial instruments of national power, and argue stablecoins are the modern version.

They also note:

- **Howard Lutnick**, now Commerce Secretary, whose former firm Cantor Fitzgerald holds a significant equity stake in Tether.
- **Kevin Warsh**, now leading the Federal Reserve, who the hosts say previously had ties to Anchorage Digital, a crypto bank that offers rewards on PayPal's PYUSD stablecoin.

### Dollarization abroad

In Argentina and Venezuela, the hosts say, people facing currency collapse understandably turn to Tether to preserve their savings. But they argue this channels them onto a private dollar that can be frozen, monitored, and potentially programmed at the US government's request, while being marketed with the "permissionless" language associated with Bitcoin. They warn similar pressures could eventually reach the US itself.

## Part 6: The debt sink

### The bankruptcy provision

Webb raises an academic paper, co-written by a Federal Reserve researcher, arguing that the GENIUS Act may enable an unusual bankruptcy maneuver: a company nearing insolvency could move valuable assets into stablecoin issuance, putting them beyond the reach of some creditors. Goodwin compares it to the J.Crew case, in which valuable assets were shifted into a subsidiary out of creditors' reach. The hosts say the concern is that this is written into federal law rather than being an exploited loophole, and that losses could end up falling on bodies like the FDIC and ultimately taxpayers, functioning like a "bail-in."

### Who buys zero-yield debt?

Drawing on macro analyst **Luke Gromen**, Goodwin outlines the US fiscal bind. With roughly $40 trillion in debt, interest payments are now one of the largest federal budget items. Raising rates increases debt service costs, so, in this view, both raising and cutting rates can be inflationary.

Goodwin suggests the only workable path is shifting debt to short-term Treasury bills and pushing rates toward zero. The obvious problem: who would buy debt that pays nothing?

His answer: **stablecoin issuers**, which by law must hold short-term Treasuries, and which (in Tether's and Coinbase's case) also hold large amounts of Bitcoin. He speculates about a scenario in which capital rules require institutions to hold stablecoins in proportion to their Bitcoin holdings, so that as Bitcoin's value rises, demand for Treasury-backed stablecoins rises with it, creating a **"debt sink"** for US government borrowing.

### Why Bitcoin rather than gold?

If the goal were simply to revalue a hard asset to cover debt, the US could reprice its gold holdings. Goodwin argues that doing so would also enrich gold-holding rivals such as China and Russia. Bitcoin, by contrast, is heavily concentrated among US and Silicon Valley holders, while countries that held notable amounts, such as Iran and Venezuela, have faced US pressure.

He also presents a second, darker interpretation: that apparent geopolitical rivalry is largely staged, and that the real project is a consolidated system in which a small group holds the deflationary asset while most people hold IOUs or stablecoins instead.

Webb adds that if this plays out, Silicon Valley oligarchs who hold much of the Bitcoin supply would become wealthier than many governments, accelerating projects such as "network states" and privatized governance.

### The COVID precedent

The hosts revisit BlackRock's 2019 "Going Direct" policy paper, which, as researcher **John Titus** has documented, proposed routing crisis stimulus more directly into markets, and BlackRock's subsequent role managing parts of the pandemic-era Fed response. Webb's advice for anticipating Trump's second term: look at what was attempted in the first, and assume they'll go further this time.

## Part 7: Freedom tools that weren't

Toward the end, the hosts extend the argument beyond Bitcoin. The personal computer, the internet, and smartphones were all presented as empowering individuals, yet the hosts say each has roots in or ties to the national security apparatus, and each has become a channel for surveillance and control. They point to choke points such as legal pressure, debanking, and DNS-level removal, and argue that generative AI now makes overt censorship less necessary because platforms can simply be flooded with synthetic content. Webb notes that forums she once found valuable for unusual ideas now feel overrun.

They return to Kissinger and Schmidt's book, which the hosts say anticipated that people would struggle to tell real from fake and would turn to AI as an arbiter of truth. The book also posed two possible futures: unrest by those left behind, or AI-centered quasi-religious devotion. The hosts suspect the powerful would prefer a mix of both, with dissent confined to controlled lanes.

They also criticize what they see as false binaries in current debates, whether "AI doom vs. AI abundance" or "Bitcoin bro vs. stablecoin serf vs. off-grid Luddite."

## Takeaways from the hosts

The hosts close with general (explicitly non-financial) observations:

- **Owning Bitcoin isn't the same as backing the system.** Goodwin argues holding Bitcoin doesn't fund the surveillance architecture the way buying shares of a surveillance company would; the debt-sink plan will proceed regardless.
- **Preserving purchasing power isn't gaining power.** Even if Bitcoin rises sharply, ordinary holders will at best maintain their position while the largest holders pull further ahead.
- **Be discreet.** Goodwin criticizes Bitcoin culture's habit of publicly advertising holdings through merchandise, conference attendance, and social media.
- **Own real things.** Both recommend, where possible, owning a home, land, tools, and food security rather than renting, to avoid dependence on systems that could become a form of "stablecoin UBI serfdom."
- **Hear out the other side.** Goodwin encourages listeners to seek out analysts who argue that stablecoins can't meaningfully absorb US debt, and to judge whose arguments hold up better.

## Context and caveats

This episode is advocacy-driven analysis from journalists with a well-defined investigative perspective. Readers should keep several points in mind:

- **Bitcoin's origin** remains unknown; its creator, Satoshi Nakamoto, has never been conclusively identified. The suggestion of government involvement is a hypothesis the hosts acknowledge they cannot prove.
- **Network claims** linking individuals and companies to Epstein or intelligence agencies are the hosts' interpretations of associations and documents; association alone does not establish coordination or intent.
- **The debt-sink scenario** is speculative. Many economists question whether stablecoin demand could approach the scale of US borrowing needs, and the capital-requirement mechanism Goodwin describes is a hypothetical, not existing policy.
- **The GENIUS Act bankruptcy concern** comes from one academic analysis; how courts would treat such cases remains untested.
- **Policy and personnel details** (such as Federal Reserve leadership, recent rate decisions, and the status of crypto legislation) reflect the moment of recording and may change.

The hosts themselves frame their work as directional rather than definitive, and say a forthcoming article will examine the early setup of what they call the Bitcoin Dollar system, along with connections to AI and to the events surrounding 9/11.
