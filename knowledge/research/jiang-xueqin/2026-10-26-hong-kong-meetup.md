# The Meritocracy Is Failing Society

*A lecture and Q&A delivered in Hong Kong as part of the speaker's world tour.*

## Opening Remarks

The speaker welcomes the audience, noting he and his son Chris flew in from Toronto the day before. He has spent the past three months touring North America, meeting viewers for the first time, and describes the community as diverse, enthusiastic, and energetic.

He says the news in recent weeks has grown more dire: the war in Iran is about to heat up, the war in Ukraine is escalating, there are riots and revolts across Europe, a mass famine is expected to begin in Africa within months, and anti-incumbent right-wing movements are rising in South America (he predicts Lula will lose to Flávio Bolsonaro). He says the general direction of what he has forecast for the past couple of years is manifesting, even if he gets some details wrong, and he expects things to escalate starting next year.

For the coming months he plans a series of lectures on what the community can do, what hope exists, and what the world will look like "after everything collapses." This lecture focuses on education, specifically the meritocracy.

He defines the meritocracy as the idea that the best and brightest should attend the best universities and then become society's leaders. He describes himself as a product of it, grateful for his full scholarship to Yale. He invites interruption, discussion, and debate.

**Central thesis:** The meritocracy is failing society and failing everyone, including our children. Though people associate it with fairness, he argues it is not fair, and he proposes a different path forward.

## The Speaker's Personal Story

### Early Childhood
- Born in July 1976 in Taishan, a poor fishing village a couple of hours from Hong Kong. He is Cantonese.
- Born at the tail end of the Cultural Revolution, a few months before Chairman Mao died.
- His father emigrated to Toronto for a better life and worked as a dishwasher his entire life.
- He moved to Canada at age 6 speaking no English; his family did not speak English at home.

### The Grade One Decision
- He spent his first year in grade one understanding nothing, daydreaming and looking out the window.
- At year's end, his teacher met with his mother (through a translator) and said he had learned nothing and his English had not improved. She recommended he go back to kindergarten.

- Even so, he is thankful to his mother. He believes education is about being "the master of your own learning destiny" — making your own choices and learning from your mistakes — and that his freedom mattered more than smoothly doing well in school.

### Reflecting on Yale
- At the time, he saw himself as proof that the meritocracy works.
- Looking back, he says he would **not** want his three kids to experience what he did at Yale.
- He notes classmates and friends are "hellbent" on getting their exceptionally gifted children (14-year-olds reading at university level, state athletes) into Harvard and Yale.

## Three Problems with the Meritocracy

### 1. It Is Unfair
The meritocracy discriminates heavily in favor of children with high IQ and good memory and discards everyone else. A fairer system would let all children prosper regardless of their interests or characteristics. He says society overvalues IQ, reason, and logic; success in life requires hard work, a passion for learning, and curiosity, and emotional intelligence is probably more useful than high IQ. Yet standardized testing like the SAT selects only for IQ.

### 2. It Is Anti-Intellectual
Universities should develop intellectual habits: curiosity, imagination, speculation, making and learning from mistakes, and open, honest debate. He says this doesn't happen at elite universities because students are on a path to "success" — Washington internships, law school, medical school — where failure isn't allowed.

From his experience with Yale and Ivy League peers, he describes many as unwilling to engage in open debate, not curious, and living by a narrow definition of success. Elite universities produce bankers, lawyers, and doctors but very few public intellectuals. He attributes much of his own YouTube success (almost three million subscribers) and podcast invitations to being one of the few public intellectuals today — not because he's right about most things, but because he is curious, imaginative, and willing to debate in good faith.

### 3. Involution
- He says he could not get into Yale today. When he applied in 1995 the acceptance rate was about 20%; today it's around 5%.
- Parents build résumés for their children from day one, leading to the "death of childhood": no play, vocabulary and math in kindergarten to get into the right primary school, high school, and university.
- He says this has destroyed children's happiness and mental health, in Western as well as Chinese education. At McGill, he observed students who don't attend class and instead smoke cannabis and play video games — burnt out after being helicopter-parented with no freedom to fail.
- It is bad for children and for society, as enormous resources are spent educating a small elite.

### Better Systems
- **Nordic countries (e.g., Finland):** Spend most resources on vocational colleges training plumbers and electricians, because most people want to learn a craft, get a job, and support a family — not read Plato, Kant, and Dante.
- **The U.S. a century ago** had three types of universities:
  1. **Ivy League** — for the rich; essentially a social club.
  2. **Research universities** (e.g., Chicago, Johns Hopkins) — for smart people becoming professors and scientists.
  3. **A&M / state schools** — training farmers and engineers to grow the economy.

  He says this system allowed the U.S. to prosper quickly.

## Why Do We Have the Meritocracy?

He acknowledges the common explanation — that Harvard and Yale have power and shaped society to their benefit — but says it doesn't explain how a few private schools came to control the whole education system or why the SAT became so prevalent.

**His argument:** The real reason has to do with the occult, or eschatology. He says powerful people in the U.S. are Freemasons with a particular understanding of the cosmos, who believe they can bring about the end times or heaven on earth. The meritocracy was the plan to create heaven on earth.

### Medieval Europe
- A static, hierarchical society led by the Catholic Church.
- People were taught the world is fallen and evil because of Adam and Eve. Catholic priests and Jewish rabbis both taught that we must redeem ourselves through penance.
- Life as serfs and peasants was hard, but understood as God's plan: live 40–50 years without sin and receive spiritual happiness after death. The priority was obeying the church and the law.

### The Protestant Reformation and Modernity
- He uses a diet metaphor: months of only water and salad create a building hunger for fatty, salty food. Likewise, centuries of repression created a cultural desire to rebel against authority and assert independence.
- Examples of this rebellion:
  - **John Milton's *Paradise Lost***, read by Romantics like William Blake as casting Satan as the hero who liberated humanity.
  - **Sabbatai Zevi and Jacob Frank** in the Jewish tradition, who said the rabbis were dictators and that people have a direct connection to God.
  - **The Freemasons**, arising to pursue science, liberty, and individualism.
  - **The French Revolution.**
- Modernity's radical departure: the idea that individuals can become God and create paradise on earth, with a divine responsibility to build a better world. The Pilgrims sought to build a "New Jerusalem" in America, and many Freemasons went to the United States.

### The Kabbalah and Gnosticism
- He summarizes the Kabbalah as applying Gnostic or Hermetic philosophy to the Bible to explain the cosmic significance of its stories.
- Gnosticism, as he describes it, holds that consciousness gives rise to the body, not the reverse. Seen as conscious energy, we are all connected and beyond time and space.
- The Kabbalah's radical idea: God created the world to complete and know itself, but human ego and defiance broke the world. To repair the world and bring God to earth, we must redeem ourselves.
- In the Masonic tradition, a system where **reason triumphs** — where reason becomes God — would perfect the world. In their eschatology, reason is the meritocracy.

### History of the SAT and IQ
- A century ago the three-tier system worked well, but the Ivy League became dominant through the SAT.
- **James B. Conant**, president of Harvard in the 1930s, wanted a scholarship program to find the best and brightest nationwide.
- He tasked **Henry Chauncey**, later the first president of ETS (Educational Testing Service — behind the TOEFL, GRE, SAT, and MCAT), with creating the SAT.
- He says both descended from Pilgrims and "we can suspect" they were Freemasons, because the goal matches the Masonic ideal of building a temple from perfect cubes — and perfect cubes are made through reason, promoted via IQ.
- **On IQ:** He says the concept is very unscientific and was made up. French psychologist Alfred Binet, worried about social inequality, intended IQ as a threshold — below it, people might face social problems — but not as a useful general measure. Americans turned it into a standard because it aligned with the idea of reason.
- **The plan:** Use the SAT to find the highest-IQ people, make IQ the academic standard, spread through the education system, make everyone smarter, and thereby perfect society.

### Why It Failed, and What Comes Next
- In practice, IQ proved exclusionary and was believed to be unchangeable, producing a more divided and unequal society.
- He says those who hold this eschatology don't conclude the project failed; they conclude external factors — family, culture, race, community — corrupted it, and that these must be removed from the equation.
- They are now shifting from the meritocracy to **AI**, which he says explains the massive growth of data centers. The eschatology stays the same; only the tactics change.
- He cites Facebook promoting a personal AI service called "Muse." He describes a scenario where, from birth, a person has an AI — framed as a soulmate, daemon, or angel — that they trust like God and obey: be good, exercise, be healthy, do well in school. If everyone does this, in theory reason is achieved in the world.

## Three Mistaken Beliefs About Children

He says three intuitive beliefs drive parenting and education today, and all three are wrong and lead to unhappy children:

1. **Children are blank slates** — empty vessels to fill, which is why three- and four-year-olds in China do math and sports.
2. **Children are "mini-me" versions of their parents** — parents who failed at ballet or tennis want their child to succeed at it by starting earlier.
3. **We must teach critical thinking and creativity** so humans can outperform AI.

### On Critical Thinking and Creativity
- He says critical thinking as taught in schools is reduction and categorization: breaking phenomena into parts, categorizing them, and analyzing their logic. He calls this the wrong approach.
- True creativity means **synthesis** rather than reduction. Following the scientific method strictly, he says, gets you nowhere.
- Creativity is a leap of imagination, which he calls **channeling**. He says great scientists like Newton, Einstein, and Galileo found the scientific method too reductive and rigid, and believed true knowledge comes from speaking to the divine.
- Instead of teaching critical thinking, we should teach children to appreciate their own divinity and live in ways that connect them to the divine.

### Children Are Not Blank Slates
- Children have an intimate connection with the divine and an infinite consciousness from day one.
- **Story:** His one-year-old daughter, who could walk but not talk, slapped her three-and-a-half-year-old brother. When he told her to apologize, she began babbling as if explaining herself, then seemed to realize she couldn't speak. He takes this to show a child's consciousness exceeds their capacity to express it.
- Children are excellent judges of character. Adults socialize themselves into self-deceit (tolerating a bad boss or in-law); children simply avoid bad people. Their conception of the universe is often purer and more truthful than ours.

### Children Come from God
- Children come with a purpose, though neither we nor they may know what it is.
- **Story:** A Toronto doctor with decades of experience told him that, having charted patients' lives for ~40 years, he saw that everything they did led them to the point of having and caring for a particular child.
- The speaker's interpretation: a child's soul may choose its parents because they are best equipped to help it fulfill its purpose, as if things are pre-ordained.
- He applies this to his own family: all three children are very sensitive with specific needs, and despite decades in education and reading hundreds or thousands of books on it, his kids "make no sense" to him. He and his wife (who doesn't work, while his job is flexible) spend most of their time strategizing about the children, and he doesn't believe anyone else could care for them. So he concludes the kids came to them intentionally.

### His Advice to Parents
> You are the best parent for your child. Don't listen to anyone else's advice — not teachers, doctors, or books. Trust your heart, because everything you've experienced — every pain, trauma, and triumph — has prepared you for this moment.

He says the meritocracy tells parents to defer to experts, but parents know best. **The fundamental bond of society is the love between parent and child**, and society should be structured around it. Rather than making everyone the same, we should celebrate diversity, since each of us came here with our own purpose.

### Story: His Middle Child, Bo
- Bo is very stubborn, and his wife predicts he'll be rich someday. The speaker wanted his children to be intellectuals, teachers, or writers who contribute to society rather than "a rich person who steals from society."
- He tried for a long time to teach Bo to be good, but Bo stayed naughty and angered him. He concluded Bo "would rather die than not be him."
- He says children aren't afraid of death because they remember reincarnation and a different life; fear of death is conditioned later.
- Through painful mistakes, he concluded parents should enable and empower children to live the lives they want.

## An Alternative Vision: A New Kind of School

**Two guiding goals:**
1. Make the child's purpose the center of the school.
2. Protect and nourish the love between parent and child.

He says achieving these will make a school successful and unleash enormous human creativity. Three things not taught in schools today should be its foundation:

### 1. Nature
- Nature is the best teacher. Biographies of great scientists show a common pattern of growing up in nature or on farms.
- Cities are abstractions that remove children from the logic of the universe. Observing birds and trees and exploring is "uncovering the mind of God," which permeates the universe and drives further study.
- Children should know where food comes from and how to make it. Today they are kept in rooms, fed food and knowledge with no idea of their source.
- Children left in a forest invent games, run their own experiments, and learn beyond our imagination.
- He imagines a **self-sufficient school community**, off the grid, where adults and children generate their own electricity and grow their own food, letting children see "the entire cosmos in a little village."

### 2. The Occult
- Every human carries three questions: **Where do I come from? What is my purpose? Where am I going?** We all seek a complete mythology of the cosmos.
- Eschatology is powerful because it is a complete story one can participate in, and the powerful always need it to structure society.
- Telling children to learn just to get a job and money doesn't motivate them ("you have money, why not support me forever?"). Telling them where they came from, why they're here, and where they're going activates their drive.
- Religion and the occult should be the core of the curriculum rather than science and math, studying the Kabbalah and Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist, and Shinto traditions as broadly as possible.
- He says all traditions say the same thing: we come from the source, aspire to return to it, and should be grateful for our time here. *Carpe diem* — work hard and enjoy life, because the experience here is unique in the cosmos.

### 3. The Great Books
- He has taught a seminar on Dante, and believes every child should read the Bible, Dante, Homer, Shakespeare, and Milton.
- The great books are "spiritual portals into the divine consciousness." The more you read Dante, the closer you approach the mind of God.
- Nature compels you to understand God; the great books let you fully enter the mind of God.

**Outcome:** A school built on these pillars could educate a new generation of artists, writers, poets, thinkers, and intellectuals to lead humanity forward. He hopes to work on, and maybe build, this over the next few years.

- His own power is **sensitivity**: he says he can hear or know someone's thoughts from far away and has deep insight into people's character and thinking, sometimes beyond what they know themselves. Just as there are warriors, intellectuals, and entrepreneurs, in the occult different people manifest different powers.

### What kind of person would your school produce, and how would they fit into meritocratic hiring?
- We live in a unique era of **mass industrial society** with 8 billion people, where categorizing people via the meritocracy makes sense.
- He believes mass industrial society will collapse due to food shortages and wars. In the transition, the aim shifts from filtering people to maximizing each individual's creative potential.
- He cites Jewish communities as a model: he says that despite being about 2% of the population, they have great influence because they hold that every Jew matters, that God is in each Jewish person, and they invest heavily in ensuring each member has a path to success. He asks why others can't do the same.
- The proposal is a school, perhaps eventually a society, where each person is valued and individuality is celebrated.

### What about single people, if life's purpose centers on family?
- Family doesn't require children; it means caring for people you love.
- Before having children, he found that joy in teaching. Those without families can find the same happiness in volunteering, teaching, and giving to others. His envisioned community is itself a family committed to helping each other.

### What if a child's purpose is to do evil?
- He agrees people in power are often evil psychopaths. He and his wife jokingly call their middle child a "lizard person" because at age 5 he talks constantly about making money and wanted his father to give a concert to monetize his fame. He worried about the child becoming materialistic.
- He concluded the universe doesn't work in straight lines but through thesis, antithesis, synthesis. Some Jewish thinkers believe the path to goodness runs through evil, illustrated by the Bible:
  - **Moses**, most devoted to God, was not rewarded and was denied entry to the promised land, only seeing it from a mountaintop.
  - **Jacob** deceived his brother Esau out of his birthright and blessing, fled into exile, met Rachel, married Leah, and fathered the twelve tribes of Israel.
  - **King David** took the throne from Saul, was in conflict with his son Absalom, took Bathsheba and had her husband killed — yet God's punishment drove him to deep reflection, making him beloved to God.
- Everyone has their own journey and it's not our place to judge, since a seemingly evil journey may lead to God. He says evil "doesn't really exist in the universe" but is created to maintain authority; the universe's fundamental principle is free will. Some Kabbalists believe committing evil forces reflection and a connection to God.

### Will everyone be forced into digital currency?
- The banks currently control money creation through loans; this power is shifting to corporations like Google and Apple that can issue tokens (e.g., Apple Pay), and banks resist.
- On compliance: if the global economy collapses with 20–30% unemployment and governments issue **universal basic income**, that would be digital currency — "If you want to eat, you have to adopt the system." A major crisis would make digital currency and ID easy to implement. "You can't stop this."

### What is your stance on Hong Kong?
- Hong Kong is a cosmopolitan crossroads of culture — open, efficient, well-organized, and probably more resilient than other places.
- He doesn't think Southeast Asia has a good future due to structural contradictions: too many people, too few resources, and many ethnic conflicts.
- Most of the region's fresh water comes from the Tibetan plateau, which China controls. He predicts that within ten years conflicts will be over water rather than oil, and Southeast Asia is very vulnerable.
- Asked whether Hong Kong is a good place to be: "No comment. I am conscious of where I am."

### What do you think of the nuclear family?
- He struggles with this question. He says much of human history was matriarchal: mass industrial society needs patriarchy and property to maximize births, but in a world of scarcity where children are precious, women hold power.
- In matriarchies, he says, family is more fluid: a matriarch has a recognized husband but is free to sleep with others, which disguises paternity so everyone shares responsibility for all children. Such societies still exist, and their members see it as good.
- He personally prefers the nuclear family, as does his wife, though he acknowledges this may reflect his upbringing. He thinks the matriarchal system might make more sense in a crisis requiring population growth.

### What about bad parents who are unprepared or unwilling?
- He mentions a recent case of a Dubai princess high on cocaine lying on a London street while her toddler ran around, requiring state intervention, and acknowledges many irresponsible and abusive parents exist. He grew up in an abusive household.
- He asks why parents are bad: his father hit him partly because he misbehaved, but also because his father faced systematic racial abuse from white superiors at the restaurant where he worked, and carried that frustration home.
- He believes parents want to do well and will if allowed to focus on the child; external stresses, anxieties, and fears disrupt the bond. "Maybe I'm naive," but he thinks people left alone to follow their instincts will do well, and if not, perhaps that too was the universe's plan.
- He calls mass industrial society unique and "very anti-human": with 8 billion people, no one's life is worth anything, and power comes from exploitation rather than creativity. He believes it and the state bureaucracy will break down, leaving reliance on parental love. He says there is currently too much state interference.

### How does one become resilient to evil forces?
- Faith in God brings him resilience and happiness. He believes God, **the Monad**, is the source of everything and is loving, open, generous, and merciful, so "you can commit no wrong in his eyes."
- The toughest problem in life is **self-forgiveness**; shame and guilt are powerful. You need to believe in God — "in Jesus basically" — to forgive yourself.
- Without this, people double down: "I've done so much evil, I'm going to hell anyway." He says the elite think this way, possibly seeking longevity through worshipping Satan or demons.
- Believing God is all-forgiving and that you're here to experience life makes you more willing to take risks and more resilient. Mistakes become part of the learning process rather than sources of shame that trap you in fear.

### How would someone like Elon Musk or Trump find self-forgiveness?
- He paraphrases Trump (noting it isn't an exact quote) as having said something like he's going to hell anyway, so who cares.
- At that power level, he says, people have a different mentality, explained through **Goethe's *Faust***: Faust, seeking absolute knowledge, makes a deal with Mephistopheles — as long as he keeps striving, the devil can't claim his soul. He gains powers, lives an eventful life, becomes satisfied at the end, but God intervenes and sends angels to take him to heaven.
- The elite, he says, believe they do God's work by advancing humanity (Musk with Starlink, Tesla, SpaceX), and that God will forgive any evil as long as they keep striving. Trump, at 80, causing daily drama, follows the same "Faustian" view that life's purpose is struggle — "some people would use the word satanic."

### How would you reverse that mindset?
- **Thought experiment:** In a forest, wolves eat rabbits and deer, but we wouldn't kill or re-engineer them, since overbreeding rabbits would destroy the ecosystem. Perhaps human society similarly always has "wolves" — psychopaths who become the elite — and they help us discover the meaning of life.
- He doesn't think Musk (with many children who he says hate him and changing partners) or Trump is happy, and understanding why lets us choose a different path.
- Only psychopaths rise in mass industrial society, a hierarchy where the top exploits the bottom. He believes it will collapse within 5–10 years, requiring new leaders with imagination and empathy. The elite may retreat to bunkers but will die off, and those dedicated to preserving human knowledge and creativity will thrive.
- **"We are heading into the age of prophets,"** which is why he wants to build a university for prophets.

### Final question: What is happiness?
- Happiness is the **harmonization of who you are** — alignment of body, soul, and spirit, as taught in traditional Chinese medicine and other ancient practices — fulfilling your *telos*, your purpose.
- Unhappiness comes from betraying yourself: doing what parents or teachers made you do, or what you think you must do to get what you want. He says active self-betrayal makes you unhappy and often leads to illnesses like cancer.
- **Example:** Someone who wants to be a baker but is an accountant should go be a baker. Otherwise they may get cancer, have an unhappy family life, and not get along with friends.
- **Closing advice:** Always do what you want to do. Have the freedom and courage to let go of ego and fear, listen to your heart, and don't betray yourself.
