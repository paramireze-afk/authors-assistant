# The Fall of Nikola: Trevor Milton's Story, the Short-Seller Who Took Him Down, and the Money Behind the Prosecution

On July 23, 2025, on Tucker Carlson's show, Trevor Milton — the founder and former CEO of the electric- and hydrogen-truck startup Nikola — sat down to tell his version of one of the strangest corporate collapses in recent American history. Milton was convicted of federal securities and wire fraud in 2022, sentenced to four years in prison in 2023, and pardoned "full and unconditional" by President Trump in March 2025. In the interview, he laid out a much bigger claim than "I was wrongly convicted." He argued that a short-selling firm called Hindenburg Research, run by a little-known 39-year-old named Nate Anderson, essentially manufactured a federal prosecution — feeding damaging material to the Department of Justice, paying witnesses, coordinating with major media outlets, and profiting from every stage of his destruction.

This article walks through Milton's account as he told it, explains the financial mechanics he's talking about (short selling, short reports, whistleblower bounties, venue rules, and so on) in plain English, and — where possible — checks his claims against court records, DOJ statements, and independent reporting. Some of what Milton says is documented and confirmed by outside sources. Some of it is a serious, specific, and so far unverified allegation. And some of it is Milton's own interpretation of events that others read very differently. I'll try to keep those categories clearly separated.

## How Nikola Started

Milton says he built Nikola "out of his basement" in Salt Lake City, initially envisioning a natural-gas-powered semi-truck that evolved into a zero-emission hydrogen truck. His inspiration, he says, came from growing up around locomotives — his father managed a railroad — and from an appreciation for electric drivetrains: their instant torque and their ability to recapture braking energy as electricity (regenerative braking), which he argues makes them especially valuable for heavy, energy-hungry vehicles like long-haul trucks.

**Why hydrogen instead of just batteries?** Milton's explanation, as he gave it to Carlson: hydrogen is abundant (it's half of every water molecule) and, unlike a battery, doesn't require mining large quantities of raw materials. A vehicle burns hydrogen through a fuel cell — a device that runs hydrogen through a membrane that generates electricity, which then powers electric motors, the same way a battery does. Milton's pitch was that hydrogen makes more sense than pure electrification for very heavy applications — semi-trucks, trains, ships, aviation — where the weight and charging time of a huge battery pack becomes impractical, while pure batteries make more sense for passenger cars.

He's also candid that hydrogen infrastructure barely exists — there's essentially nowhere to fill up a hydrogen truck today — and frames Nikola's actual business plan as an infrastructure play as much as a truck-manufacturing one: build the "gas stations" (hydrogen production and fueling sites) alongside the trucks, the same way cell networks had to be built out before cell phones were useful. His ultimate ambition, he says, was to become something like a hydrogen-era oil company — a supplier of the fuel itself, not just the vehicles that burn it.

This is Milton's framing of his own motives and business model; it is not something outside sources dispute in a fundamental way. Nikola genuinely was pursuing both hydrogen trucks and hydrogen fueling infrastructure, and the difficulties of scaling hydrogen infrastructure are real, widely-discussed industry problems, not something unique to Milton's account.

## The Video That Became "the Lie That Destroyed My Life"

The centerpiece of the whole saga — the detail almost everyone who has followed this story knows — is a 2018 promotional video showing Nikola's first prototype truck, the "Nikola One," appearing to drive under its own power. In reality, the truck had no working powertrain integration at the time and was towed to the top of a hill and released to roll down under gravity for the cinematic shot.

Milton's defense of this, in the interview, is nuanced: he says the truck itself was real — the frame, suspension, cab, batteries, axles, and other components were genuinely engineered and built by Nikola's own team, not simply a rebadged competitor's truck — but the vehicle was not yet safe or ready to drive itself for a commercial shoot, so the crew let it roll downhill instead. He compares this to Hollywood using a real Chevy Camaro to portray Bumblebee in the *Transformers* movies: using a real vehicle in a stylized way for a commercial doesn't mean the underlying product doesn't exist. He insists this was disclosed truthfully when Bloomberg later asked him about it directly, and that Bloomberg's published story cut the truthful part of his answer, leaving only the misleading headline.

**What's documented independently:** This episode is genuinely one of the seven core allegations in Hindenburg's original September 2020 report, and it became a central plank of the federal indictment and, later, of a CNBC "American Greed" episode. The government's position — laid out in DOJ's own sentencing announcement — was not simply that the truck rolled downhill, but that Milton *told investors and the public the truck was capable of driving under its own power* when he knew that wasn't true, and that this was one of several similar misrepresentations (others involved Nikola's pickup truck, called the "Badger," and claims about proprietary, in-house battery technology that prosecutors said Nikola was actually buying from outside suppliers). Milton's dispute with Bloomberg over the edited quote is a claim only he makes on the record here; I could not independently verify it, though it is the kind of factual dispute that would show up, if true, in his trial exhibits.

## What Is Short Selling, and What Is a "Short Report"?

This is worth explaining carefully, because the incentive structure is the real heart of Milton's argument.

Ordinary investing is straightforward: you buy shares of a company hoping the price rises, so you can sell later for a profit. A **short seller** does the reverse. They borrow shares they don't own, sell them immediately at today's price, and hope to buy them back later at a *lower* price — pocketing the difference. If the stock falls, the short seller profits; if it rises, they lose money, potentially without limit (a stock can only go to zero, but it can theoretically rise forever, which makes short selling unusually risky).

An **activist short seller** — a category that includes firms like Hindenburg, Muddy Waters, and Citron Research — doesn't just place a bet and wait. It builds a large short position in a company it believes is overvalued or fraudulent, then publishes a detailed, public "short report" laying out its case, explicitly designed to move the market and drive the stock price down quickly, generating profit on the position it has already taken. This is legal in the United States and has an important social function: some of the most consequential corporate frauds of the last few decades (Enron, Wirecard, Luckin Coffee) were flagged early by short sellers before regulators caught up. Short-seller research has genuinely uncovered real problems on multiple occasions, including, arguably, parts of the Nikola story itself — Nikola later backed away from claims that it had developed its battery technology in-house, acknowledging it relied on outside suppliers, and paid $125 million to settle SEC fraud charges without admitting or denying wrongdoing.

But the same structure creates an obvious temptation. A short report is not peer-reviewed, not filed with a regulator before release, and not subject to any of the disclosure requirements that apply to a company's own statements. The short seller is simultaneously the accuser, the author of the "evidence," and the direct financial beneficiary of the accusation landing. Milton's summary of this to Carlson — "they'll mix in 5% truth and 95% lies just to scare the market" — is obviously self-interested hyperbole given his own conviction, but the underlying incentive problem he's describing is real and widely discussed by finance academics and regulators: nothing structurally prevents an activist short seller from exaggerating, selectively presenting facts, or timing a report's release to maximize market panic rather than to maximize accuracy.

## Hindenburg Research and Nate Anderson

Hindenburg Research was a small, until-then largely unknown short-selling research firm founded in 2017 by Nathan (Nate) Anderson, an American who had studied at Hebrew University in Israel and worked there as a paramedic before moving into finance, including a stint at FactSet. The firm — named after the 1937 Hindenburg airship disaster, evoking a disaster that was, in Anderson's framing, both foreseeable and preventable — built its reputation on a series of aggressive reports accusing publicly traded companies of fraud, most famously later taking on the Adani Group (one of India's largest conglomerates), activist investor Carl Icahn's Icahn Enterprises, and India's market regulator chief. Hindenburg voluntarily shut down in January 2025, with Anderson citing the completion of its "pipeline" of investigations rather than any external pressure; multiple retrospectives on the firm's closure cite the September 2020 Nikola report as its breakout, most consequential piece of work.

Hindenburg's Nikola report, published September 10, 2020, accused Nikola of being "an intricate fraud built on dozens of lies," identifying Milton personally as chiefly responsible. Two days after publication, Milton stepped down as executive chairman. Nikola's stock, which had traded above $90 a share at its June 2020 peak (giving the pre-revenue company a market capitalization briefly larger than Ford's), collapsed and continued declining for years afterward; as of early 2025, shares traded around $1, and Nikola filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in February 2025.

Milton's claims about Anderson personally go considerably further than the documented record and are worth stating precisely as his own allegations, not established fact:

- That Hindenburg paid contractors and former associates of Nikola — including, he says, roughly half a dozen people — for information used in its report and, separately, gave some of them a cut of trading profits.
- That Hindenburg was in direct communication with federal prosecutors *before* releasing its report, effectively pre-coordinating a Department of Justice response to a report that hadn't been published yet.
- That the Eastern District of New York initially examined the allegations, visited Nikola's facility, and concluded there was no case — and that Hindenburg (or people working with Hindenburg) then approached federal prosecutors in the Southern District of New York, who took up the matter instead.
- That Nate Anderson personally profited between $30 million and $100 million from his short position, according to what Milton says has emerged in discovery from a related civil lawsuit.
- That a witness at Milton's criminal trial — a former outside contractor named Paul Lackey, who Milton says worked on Nikola's batteries and had visited Nikola's facility only once — was paid $600,000 by Hindenburg and was positioned to make millions more through a "whistleblower group," and that this was disclosed on the stand at trial.
- That, before trial, Anderson told federal prosecutors that Milton was a "Russian asset" attempting to hack Hindenburg and the U.S. government, prompting an FBI sting operation in New York City designed to catch Milton and an alleged Russian contact — a sting that, in Milton's telling, involved either Anderson or an Anderson lookalike, ended when the target realized something was wrong and fled (allegedly striking an FBI vehicle in the process), and was captured on video that Milton says his attorneys have seen but that has never become public.

**On verification:** The Paul Lackey/whistleblower payment claim is at least partially corroborated by outside reporting — CNBC's own "American Greed" episode about Milton refers to "whistleblowers" whose report, developed alongside Hindenburg, formed the basis of the case, and to a civil lawsuit brought on behalf of those whistleblowers by attorney Mark Pugsley; this indicates that paid informants working with Hindenburg were a real and acknowledged part of how the case against Milton was built, which lends some credibility to Milton's broader claim about paid sources, even though the $600,000 figure and Lackey's specific role are not things I could independently confirm from public reporting. The claim that a DOJ chief witness was being paid by the same short seller whose report triggered the prosecution is, if accurate, a genuinely serious conflict-of-interest question — and it is the kind of detail that would appear in a public trial transcript, which Milton says it does.

The **Russian-asset FBI sting story**, the claim that Hindenburg pre-coordinated its report with DOJ before publication, and the claim about Eastern District declining the case before Southern District picked it up, are, as far as I can determine, not corroborated anywhere in public reporting, court opinions, or DOJ statements. These are Milton's account alone, told for what appears to be the first time publicly in this interview. That doesn't make them false — but readers should understand these are uncorroborated allegations from someone with an obvious personal stake in the story, made in a friendly interview format, about matters (an active FBI operation, sealed prosecutorial communications) that would be extraordinarily difficult for an outside journalist to verify independently even if true.

### Why the underlying incentive question matters

Whatever the truth of Milton's specific claims, he is pointing at a real structural tension that's worth taking seriously on its own terms, separate from his case. Insider trading law makes it illegal to trade on material nonpublic information obtained from a company's insiders. A short seller who pays a company's former employees or contractors for information and then trades on it occupies a genuinely murky area: paying someone for information and then shorting the stock is not automatically illegal the way classic insider trading is, particularly if the information itself isn't "inside" information in the legal sense (e.g., if it's information about how a product actually works, rather than confidential financial results). But the closer that paid information resembles material nonpublic information obtained through a breach of duty, the closer the arrangement gets to conduct the SEC has prosecuted in other contexts. Milton's framing — "if I pay your employees for information and invest based on it, I go to prison; if I pay them and bet against you, that's supposedly fine" — oversimplifies the law, but it identifies a genuine asymmetry that securities regulators and legal scholars have debated: the rules built to prevent people from profiting off a company's rise are more developed than the rules governing people who profit off engineering, or accelerating, its fall.

## The SEC and the Criminal Case

Independent of anything Hindenburg said or did, the SEC opened its own investigation, and it produced consequences separate from Milton's personal criminal case. In late 2021, Nikola itself — as a corporation — agreed to pay $125 million to settle SEC charges that it had defrauded investors, without the company admitting or denying the SEC's findings, a standard structure in SEC settlements. That is a matter of public record and is not something Milton disputes; his trial defense was about his own personal intent, not about whether Nikola made statements that turned out to be inaccurate.

Milton, personally, was indicted by federal prosecutors in the Southern District of New York in July 2021 on two counts of securities fraud and two counts of wire fraud. Prosecutors alleged he made false statements about "nearly all aspects" of Nikola's business: that the company had built its "Badger" pickup truck from the ground up when it was actually developed with a partner; that it had developed proprietary battery technology in-house when it was buying batteries elsewhere; and that the Nikola One prototype was a functioning truck when, according to prosecutors, it had not been completed, tested, or validated at its 2016 unveiling.

At trial in September–October 2022, Milton's defense argued he had no intent to defraud investors and that other Nikola executives, including the company's general counsel, had approved his public statements. After a roughly one-month trial, the jury deliberated for about five hours and convicted him on one count of securities fraud and two counts of wire fraud, acquitting him on a second securities fraud count. Notably — and this is independently documented, not just Milton's claim — several jurors told the Wall Street Journal afterward that they did not believe Milton had acted with criminal intent, even though intent to deceive was a required legal element of the charges they convicted him on; Milton's lawyers used exactly this inconsistency, along with allegedly confusing jury instructions, in a subsequent motion for a new trial.

**On the juror issue Milton raised in the interview:** this part of his account is substantially corroborated by outside sources, not just his own telling. Milton's lawyers did formally argue, in December 2022 court filings, that one juror lied during jury selection about not using social media, when in fact — according to Bloomberg's reporting on the same filings — her Twitter and Facebook accounts were, in the paper's words, "riddled with attacks on wealthy executives." Prosecutors opposed the motion, and Judge Edgardo Ramos ultimately denied it and proceeded to sentencing. The other specific details Milton gave Carlson about this juror — her comments to media about not wanting an alternate juror seated, her alleged social-media posts about "the billionaire class," her praise for Elon Musk — are consistent in kind with the underlying dispute but go beyond what I could confirm from public reporting; the core fact that a documented juror-conduct dispute occurred and was formally litigated, however, is real.

Milton was sentenced in December 2023 to four years in prison and a $1 million fine — a sentence federal sentencing guidelines suggested could have been 27 to 33 years, and which prosecutors sought closer to 11 years for. Milton remained free on a $100 million bond throughout his appeal, and prosecutors also separately sought roughly $680 million in restitution to shareholders in the weeks before the pardon; because of the pardon, that restitution can no longer be ordered.

## Kirkland & Ellis, Legal Fees, and the "Chaos Economy"

One of the more pointed arguments Milton makes concerns Nikola's outside law firm, Kirkland & Ellis, which represented the company (not Milton personally) once the investigation began. Milton's account: prosecutors pressured Kirkland to get Nikola executives to testify against him under threat of their own indictment; Kirkland then cut him off from company communications, employees, and documents even though he was contractually entitled to them, and effectively told him to sue if he wanted access — knowing litigation would take years he didn't have. He says he personally paid over $80 million in legal fees, while Nikola (the company) paid Kirkland over $100 million. His argument is essentially an incentive critique: a law firm that quietly resolves a government inquiry might bill a few million dollars, while a firm that manages years of indictments, shareholder lawsuits, and government investigations can bill vastly more — meaning the law firm's financial interest is not obviously aligned with a fast, quiet resolution.

This is a claim about internal corporate and privileged communications that, by its nature, is very hard for outside reporting to verify or refute; I found no independent corroboration or denial of the specific $80 million/$100 million figures, though Milton's broader point — that large firms sometimes earn dramatically more from prolonged legal chaos than from swift resolution — is a structural observation about the legal industry that outside commentators have made in other high-profile corporate scandals as well, not something unique to his case.

Separately worth noting, because Milton doesn't mention it but it bears on the story's "financial interests intertwined with government" theme he's pushing: Milton's defense at trial was led by Marc Mukasey and, according to Reuters, later legal filings, Brad Bondi — brother of Pam Bondi, who became U.S. Attorney General under the second Trump administration and was in that role at the time of Milton's pardon. That overlapping relationship, along with the more than $1.8 million Milton and his wife donated to Trump's 2024 campaign efforts shortly before the pardon, is something multiple news outlets (Axios, the New York Times, USA Today) have flagged as a relevant, documented fact about the pardon's context — not an allegation against Milton, simply a piece of the record that a reader trying to evaluate the full picture should have.

## Venue: Why New York?

Milton devotes considerable time to a genuinely interesting legal question: why was he tried in the Southern District of New York (Manhattan) when he says he had essentially no personal connection to New York — he lived and worked in Arizona and Utah, where Nikola's operations were based.

The legal mechanism he describes is real: the U.S. Constitution's venue clause generally requires that a criminal defendant be tried in the district where the alleged crime occurred, a protection with roots in colonial-era grievances about the British Crown hauling defendants to distant, unsympathetic venues. Prosecutors argued venue was proper in the Southern District of New York because Nikola's stock traded on markets whose infrastructure and communications pass through that district — a legal theory that has been used in other securities cases involving nationally traded stocks, though it's a genuinely contested and, in Milton's telling, unusually aggressive application of it, given that virtually none of the underlying conduct he was charged with took place in New York.

Milton argues — and this is his opinion, not an established legal fact — that Southern District of New York juries are unusually likely to convict, citing what he describes as an over 90% conviction rate, and that this made New York a strategically preferable venue for prosecutors regardless of where the alleged conduct occurred. His broader proposal — that criminal defendants should be able to choose their own venue — is a genuine, if unconventional, reform idea; it is not how the law currently works, and would represent a significant departure from existing venue doctrine, which is generally set by where the crime is alleged to have occurred, not by defendant preference.

## What Happened to the Company, and to Milton's Life

Independent of the truth of his more explosive claims, several outcomes here are simply documented fact: Nikola never became a mass-market truck manufacturer at anything close to the scale Milton once described. It began limited production in 2022 and, according to reporting on its bankruptcy filing, produced fewer than 1,000 electric trucks total before filing for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in February 2025 and beginning to liquidate its assets. Shareholders — including many retail investors who bought in during the 2020 SPAC boom, when Nikola's valuation briefly exceeded $30 billion — lost the overwhelming majority of their investment; the stock, which peaked above $90, was trading around $1 by the time of the bankruptcy filing.

Milton's account of the personal toll — his wife's serious autoimmune and diabetic health complications, which he says arose after a medical error; the years spent living partly in New York during trial; the psychological weight of a potential decades-long sentence — is his own testimony and not the kind of claim that lends itself to independent verification, though it is consistent in tone with the acknowledged severity of what he went through (an actual four-year prison sentence and a $100 million bond are not disputed facts).

## What's Happened Since the Interview

Three developments from the year after the interview aired are worth flagging, because they complicate or push back on parts of Milton's account.

First, Nate Anderson himself responded publicly within a day, in a lengthy post calling the interview "bizarre and fantastical" and disputing several of Milton's specific factual claims — including denying that Hindenburg paid for inside information and disputing Milton's $30–100 million profit estimate ("not even close," Anderson wrote). Anderson also pointed to context he said Milton had omitted: that Milton and his wife had donated roughly $1.8 million to Trump's 2024 campaign effort shortly before the pardon, and that two women had separately filed sexual-assault complaints against Milton with Utah authorities in 2020, alleging incidents from 1999 and 2004 when they were 15. Those complaints, which Milton has strongly and consistently denied, did not result in criminal charges as far as public records show. This dispute is a useful reminder of the limits of a one-sided interview format: Carlson did not raise these facts or press Milton on Anderson's specific rebuttals during the conversation, and investor Bill Ackman was among those who publicly criticized Carlson afterward for not doing so.

Second, Milton's civil lawsuit against CNBC and Hindenburg — the one he told Carlson had "just cleared the motion to dismiss," at a stage when a trial-level judge had allowed part of the case to proceed — did not ultimately succeed. In December 2025, a New Jersey appellate court threw out the lawsuit entirely, ruling that Milton's claims were, in substance, ordinary defamation claims filed well past New Jersey's one-year statute of limitations, and that his attempt to relabel them as "trade libel" (which has a longer filing window) didn't hold up legally. Because the case was dismissed under New Jersey's anti-SLAPP statute — a law designed to penalize meritless lawsuits targeting protected speech — the court ordered Milton to pay the defendants' legal fees; in July 2026, a judge set that amount at roughly $286,000 to CNBC and $295,000 to Hindenburg, over $580,000 combined. This doesn't resolve the underlying factual dispute about whether CNBC's or Hindenburg's reporting was accurate, but the lawsuit Milton pointed to as validation ended, instead, in a dismissal and a financial penalty against him.

Third, it's worth being clear about what caused Nikola's stock decline and eventual bankruptcy, since Milton's account attributes essentially all of it to the short-selling campaign and prosecution. Nikola's own $125 million SEC settlement, its acknowledgment that it had overstated its in-house battery development, and the production shortfalls that preceded its February 2025 bankruptcy filing are facts that exist independently of anything Hindenburg said, and point toward real problems with Nikola's public statements and execution, not simply a financially motivated smear campaign. That doesn't necessarily contradict Milton's claims about paid informants or prosecutorial coordination — both things could be true at once — but it's a reminder that "Hindenburg profited from Nikola's collapse" and "Nikola's public statements were inaccurate" are not mutually exclusive claims, and the second has considerably more independent corroboration than the first.

## The Larger Question: Who Has an Incentive to Find the Truth?

Stepping back from the specifics of Milton's case, the interview raises a question that's worth taking seriously on its own terms, independent of whether you find Milton personally credible.

Activist short selling exists because ordinary market mechanisms are often slow to correct for fraud or overvaluation — a company that lies about its products can, for a while, keep its stock price inflated simply because most investors and analysts take public statements at face value. Short sellers who do genuine forensic work — reading regulatory filings closely, interviewing former employees, checking physical facilities, cross-referencing supplier relationships — have caught real fraud that took years for regulators to act on. That's a legitimate, valuable function, and Hindenburg's other work (on the Adani Group, for instance) has generally held up to scrutiny.

But the same financial structure that rewards accurate fraud detection also rewards *exaggeration and selective presentation*, because the short seller's profit depends on market perception collapsing quickly and dramatically, not on being narrowly, technically correct. A short report doesn't need to be defamatory or legally false to be effective — it can be accurate on its most damaging claims and misleading in its overall impression, front-loading the most inflammatory allegations while burying caveats, exactly the kind of imbalance Milton alleges (without full verification) in Bloomberg's editing of his interview answer. Nothing in securities law requires a short report to be balanced, fair, or complete — only that it not contain knowing, material falsehoods that a court would recognize as actionable fraud or defamation, a considerably lower bar.

Layer onto that the possibility — real in Milton's case, according to reporting on the CNBC "American Greed" episode itself — that some of the people supplying information to a short seller are financially compensated contractors or "whistleblowers" with a direct stake in the stock's decline, and the incentive alignment gets murkier still: the source, the researcher, and the eventual government witness can all, in principle, be the same interconnected group of people with a shared financial outcome riding on a conviction. That doesn't mean the underlying fraud allegations were false — Nikola's own SEC settlement suggests they weren't entirely false — but it does mean the chain of "who benefits if this story is told a certain way" is longer and more tangled than the simple picture of a disinterested government prosecuting a straightforward fraud.

Milton's case is, in that sense, a useful (if one-sided) illustration of a genuine structural question that doesn't have an easy answer: modern financial markets, media, and law enforcement all depend on some mix of private actors — journalists, researchers, whistleblowers, short sellers — surfacing information that regulators alone would be too slow or too under-resourced to find. But every one of those private actors can also have a financial or reputational stake in how the story turns out, and current law does relatively little to require that those overlapping interests be disclosed, audited, or checked against each other before they combine into a criminal prosecution. Whether that describes what actually happened to Trevor Milton — a legitimate fraud case that also happened to financially benefit several private parties, or a fraud case substantially manufactured by those same private parties for profit — is a question this interview answers only from one side of it.
