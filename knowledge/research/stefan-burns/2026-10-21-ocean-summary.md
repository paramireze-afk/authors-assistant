# Is the Sun Cooking the Pacific?

The Pacific Ocean has a fever, and it's getting worse.

In mid-October 2026, the weekly Niño 3.4 index, the patch of the equatorial Pacific everybody watches to measure El Niño, hit **+3.3°C** above normal. That's not a strong El Niño. That's a monster. The 1997–98 event, the one people still talk about, the one that gave us the January '98 ice storm and a year of rain dumped on California in a month, peaked at around +3.6°C. This one is on track to blow past it.

And it isn't done. The WMO's October outlook has the October through December average reaching roughly **+3.7°C**, with the peak not expected until December. Every one of the 27 models in Columbia's IRI ensemble is calling for a "very strong" El Niño that holds into late spring 2027. Back in July, global sea surface temperatures had already set an all-time record for the month. Europe spent the summer burying tens of thousands of people killed by heat.

The official explanation is clean. Trade winds weaken, warm water sloshes east, greenhouse warming sits underneath the whole thing like a stove burner that never gets turned off.

But there's another explanation floating around, and it points up. At the sun.

## The Space Weatherman

Stefan Burns describes himself as a "space weatherman." He's a geophysicist who runs a YouTube channel tracking solar flares, coronal mass ejections, geomagnetic storms, planetary alignments and earthquakes. In his own words, he does reports on what's happening with the Earth energetically.

His worldview is pretty simple at its core. The Earth isn't a closed box sitting in a vacuum. It's plugged into the sun, constantly getting hit with streams of high-speed charged particles, and that energy has to go somewhere. Auroras are the visible part. Burns thinks a lot more of it shows up in places we don't usually connect to the sun: in the weather, in the ground, in our bodies.

He's also not shy about the spiritual side of it. His channel frames all of this as understanding "the quality of the energies" of the solar system, as part of a consciousness journey, and he sells grounding and earthing products alongside the forecasts. That's worth knowing going in. It doesn't make him wrong, but it's part of the picture.

## El Niño as the Earth's Heartbeat

The clearest summary of how Burns sees El Niño comes secondhand, from a wellness blog that walks through his ideas, so take the exact framing as the blogger's.

The argument goes like this. Solar storms stir up the energetic currents high in the atmosphere. That changes the winds. When the winds change, the warm water that normally piles up in the western Pacific breaks loose and surges east toward the Americas. That surge is El Niño.

Then there's the part coming from below. Deep under the ocean, tectonic movement and volcanic fault lines are bleeding geothermal heat into the water. So you get energy coming down from the sun and energy coming up from the Earth, meeting in the ocean.

In this view, El Niño isn't a climate change symptom or a random Pacific tantrum. It's described as part of "the natural heartbeat of the Earth," driven by solar cycles that were running long before anybody burned a gallon of gas.

And the timing argument is obvious. We're sitting at or just past the peak of Solar Cycle 25, the most energetic stretch of the sun's 11-year rhythm. The ocean breaks every record we have, at the same time the sun is at full throttle. Coincidence?

## Something Is Off With the Solar Wind

Burns watches the solar wind the way Ryan Hall watches radar.

In September he flagged what he called an unusual space weather impact: strong, rapid swings in the speed of the incoming plasma, with the interplanetary magnetic field running at about **15 nanotesla instead of the usual 5**. Three times normal. He followed it with a question that sums up his whole approach:

> "Could solar weather impact the way someone responds to the heat?"

That's the thread running through everything he does. The sun isn't just lighting the planet. It's acting on it, and on us.

## The Shield

Here's where it gets darker, and it isn't only Burns saying it.

Earth's magnetic field is the thing standing between us and the full force of that solar wind. And it's been doing strange things. The magnetic north pole has broken into a run toward Siberia. A weak spot over the South Atlantic, where satellites already glitch as they pass through, has been growing.

Burns has been asked about this directly. In a recent interview with Neal K. Shah, the conversation went straight to whether a weakening field is making us more vulnerable to earthquakes, and whether the sun, fracking or even HAARP could be playing a role in a run of big quakes, including a Venezuelan doublet where two major earthquakes hit 39 seconds apart and the second was bigger than the first.

The solar-earthquake link isn't something he invented, either. There's a line of research going back years, Russian and Central Asian studies especially, finding that earthquake counts jump after sudden increases in solar wind speed and after magnetic storms. Odintsov and colleagues reported in 2007 that the number of earthquakes correlated directly with sudden spikes in solar wind velocity. It sits outside the mainstream, but it's in the literature.

Now, to be fair to Burns, he doesn't actually go full doom on the magnetic field. When Straight Arrow News interviewed him, he said that despite all the worry, Earth's field is still fundamentally very strong. Even the space weatherman isn't saying the shield is about to fail.

What's harder to shake is what's happening on the other side of the equation.

## The Sun Is Changing Too

The sun's own magnetic field is fading at the poles.

In March 2026, the field at the sun's north pole measured about negative 0.72 gauss. A study this year compared that against the same point in the previous five solar cycles, about six decades of records. It was the weakest of all of them.

That polar field matters. It seeds the strength of the next solar cycle, and it inflates the heliosphere, the giant magnetic bubble that shields the whole solar system from galactic cosmic rays. And this isn't a one-cycle blip. Solar activity hit its modern high point in the mid-20th century, around Solar Cycle 19, and has been sliding downward since, back to levels the sun hasn't seen in about a century.

So picture it: a sun whose magnetic backbone is weakening cycle after cycle, an Earth whose own magnetic field is wandering and thinning in spots, and an ocean running hotter than anything in the instrument record. You don't have to believe any single piece of the cosmic explanation to feel like the whole system is shifting under our feet.

## Where It Gets Hard

I want to be honest about where Burns's argument has to do the heavy lifting, because this is exactly where the mainstream will push back.

The energy problem is real. The charged particles from the sun carry very little energy compared to sunlight itself, and most of it gets absorbed way up in the upper atmosphere, which is why we get auroras instead of boiling seas. Geothermal heat leaking from the seafloor is real too, but on average it's tiny next to what the sun delivers as light. For Burns's version to work, there has to be a mechanism that takes that solar particle energy and gets it down to the trade winds, or there has to be a big increase in heat coming out of the ocean floor that nobody is measuring.

The timing isn't perfect either. The 1997–98 monster happened near solar minimum, not maximum. And the "weakening sun" story cuts both ways: if the sun has been getting quieter for 70 years, it's strange that it would be the thing heating the ocean to record levels.

There's a version of this that Burns would probably answer with resonance, electrical coupling, things that don't show up neatly in an energy budget. That's where his framework turns spiritual, and where a lot of people will get off the train.

## The Question I'm Left With

Here's what I can't let go of.

Everybody agrees on the mechanism of El Niño. The winds weaken, the warm water rushes east. The fight is over what changes the winds, and where the extra heat came from in the first place.

The official answer is carbon dioxide, plus natural variability. Burns's answer is the sun and the Earth itself. Somewhere in between is a set of research on solar wind, magnetic storms and earthquakes that most people have never heard of.

What I'd really want to see is someone take the solar wind data Burns tracks every day and line it up against the wind bursts that kick off El Niño events. Not just this one. 1982, 1997, 2015, 2023, 2026. If the space weather signal is there, it should show up more than once.

Until then, the Pacific keeps heating up, the forecasts keep getting worse, and the peak hasn't even hit yet.
