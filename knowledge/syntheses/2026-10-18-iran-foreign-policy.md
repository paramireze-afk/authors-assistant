# Why They Chant: A Century of Western Policy Toward Iran and the Logic of Blowback

*This piece presents the case as non-interventionist critics like Scott Horton and Daryl Cooper make it. It doesn't quote them; it lays out the argument in the spirit of their work. A section at the end covers the strongest counterarguments, and items marked **⚑** are flagged as contested or debated.*

---

## The Question Nobody in Washington Asks

When an American president cites Iran's "Death to America" chant, it's offered as self-explanatory proof that Iran's rulers are irrational fanatics who hate America for what it is. The non-interventionist tradition asks a different question: **what did we do?**

That isn't the same as saying America deserves to be hated, or that Iran's government is good. It's the question any serious strategist should ask about an adversary: what grievances does the other side hold, which are real, and which of our own policies keep producing new enemies?

The CIA itself has a word for this. **"Blowback"** first appeared in a 1954 internal CIA history of the operation that overthrew Iran's government the year before. The term meant the unintended consequences of covert operations, which the public would experience without ever knowing their cause. Political scientist Chalmers Johnson later built a book around the idea, published a year before 9/11.

Americans usually start the story in 1979. Critics of U.S. policy usually start it in 1953. But to understand why Iranians reacted to 1953 the way they did, you have to go back further, to the half-century when Britain and Russia treated Persia as a possession, and to one year in particular: **1917**.

---

## Part I: The British Century (1872–1951)

### Concessions and the Price of Weakness

By the late 1800s, Persia's Qajar monarchy was weak, broke, and wedged between two empires: Russia pushing down from the north, and Britain guarding the approaches to India from the south. The shahs raised money by selling **concessions**, exclusive rights to a nation's resources, to foreigners.

In 1890, the shah granted a British subject a monopoly over Persia's entire tobacco trade. The result was the **Tobacco Protest** of 1891–92: a nationwide boycott, backed by a religious edict from the leading Shi'ite cleric, that forced the shah to cancel the deal. It was the first great alliance between Iran's clergy, merchants, and ordinary people against foreign economic control. That alliance would return in 1906, in 1953, and in 1979.

### Oil: The D'Arcy Concession

In 1901, British financier **William Knox D'Arcy** won a sixty-year concession to search for and extract oil across most of Persia, with Persia receiving 16% of net profits. Oil was struck at **Masjed Soleyman** in 1908, the first major find in the Middle East. The **Anglo-Persian Oil Company** (later Anglo-Iranian, and eventually BP) was formed the next year.

In 1914, on the eve of World War I, the British government bought a majority stake in the company at the urging of **Winston Churchill**, then First Lord of the Admiralty, who was converting the Royal Navy from coal to oil. From then on, Persian oil wasn't just a business interest. It was a pillar of British imperial power, and the British government itself owned it.

### 1906–1911: Persia's Democratic Revolution, Strangled

In 1906, the **Constitutional Revolution** forced the shah to accept a constitution and an elected parliament, the **Majlis**, one of the first in Asia. It was a genuine democratic movement.

A year later, in 1907, Britain and Russia signed the **Anglo-Russian Convention**, dividing Persia into spheres of influence: Russia in the north, Britain in the southeast, a neutral zone between. Nobody asked the Persians. To the new constitutionalists, it was a betrayal by Britain, which they'd seen as a liberal ally against the shah.

In 1911, the Majlis hired an American, **Morgan Shuster**, to reform Persia's chaotic finances. He took the job seriously, which threatened Russian and British interests. Russia issued an ultimatum demanding his dismissal, sent in troops, and in December 1911 the parliament was shut down. Shuster wrote a book about it with a title that summed up the experience: *The Strangling of Persia*.

There's an irony here that Horton's camp would underline. In those years, Americans in Persia were often seen as the good guys, people like Shuster, or Howard Baskerville, an American teacher killed in 1909 fighting alongside the constitutionalists and still honored in Iran. The United States began the century with goodwill in Iran. It had to work to lose it.

### 1917: The Year Everything Turned

World War I ended the Ottoman Empire, broke the Russian Empire, and redrew the Middle East. Many of the threads that still tie the region in knots run through a single year.

**Persia: occupied, starving, and neutral.** Persia declared neutrality in 1914, and it made no difference. Russian, British, and Ottoman armies fought across its territory. By 1917, the occupation was compounded by crop failures, armies requisitioning food, and then epidemics of cholera, typhus, and the 1918 influenza. The **Great Persian Famine of 1917–1919** killed an enormous number of people; estimates range from hundreds of thousands to several million. **⚑1** Iranians remember it as a catastrophe imposed on a neutral country by foreign armies fighting someone else's war.

**The Russian Revolution.** In 1917 the Russian Empire collapsed. The Bolsheviks pulled Russian troops out of Persia and publicly renounced the tsar's imperial privileges there. Britain moved to fill the vacuum, with forces such as the South Persia Rifles and, in 1918, a British expedition that crossed northern Persia toward Baku. By the war's end, Britain was the dominant power across the country.

**Sykes-Picot exposed.** In November 1917, the Bolsheviks published the tsar's secret treaties, including the **Sykes-Picot Agreement** of 1916, in which Britain and France had quietly agreed to carve up the Ottoman Arab lands between them. Arabs who had been promised independence in exchange for revolting against the Ottomans learned they had been dealt away in advance.

**The Balfour Declaration.** On November 2, 1917, British Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour wrote that the British government favored the establishment in Palestine of a "national home for the Jewish people," while stating that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of Palestine's existing non-Jewish communities, who were then the overwhelming majority. **⚑3** The declaration became the foundation of the British Mandate for Palestine and, ultimately, of the conflict that would shape the region for the next century, including American support for Israel, which bin Laden would later name among his core grievances.

**Baghdad and Jerusalem fall.** In March 1917, British forces captured **Baghdad**, and their commander proclaimed that they came as liberators, not conquerors. Three years later, Iraqis rose in a major revolt against British rule, which Britain suppressed partly through aerial bombing of villages. In December 1917, General Allenby's forces took **Jerusalem**. The modern states of Iraq, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Israel would all be drawn out of the territory Britain and France now held.

For the non-interventionist reading, 1917 is when the region's modern pattern was set: great powers making promises they didn't intend to keep, drawing borders for their own convenience, and treating local peoples as obstacles to manage. When the United States inherited Britain's role after World War II, it inherited this history too.

### 1919–1921: A Protectorate in All but Name

In 1919, Britain's Lord Curzon negotiated the **Anglo-Persian Agreement**, which would have put British advisers in charge of Persia's army and finances. Persian officials who signed it were paid large sums by the British. The agreement provoked such outrage that the Majlis never ratified it. Notably, the United States publicly objected to it.

In February 1921, a Cossack officer named **Reza Khan** marched on Tehran in a coup. British officers in Persia, including General Edmund Ironside, encouraged and facilitated it; how much London directed it is still debated. **⚑2** By 1925, Reza Khan had made himself shah and founded the **Pahlavi dynasty**. He was a modernizer and an autocrat who built roads, railways, and a modern army while crushing dissent.

### 1933–1951: The Oil Grievance Deepens

In 1933, Reza Shah tried to cancel the D'Arcy concession, then accepted a renegotiated deal that extended it to 1993. Iranians watched the company pay more in taxes to the British government than it paid in royalties to Iran, while Iranian oil workers in Abadan lived in slums beside the British managers' compound.

### 1941: The Shah Installed by Invasion

In August 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union **invaded Iran** to secure its oil and a supply route to the USSR, and because they thought Reza Shah too friendly to Germany. Iran was neutral again; it didn't matter again. Reza Shah was forced to abdicate in favor of his 21-year-old son, **Mohammad Reza Pahlavi**. In 1943, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin met in Tehran to plan the war's endgame — in an occupied country.

This is the man the U.S. would restore to power in 1953. To many Iranians, he had been put on the throne by foreign armies the first time, and he would be put back by a foreign intelligence service the second.

## Part II: The American Era in Iran

### 1953: The Original Sin

In 1951, Iran's parliament voted to nationalize the oil industry, and Prime Minister **Mohammad Mossadegh**, a secular nationalist, became enormously popular for standing up to Anglo-Iranian. Britain responded with an embargo and pressed Washington for help.

The Truman administration resisted. The Eisenhower administration, framing Mossadegh as a communist risk, agreed. **⚑4** In August 1953 the CIA and British intelligence organized **Operation Ajax** (TPAJAX): paying crowds, bribing officers and politicians, and planting propaganda. Mossadegh was overthrown and spent the rest of his life under house arrest.

The U.S. government officially acknowledged the CIA's role in documents declassified in 2013, sixty years after the fact. Iranians never needed the confirmation.

Seen against the British century, 1953 wasn't an isolated event to Iranians. It was the latest in a fifty-year pattern: concessions, partition, occupation, a strangled parliament, an imposed shah, and now an elected government overthrown — this time with America taking Britain's place. That's why the U.S. went from being seen as Iran's friend to being seen as the new empire.

### 1953–1979: The Shah and SAVAK

The Shah ruled as an increasingly absolute monarch for twenty-six years, armed and backed by Washington. His secret police, **SAVAK**, trained with American and Israeli help, became notorious for surveillance, imprisonment, and torture of dissidents.

In 1964, the Shah's government granted U.S. military personnel legal immunity in Iran. A cleric named **Ruhollah Khomeini** denounced it as a "capitulation," using the very word Iranians had used for the humiliating legal privileges foreigners enjoyed in the Qajar era. He was exiled for it, and the speech made him a national figure.

### 1979: Revolution and Hostages

The Shah was overthrown in early 1979. In October, the Carter administration admitted him to the U.S. for cancer treatment. Many Iranians, remembering 1953, feared Washington was preparing to restore him once more. In November, students stormed the U.S. embassy and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days.

Horton-style accounts don't excuse the hostage-taking. They do insist on the sequence. Americans remember 1979 as the beginning. Iranians remember it as the end of a story that started generations earlier.

### 1980–1988: Backing Saddam

In September 1980, Iraq's **Saddam Hussein** invaded Iran. The war lasted eight years and killed hundreds of thousands of people, with trench warfare, human-wave assaults, and attacks on cities.

The United States tilted toward Iraq:

- In 1982 it removed Iraq from the list of state sponsors of terrorism, opening the way for aid.
- In December 1983, special envoy **Donald Rumsfeld** met Saddam in Baghdad to improve relations.
- The U.S. provided Iraq with credits, dual-use technology, and battlefield intelligence.
- Iraq used **chemical weapons** against Iranian troops and, at Halabja in 1988, against Iraqi Kurdish civilians. Declassified CIA files reported by *Foreign Policy* in 2013 indicate U.S. officials provided targeting intelligence in 1988 knowing Iraq was likely to use chemical weapons in those offensives. **⚑5**

At the same time, in the **Iran–Contra** scandal, the Reagan administration secretly sold weapons to Iran, partly to free hostages held by Hezbollah in Lebanon and partly to fund Nicaraguan rebels. Critics see in this not a coherent strategy but cynical manipulation of both sides.

Iran's own leaders, including Parliament Speaker Ghalibaf, who fought as an IRGC commander in that war, still invoke it today. Thousands of Iranian veterans live with the lung damage from mustard gas. To them, the U.S. was a co-belligerent. And once again, as in 1917 and 1941, Iranians experienced the outside world as indifferent to their suffering.

### 1988: Iran Air Flight 655

In July 1988, the cruiser **USS Vincennes** shot down **Iran Air Flight 655**, a civilian Airbus on a scheduled route, killing all **290 people aboard**, including 66 children. The ship was inside Iranian territorial waters at the time.

The U.S. called it a tragic mistake. It never formally apologized, though it paid compensation in 1996 without admitting liability. The ship's commander later received a medal for his service during that tour. **⚑6**

Most Americans have never heard of it. Every Iranian has.

### The 1990s: Dual Containment and Sanctions

After the Cold War, the Clinton administration adopted **"dual containment"** of both Iraq and Iran, building up a permanent U.S. military presence in the Gulf. Sanctions on Iran tightened throughout the decade, including the 1996 Iran and Libya Sanctions Act.

Iran's reformist president **Mohammad Khatami**, elected in 1997, called for a "dialogue of civilizations" and made overtures to the American people. Non-interventionists argue these openings were largely squandered.

### 2001–2003: Cooperation, Then "Axis of Evil"

After 9/11, Iran, which considered the Taliban an enemy, **helped the United States**. Iranian diplomats played a constructive role at the December 2001 Bonn conference that set up Afghanistan's post-Taliban government, a story told by U.S. envoy James Dobbins.

A month later, in his January 2002 State of the Union address, President George W. Bush named Iran part of an **"Axis of Evil."** Iranian reformists who had argued for engagement were humiliated.

In May 2003, a proposal reached the State Department via the Swiss ambassador in Tehran outlining a possible **"grand bargain"**: talks covering the nuclear program, support for Hezbollah and Hamas, and recognition of a two-state solution, in exchange for security guarantees and lifted sanctions. The Bush administration didn't respond. **⚑7**

For Horton, this is one of the great missed chances: the moment when Iran was most willing to deal, and the U.S. was most sure it didn't need to.

### 2003: The Iraq War Hands Iran Its Greatest Victory

This is one of Horton's signature arguments. When the U.S. invaded Iraq in 2003, it toppled Iran's worst enemy, the very man it had backed against Iran in the 1980s, and then put in power the **Shi'ite exile parties** that had spent the Saddam years based in or allied with Iran, including SCIRI and the Dawa party.

The United States fought a war that turned Iraq, a country Britain had assembled out of Ottoman provinces after 1917, from Iran's chief rival into a partner. Horton argues the Bush administration essentially did Tehran's work for it, then blamed Iran for its growing influence.

Iran also backed militias that fought U.S. troops during the occupation, supplying explosively formed penetrators (EFPs) that killed hundreds of American soldiers. Non-interventionists acknowledge this, and their point is that American soldiers were there to be targeted only because of a war that never needed to be fought.

The occupation also gave birth to al-Qaeda in Iraq, which later became **ISIS**, a group whose propaganda made a show of bulldozing the Iraq–Syria border and declaring the end of Sykes-Picot.

### 2007–2015: The Nuclear Question

In 2007, a **U.S. National Intelligence Estimate** concluded with high confidence that Iran had halted its nuclear weapons program in 2003. The report helped head off a war in Bush's last years.

In the years that followed, Iranian nuclear scientists were assassinated, and the U.S. and Israel deployed the **Stuxnet** computer worm against Iran's enrichment centrifuges. Sanctions grew tighter.

In 2015, the Obama administration and other world powers reached the **Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA)**. Iran accepted strict limits on enrichment and the most intrusive inspections in the world in exchange for sanctions relief. The IAEA repeatedly verified Iranian compliance.

### 2018–2020: Tearing It Up

In 2018, President Trump withdrew from the JCPOA and imposed **"maximum pressure"** sanctions. Iran waited about a year, then began exceeding the deal's limits, eventually enriching uranium to 60%.

In January 2020, a U.S. drone strike in Baghdad killed **Qassem Soleimani**, commander of the IRGC's Quds Force. Iran retaliated with a missile strike on U.S. troops at Ain al-Asad in Iraq. In the tense hours that followed, Iranian air defenses mistakenly shot down **Ukraine International Airlines Flight 752**, killing 176 people — a grim mirror of Flight 655.

The non-interventionist verdict: the U.S. had a working, verified agreement limiting Iran's nuclear program and threw it away. Everything after was a choice.

### 2025–2026: Open War

In June 2025, Israel launched a major air campaign against Iran, and on June 22 the United States bombed Iran's nuclear sites at Fordow, Natanz, and Isfahan. A ceasefire followed after twelve days.

The current war, which brought a U.S. blockade, a crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, and a still-disputed memorandum of understanding signed in June 2026, is in this view the logical endpoint of more than a century of outside pressure: each act producing the hostility that justifies the next.

## Part III: The Wider Pattern — Other Lessons That Should Have Been Learned

The non-interventionist argument isn't only about Iran. It's that the same mistake keeps repeating across the region, and much of the region was built on the decisions of 1917.

### Palestine: From Balfour to the Present

The Balfour Declaration promised a Jewish national home in a land whose Arab majority was never consulted. The British Mandate that followed saw rising Jewish immigration, Arab revolts (most significantly in 1936–1939, which Britain crushed), and eventually Britain's withdrawal in 1948. Israel's founding that year came with the flight and expulsion of roughly 700,000 Palestinians, known to them as the **Nakba**.

The United States took up Britain's role as Israel's principal patron, especially after 1967. Non-interventionists argue that this relationship, including unconditional military aid and diplomatic cover, is a major source of anti-American sentiment across the Muslim world, and that it drags the U.S. into Israel's conflicts, including the current war with Iran.

### Iraq: An Empire's Creation

Britain assembled Iraq from three Ottoman provinces, installed a Hashemite king from the Hejaz in 1921, and kept control of its oil. The 1920 revolt, the 1941 British re-invasion, the 1958 revolution that killed the king, and the rise of the Ba'ath Party all followed. In 1963, according to accounts by participants and later historians, the CIA had contacts with the Ba'athists who overthrew Iraq's leader Abd al-Karim Qasim, the party that would later bring Saddam to power.

The U.S. then armed Saddam against Iran, went to war with him in 1991, imposed sanctions through the 1990s, and invaded in 2003.

### Lebanon, 1982–1983: The Birth of Hezbollah

Israel invaded Lebanon in 1982, besieging Beirut. **Hezbollah** emerged in the Shi'ite south largely in response to the Israeli occupation, with Iranian support. U.S. Marines deployed to Beirut as peacekeepers but came to be seen as taking sides, especially after U.S. warships shelled Druze and Shi'ite positions.

In October 1983, a truck bomb destroyed the Marine barracks, killing **241 U.S. servicemen**. President Reagan withdrew the Marines a few months later, a decision non-interventionists often cite approvingly as the rare time a president cut his losses.

### Afghanistan, 1979–2001: Arming the Mujahideen

Beginning under Carter and expanding massively under Reagan, the CIA funneled billions of dollars, through Pakistan's intelligence service, to Islamist fighters resisting the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan. Carter's national security adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski later said the aid began before the Soviet invasion and that he was unbothered by its long-term consequences.

The Arab volunteers drawn into that war formed the network that would become **al-Qaeda**. When the Soviets left, Afghanistan collapsed into civil war, and the Taliban emerged from it.

### 9/11 and Osama bin Laden's Stated Reasons

Horton returns again and again to this point: **bin Laden told us why.** In his 1996 and 1998 declarations, he cited three main grievances:

1. U.S. troops stationed in Saudi Arabia after the 1991 Gulf War
2. The sanctions and bombing of Iraq through the 1990s, which UN agencies linked to large numbers of child deaths **⚑8**
3. U.S. support for Israel against the Palestinians

His statements also invoked the post–World War I breakup of the Ottoman world, which he presented as a humiliation still unavenged. The goal, in Horton's reading, was to provoke the U.S. into wars across the Muslim world that would bleed it and radicalize the region. The U.S. obliged.

Political scientist **Robert Pape**'s research on suicide attacks supports the broader point: he found the common thread in suicide terrorism is less religion than military occupation of territory the attackers consider their homeland.

### Libya, 2011

NATO's intervention to topple Muammar Gaddafi, sold as a humanitarian mission, left Libya a failed state, with rival governments, militias, and open-air slave markets. Weapons from Gaddafi's arsenals spread across North Africa and the Sahel. The 2017 Manchester Arena bomber was the son of a Libyan family linked to anti-Gaddafi fighters.

The lesson that should have been learned: having already watched Iraq collapse, the U.S. did it again.

### Syria, 2011–2024

Through the CIA program **Timber Sycamore** and allied Gulf states, the U.S. helped arm Syrian rebels in an effort to topple Bashar al-Assad. Many of those weapons and fighters ended up with jihadist factions, including al-Qaeda's Syrian affiliate. Horton has called this one of the most indefensible episodes of the War on Terror: the U.S. effectively on the same side as the group that attacked it on 9/11.

### Yemen, 2015–Present

The U.S. backed Saudi Arabia's war in Yemen with weapons, intelligence, and, for years, midair refueling. The Saudi blockade and bombing campaign helped produce one of the world's worst humanitarian crises. It also strengthened the very group the war was meant to defeat: Ansarallah (the Houthis) emerged as a regional military power capable of striking Saudi oil facilities and shipping.

## Part IV: The Cooper Angle — Stories and Mythmaking

Daryl Cooper's contribution to this conversation is less about policy and more about **narrative**. Across his *Martyrmade* series, his recurring theme is that nations and movements act according to the stories they tell about themselves, and that the most dangerous conflicts happen when two peoples hold incompatible stories about the same events. His long series on Israel and Palestine, *Fear & Loathing in the New Jerusalem*, spends much of its length in exactly this pre-1948 period, when British promises and imperial interests set the terms of the conflict.

Applied to Iran, the Cooper-style question would be: what is the story each side tells?

- **The American story** begins in 1979: a hostage crisis, a fanatical regime, "Death to America," terrorism, nuclear ambitions. Iran is a threat that must be contained.
- **The Iranian story** begins generations earlier: concessions sold to foreigners, a democratic parliament shut down by Russian troops, a country partitioned without being asked, a famine under foreign occupation in 1917, a shah installed by invasion, an elected government overthrown, a war in which America helped Saddam gas them, a civilian airliner shot down, sanctions, assassinations, bombings. In this story, America is simply the latest empire that never stopped trying to control them.

Each side's story is built from real events, and each leaves out the events that complicate it. The chant in Trump's post is, in this reading, the product of the second story, and it can't be understood without it.

Cooper also tends to emphasize the human texture beneath the geopolitics. The generation of Iranian commanders now running the country, Ghalibaf among them, were young men in the trenches of the Iran–Iraq War. Their worldview was formed by a war in which, as they see it, the whole world sided with Saddam and Iran survived alone. That experience shapes how they read American promises today, and it rhymes with a national memory, going back to 1917, of foreign powers deciding Iran's fate over its head.

## Part V: The Counterarguments

The non-interventionist case is powerful, but it has serious critics. Any honest reading should weigh these.

**Britain isn't America.** Much of the pre-1953 history is British and Russian imperialism, not American. Critics argue it's unfair to load that history onto the U.S., which in that era was often a counterweight to British designs, including opposing the 1919 agreement. Non-interventionists respond that this is precisely the point: the U.S. squandered that goodwill by taking up Britain's role.

**Iranian agency.** The Islamic Republic is not simply a reaction to foreign powers. It's a revolutionary theocracy with its own ideology, which from the start rejected both the U.S. and the Soviet Union. Critics argue that blowback framing turns Iranians into objects of Western action rather than actors responsible for their own choices. Many Iranian elites, from Qajar shahs selling concessions to officials taking British money in 1919, were active participants in the history Iranians resent.

**The regime's record.** Iran's government has executed thousands of political prisoners (notably in the 1988 mass executions), crushed repeated protest movements (2009, 2019, 2022), and is among the world's leading executioners. Many Iranians who suffered under the Shah suffered just as much or more under the Islamic Republic.

**Terrorism and proxies.** Iran and Hezbollah are linked by investigators to attacks far removed from any U.S. provocation, such as the 1994 AMIA bombing of a Jewish community center in Buenos Aires, which killed 85 people. The U.S. has also tied Iran to the 1996 Khobar Towers bombing. Iran's support for armed groups across the region — Hezbollah, Iraqi militias, the Houthis, Hamas — has fueled wars that killed many civilians.

**The 1953 debate.** Historians like Ray Takeyh argue that the coup succeeded mainly because of Iranian actors, including clergy, the army, and royalists who opposed Mossadegh, and that the CIA's role, while real, has been overstated. **⚑9**

**The 1979 debate.** Similarly, many historians see the Shah's fall as driven chiefly by domestic forces — rapid modernization, inequality, religious opposition — rather than by resentment of foreign powers alone.

**Deterrence and nuclear risk.** Supporters of pressure argue that Iran's expansion of enrichment to 60%, close to weapons-grade, shows the danger was real, and that sanctions and military action, not restraint, slowed it down. Critics of the JCPOA argued its time-limited "sunset clauses" would have let Iran expand its program legally within a decade. **⚑10**

**Balfour and Israel.** Supporters of Israel argue that the Balfour Declaration recognized a legitimate national claim of a persecuted people with deep historical ties to the land, that Arab leaders rejected repeated partition proposals, and that the conflict's persistence owes as much to Arab and Palestinian choices as to British or American policy.

**"Why do they hate us" cuts both ways.** Bin Laden's grievances also included broad religious and civilizational claims, and withdrawing from the region doesn't necessarily end hostility. Some point to the rise of ISIS after the 2011 U.S. withdrawal from Iraq as evidence that disengagement can also produce blowback.

**Selective history.** Just as hawks start the story in 1979, critics say non-interventionists sometimes start it in 1953, or 1917, and minimize everything after. Every account chooses where the story begins.

Whatever one concludes, the core non-interventionist challenge stands as a question worth asking: if the goal is fewer enemies, have more than a hundred years of concessions, partitions, coups, sanctions, wars, and assassinations produced more of them or fewer?

## ⚑ Flagged for Your Attention

**⚑1 — The Persian famine death toll.** Estimates vary enormously. Historian Mohammad Gholi Majd has argued for 8–10 million deaths and called it a deliberate British-caused genocide, but most historians consider that figure implausible given Persia's total population at the time (roughly 10–12 million). More conservative estimates run from several hundred thousand to around two million. Responsibility is also debated: crop failure and disease played major roles alongside requisitioning by occupying armies, including Russian and Ottoman forces, not only British ones.

**⚑2 — Britain's role in the 1921 coup.** That British officers encouraged and facilitated Reza Khan's coup is well documented. Whether London planned it, or local British officers acted on their own initiative, remains debated.

**⚑3 — Interpreting Balfour.** What a "national home" meant, whether Britain's wartime promises to Arab leaders (the Hussein–McMahon correspondence) included Palestine, and whether the declaration's protections for non-Jewish communities were ever meant seriously are all long-running historical disputes.

**⚑4 — The communist-threat rationale.** Whether Mossadegh posed a real risk of a communist takeover (the Tudeh Party was active) is still debated. Most historians see it as an exaggerated justification, but not all do.

**⚑5 — U.S. knowledge of chemical weapons use.** The 2013 *Foreign Policy* report on declassified CIA files is well sourced. What's debated is how much U.S. intelligence directly enabled chemical attacks, versus general battlefield support.

**⚑6 — Vincennes details.** The ship's commander, Will Rogers III, received the Legion of Merit for his overall tour, not specifically for the shootdown, though critics see the timing as telling. A famous line attributed to George H.W. Bush about never apologizing for America was said in 1988 but in a different context; it's often misquoted as a direct response to the shootdown.

**⚑7 — The 2003 "grand bargain."** It's disputed whether the proposal had the full authority of Iran's Supreme Leader or reflected one faction's (and possibly the Swiss intermediary's) drafting. Its significance depends on that question.

**⚑8 — Iraq sanctions death tolls.** Early UNICEF-linked estimates of around 500,000 child deaths have been challenged by later research suggesting Saddam's government manipulated survey data. The suffering was real; the scale is contested.

**⚑9 — Who really made the 1953 coup.** A live scholarly debate (Kinzer and Abrahamian vs. Takeyh and others), not a settled question.

**⚑10 — JCPOA effectiveness.** Whether the deal was the best available outcome or a flawed one that delayed the problem is one of the most politically contested foreign policy debates of the last decade.

**⚑11 — On the sources.** Scott Horton and Daryl Cooper are both influential and both polarizing. Cooper in particular drew wide criticism in 2024 for remarks on World War II history in an interview with Tucker Carlson. If you present this piece publicly, it's worth being clear that it reflects their analytical tradition on U.S. foreign policy, not an endorsement of everything either has said.
