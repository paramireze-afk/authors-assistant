# The Unnecessary War? Buchanan, Griffin, and Cooper Explain World War II

## Introduction

Here's a question that sounds simple and turns out to be one of the most fascinating in modern history: did World War II have to happen?

Most of us grew up with a clean version of the story. Hitler wanted to conquer the world. Britain and France tried to buy him off at Munich and failed. Churchill stood alone, refused to give in, and held the line until America and the Soviet Union joined the fight and crushed the Nazis. Appeasement became a dirty word, and Churchill became the model of the leader who never backs down.

But pull on almost any thread in that story and something more complicated unravels. Why did a dispute over one city on the Baltic, Danzig, end up dragging in the whole world? Why did Britain go to war to save Poland and end the war with Poland under Stalin? How did governments pay for a war that cost more than any war in history, and who came out ahead? And why does the story of this war carry so much moral weight, still, more than eighty years later?

Three writers have built their explanations around exactly those questions. Pat Buchanan, the former Nixon and Reagan adviser and three-time presidential candidate, argues in *Churchill, Hitler, and "The Unnecessary War"* (2008) that a chain of British blunders turned a regional crisis into a world war and cost Britain its empire. G. Edward Griffin, author of *The Creature from Jekyll Island* (1994), follows the money, showing how central banking made total war affordable and who profited along the way. And Darryl Cooper, the podcaster behind *Martyrmade*, argues that Churchill bears the heaviest responsibility for what the war became and that the story we tell about it works as the founding myth of the world built afterward.

Let's dig in.

## Part One: Churchill's Own Phrase

Start with the title of Buchanan's book, because there's a great twist hiding in it. "The Unnecessary War" was Churchill's phrase. In the preface to *The Gathering Storm*, the first volume of his war memoirs, Churchill recalled that President Roosevelt once asked him what the war should be called. Churchill answered without hesitation: "The Unnecessary War."

Churchill meant that the war could have been stopped if Britain and France had stood up to Hitler early, rearming in time and calling his bluff in the Rhineland or over Czechoslovakia while Germany was still weak.

Buchanan grabs that phrase and flips it on its head. Yes, he says, the war was unnecessary, but not because Britain failed to confront Germany soon enough. It was unnecessary because British leaders made one strategic mistake after another, from the harsh peace of 1919 to the guarantee to Poland in 1939, and because Churchill, once in charge, chose to fight on and widen the war when other paths were open. Same three words, completely opposite story.

## Part Two: The Roots in Versailles

Buchanan's story starts where World War I ends, and he argues that Britain's entry into that first war was itself the original blunder. But the real seeds of the second war, he says, were planted at the peace table.

The Treaty of Versailles, signed in June 1919, put responsibility for the war on Germany and its allies in Article 231, piled on heavy reparations, took away Germany's colonies, and capped its army at 100,000 men. And here's the part Buchanan zeroes in on: it placed millions of Germans under foreign rule, even though the Allies had promised a peace built on national self-determination.

Look at the map. More than three million Germans in the Sudetenland ended up inside the new state of Czechoslovakia. Danzig, an overwhelmingly German port city, was cut off from Germany and turned into a Free City under the League of Nations. The Polish Corridor, a strip of land giving Poland access to the sea, sliced East Prussia off from the rest of Germany. And Austria, now a small German-speaking country, was forbidden from uniting with Germany.

It gets worse. The Allies kept their naval blockade of Germany going for months after the November 1918 armistice, right up until the treaty was signed, while German civilians went hungry. The British economist John Maynard Keynes was at the peace conference, quit in disgust, and wrote *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), warning that the treaty would wreck Germany and Europe with it. The French commander Marshal Ferdinand Foch is reported to have said the treaty wasn't peace at all, just an armistice for twenty years. Twenty years later, almost to the month, the war came.

For Buchanan, this changes how we see Hitler's demands in the 1930s. Union with Austria, the Sudetenland, Danzig: in the eyes of many Germans, and plenty of British observers at the time, these were demands to bring Germans back into Germany and undo the injustices of Versailles.

## Part Three: Throwing Away Japan

Here's a piece of the story that almost nobody talks about. From 1902, Britain and Japan were allies. Japan fought on Britain's side in World War I. Then, at the Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22, under pressure from the United States and Canada, Britain let the alliance lapse and replaced it with a vague four-power agreement.

Buchanan calls this a huge strategic mistake. Japan, proud and rising fast, felt snubbed and cut loose, and Britain lost its influence in Tokyo. Over the next twenty years Japan slid toward militarism and expansion, and eventually into the Axis. And when war came, Britain's Asian empire, Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore, fell to the very country that had once been its ally. Think about that for a second: the empire's greatest defeat in Asia came at the hands of a former friend Britain had chosen to let go.

## Part Four: Driving Mussolini to Hitler

Now for one of the most surprising chapters. In the early 1930s, Mussolini couldn't stand Hitler. When Austrian Nazis tried to seize power in 1934 and murdered the Austrian chancellor, Engelbert Dollfuss, Mussolini rushed Italian troops to the Brenner Pass as a warning to Germany: hands off Austria.

In April 1935, after Hitler announced German rearmament, Britain, France, and Italy formed the Stresa Front, pledging to stand together against any further breach of the peace treaties. A coalition that could have boxed Germany in was right there.

And then it fell apart, and Buchanan lays the blame on Britain. Just two months later, in June 1935, Britain signed its own separate naval deal with Germany, accepting a German fleet up to 35 percent the size of the Royal Navy, effectively blessing German rearmament without even asking its partners. Then, when Mussolini invaded Ethiopia in October 1935, Britain led the League of Nations in slapping sanctions on Italy. The sanctions were too weak to stop the invasion but plenty strong enough to infuriate Mussolini. Within a year he'd turned to Hitler, and the Rome–Berlin Axis was born in 1936. In Buchanan's telling, Britain managed to push a potential ally straight into the arms of the power it most needed to contain.

## Part Five: Rhineland, Austria, and Munich

Next come the famous episodes everyone knows. In March 1936, Hitler marched troops into the demilitarized Rhineland, German territory that the treaties had stripped of soldiers. In March 1938, he annexed Austria, where crowds turned out to cheer. In September 1938, at Munich, Britain and France agreed to let Germany take the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia.

The usual story treats every one of these as a shameful surrender that only fed Hitler's appetite, with Munich as the ultimate act of cowardice. Buchanan sees it differently. Britain in 1938 wasn't ready to fight. The Sudeten Germans themselves largely wanted to join Germany. And the British Dominions, Canada, Australia, South Africa, had very little appetite for a war over Czechoslovakia.

He's got company here. The Oxford historian A.J.P. Taylor shook up the whole field in 1961 with *The Origins of the Second World War*, which portrayed Hitler not as a man following a master plan but as an opportunist grabbing whatever the weakness of others put within reach. The 1930s, in Taylor's view, were a series of improvisations, not a straight road to war.

For Buchanan, the real turning point was still ahead.

## Part Six: The Guarantee to Poland

### The shock of Prague

On March 15, 1939, Hitler tore up the Munich deal and occupied the rest of the Czech lands, Bohemia and Moravia, setting up a German protectorate in Prague. For the first time, he'd seized territory where the people were mostly not German. British opinion swung hard against him overnight, and Chamberlain, humiliated, changed course.

On March 31, 1939, Chamberlain stood up in the House of Commons and announced that if Poland's independence were threatened and Poland chose to resist, Britain and France would come to its aid.

Buchanan calls this the greatest blunder in British history. To see why, you have to look at what the fight was actually about.

### Danzig

In October 1938, the German foreign minister, Joachim von Ribbentrop, put a proposal to the Polish ambassador. Germany wanted Danzig back, and it wanted to build a highway and railway across the Polish Corridor to East Prussia, under German control. In return, Germany offered to guarantee Poland's borders, extend the existing German-Polish non-aggression pact, and invited Poland to join the Anti-Comintern Pact against the Soviet Union. Poland, under its foreign minister Józef Beck, said no.

Buchanan's point is that, whatever you think of Hitler, these particular demands weren't outrageous. Danzig was a German city. British officials had privately admitted since the 1920s that the Danzig and Corridor arrangements couldn't last. The real question was whether Britain should go to war over them.

### Why Buchanan calls it a catastrophe

Buchanan's case against the guarantee is one of the most compelling parts of his book:

- **Britain couldn't actually defend Poland.** It had no army that could fight in Eastern Europe, and the Royal Navy couldn't get into the Baltic in strength. France had the only big Western army, and its whole strategy was to sit behind the Maginot Line. When Germany invaded in September 1939, Britain and France declared war and then did almost nothing. Poland was crushed in about five weeks.
- **It handed Britain's decision to Warsaw.** By promising to fight if Poland resisted, Britain gave the Polish government, not the British Cabinet, the power to decide whether Britain went to war. Buchanan argues this encouraged Polish leaders to refuse any compromise over Danzig.
- **It drove Stalin into Hitler's arms.** With Britain committed to Poland, the Soviet Union suddenly held the balance in Eastern Europe. Britain's half-hearted talks with Moscow went nowhere, and on August 23, 1939, Germany and the Soviet Union stunned the world with the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, including a secret protocol carving up Poland and the Baltic states. A week later Germany invaded Poland from the west. On September 17, the Soviet Union invaded from the east.
- **It didn't even save Poland.** Here's the kicker. Britain declared war on Germany, but not on the Soviet Union, which had invaded Poland too. And when the war ended, Poland, the very country Britain had gone to war to protect, was handed over to Soviet domination for nearly half a century. The war fought for Polish independence ended without it.

The guarantee was issued by Chamberlain, and Churchill was out of office at the time. But Buchanan argues that Churchill had been the loudest voice demanding confrontation, and that his vision shaped the policy.

## Part Seven: Hitler's Aims and the Peace Offers of 1940

One of the pillars of Buchanan's argument is that Hitler didn't want a war with Britain. His ambitions, Buchanan argues, pointed east, toward "living space" in Poland, Ukraine, and Russia, just as he'd written in *Mein Kampf*. Hitler openly admired the British Empire and said again and again that he wanted an understanding with Britain: Germany would run the continent, and Britain would keep its empire and its navy.

Buchanan and others point to a string of remarkable moments:

- **The halt at Dunkirk.** In May 1940, with the British army trapped against the sea at Dunkirk, Hitler approved an order that stopped German tanks for several days. More than 300,000 British and French troops got away. Some writers see this as a deliberate signal to Britain. Most military historians put it down to worries about the tanks and the terrain and Göring's boast that the Luftwaffe could finish the job.
- **The May 1940 Cabinet showdown.** Over several tense days in late May 1940, Britain's War Cabinet argued about whether to explore peace terms through Italian mediation. The Foreign Secretary, Lord Halifax, wanted at least to find out what terms might be on offer. Churchill, Prime Minister for barely two weeks, won the argument: Britain would fight on.
- **The July 1940 "appeal to reason."** After France fell, Hitler went before the Reichstag on July 19, 1940, and presented himself as offering Britain peace. Britain turned it down within days.
- **Rudolf Hess's flight.** In May 1941, Hitler's own deputy, Rudolf Hess, climbed into a fighter plane and flew alone to Scotland, apparently to negotiate peace. He spent the rest of the war in prison and the rest of his life in Spandau after Nuremberg. To this day, plenty of people believe the full story of the Hess mission has never been told.

For Buchanan, all of this shows that a negotiated peace in the West was possible in 1940. By refusing it, Churchill locked Britain into a long war it couldn't win alone, one that could only be won by bringing in the Soviet Union and the United States, at the cost of the empire itself.

## Part Eight: Churchill's War

This is where Buchanan saves his sharpest criticism, and he's not alone. The British historian John Charmley made a similar case in *Churchill: The End of Glory* (1993), arguing that Churchill's policies cost Britain its empire and made it a junior partner to America. The libertarian historian Ralph Raico's essay "Rethinking Churchill" has become a classic in antiwar circles.

Here's how the argument unfolds:

- **Britain becomes dependent on America.** To keep fighting, Britain needed the United States. In September 1940, in the destroyers-for-bases deal, Britain handed over 99-year leases on bases across its Atlantic possessions in exchange for fifty aging American destroyers. Lend-Lease, starting in March 1941, kept Britain supplied, but it came with American pressure to open up the empire's protected trading system. By 1945, Britain was effectively broke and dependent on American loans.
- **The war escalates.** Churchill's critics point to the bombing of German cities, which killed hundreds of thousands of civilians, and to the demand for Germany's unconditional surrender, announced with Roosevelt at Casablanca in January 1943. Critics argue that this left German opponents of Hitler with nothing to bargain with and may have dragged the war out longer.
- **Eastern Europe goes to Stalin.** In October 1944, in Moscow, Churchill and Stalin struck the famous percentages agreement, literally dividing influence in the Balkans by numbers scribbled on a sheet of paper. At Yalta in February 1945, the Allies effectively accepted Soviet control of Poland and much of Eastern Europe. For Buchanan, a war that started over Poland ended by delivering Poland, and a hundred million Eastern Europeans, to a dictator who had already killed millions of his own people.
- **The empire disappears.** Within two years of victory, Britain lost India. Over the next two decades, most of the rest of the empire followed. Buchanan's subtitle says it all: Britain lost its empire, and the West lost the world.

## Part Nine: The American Side: The "Back Door to War"

Across the Atlantic, there's a parallel story about Franklin Roosevelt, and it was first told by some of the most respected historians in America. The argument goes like this: Roosevelt promised Americans he'd keep them out of the war while working to bring them in, and when the public wouldn't accept war with Germany, he came in through Japan, the "back door."

Charles Beard, one of the giants of American history in his day, made the case in *President Roosevelt and the Coming of the War, 1941* (1948). Charles Tansill followed with *Back Door to War* (1952). Their evidence is a series of documented steps:

- **The Atlantic heats up.** Through 1940 and 1941, Roosevelt edged the country closer and closer to war with Germany: the destroyers deal, Lend-Lease, American naval escorts for British convoys, and, after the destroyer USS *Greer* traded fire with a German U-boat in September 1941, a "shoot on sight" order. By that autumn, the U.S. Navy was fighting an undeclared war in the Atlantic. Yet Germany didn't declare war, and most Americans still didn't want full entry. The America First Committee, the biggest antiwar movement the country had ever seen, had hundreds of thousands of members.
- **The squeeze on Japan.** In July 1941, after Japan occupied southern French Indochina, the United States froze Japanese assets, and in August an oil embargo kicked in, cutting Japan off from most of its oil. Japan's leaders now faced a stark choice: pull out of China and Indochina, or grab the oil fields of the Dutch East Indies by force. On November 26, 1941, Secretary of State Cordell Hull handed Japan a note demanding withdrawal from China and Indochina, which Tokyo read as an ultimatum.
- **The McCollum memo.** In 2000, the journalist Robert Stinnett published *Day of Deceit*, spotlighting an October 1940 memo by Lieutenant Commander Arthur McCollum of naval intelligence. It listed eight actions likely to provoke Japan into an "overt act of war," and several of them look a lot like policies the United States went on to adopt. Stinnett also argued that Washington had more warning of the Pearl Harbor attack than it ever admitted.

On December 7, 1941, Japan struck Pearl Harbor. Four days later, Hitler declared war on the United States, and America was in the European war at last.

## Part Ten: The Money Trail

Now follow the money, because this is where things get really interesting. Antony Sutton, a research fellow at Stanford's Hoover Institution, argued in *Wall Street and the Rise of Hitler* (1976) that American and British corporations and banks helped finance and equip the Nazi war machine.

And a lot of the specifics are documented. Standard Oil of New Jersey had a close partnership with the German chemical giant IG Farben, which later ran a synthetic fuel and rubber plant at Auschwitz using slave labor. General Motors owned the German carmaker Opel, and Ford had a big German subsidiary; both turned out military vehicles for the Wehrmacht. In 1942, the U.S. government seized the Union Banking Corporation in New York, where Prescott Bush, father and grandfather of two future presidents, was a director, under the Trading with the Enemy Act, because of its ties to the German industrialist Fritz Thyssen, an early financial backer of Hitler. And the Bank for International Settlements in Switzerland, created by central banks in 1930, kept doing business with Nazi Germany all through the war.

Writers in this tradition argue that the same financial interests profited from building Germany up, from the war that tore it down, and from the order that followed. Mainstream historians accept many of the individual business ties but generally see companies chasing profits and protecting investments rather than a coordinated plan to produce a war.

## Part Eleven: G. Edward Griffin and the Machinery of War Finance

That brings us to G. Edward Griffin, who takes the money question deeper than anyone. Buchanan asks why statesmen made the choices they made. Griffin asks something more basic: how do governments even pay for wars this big, and who wins from the way they pay? His 1994 book, *The Creature from Jekyll Island: A Second Look at the Federal Reserve*, became a phenomenon, a founding text for the movement to audit or end the Fed and a major influence on figures like Ron Paul.

### The secret meeting at Jekyll Island

Griffin's story opens like a thriller. In November 1910, a handful of the most powerful men in American finance slipped off in secret to the Jekyll Island Club, a private resort off the Georgia coast owned by some of the richest families in the country. The group included Senator Nelson Aldrich, chairman of the National Monetary Commission; Paul Warburg of the investment bank Kuhn, Loeb; Frank Vanderlip, president of National City Bank; Henry Davison, a partner of J.P. Morgan; Benjamin Strong of Bankers Trust; and A. Piatt Andrew of the Treasury. They traveled using only first names to avoid attention. Over about ten days, they drafted the plan that became the foundation of the Federal Reserve Act, passed in December 1913. And this isn't speculation: participants later wrote about the trip themselves.

Griffin's argument is that the Federal Reserve is, at its core, a cartel, a system designed by the biggest banks to shield themselves from competition and from failure, with the government as their partner and the public footing the bill.

### The "Mandrake Mechanism"

The heart of Griffin's book is what he calls the Mandrake Mechanism, named after the comic-strip magician Mandrake. It's his term for how the modern banking system creates money out of thin air. When the government spends more than it takes in, it issues bonds. When the central bank and commercial banks buy those bonds, brand-new money is created to pay for them. Nobody sends you a tax bill. Instead, the cost shows up later as inflation, quietly eating away at the value of everyone's savings and wages. Griffin calls inflation a hidden tax.

And here's why this matters so much for war. Before central banking, Griffin argues, a government that wanted to fight a long war had to raise taxes or borrow real savings, and both put natural brakes on how long and how hard it could fight, because people felt the cost directly. A central bank takes the brakes off. It lets a government spend almost without limit and spreads the cost invisibly across the entire population. In Griffin's view, central banking is what made total war possible.

### The war cycle

Griffin describes what he calls the "Rothschild Formula," drawn from the history of the great banking houses of the Napoleonic era: lending to governments at war is one of the most profitable businesses there is, and financiers who serve rival governments can profit from both sides of a conflict, and then again from the rebuilding afterward. He presents it as a pattern in how war finance works, and he applies the same logic to American banks in the twentieth century.

His take on World War I sets the stage for World War II. While America was still neutral, from 1914 to 1917, J.P. Morgan & Co. served as Britain's purchasing agent in the United States and arranged enormous loans to the Allies, dwarfing anything lent to Germany. Griffin argues that by 1917, an Allied defeat would have meant catastrophic losses for American banks, giving them a powerful stake in American entry.

He pays special attention to the *Lusitania*, the British liner sunk by a German U-boat in May 1915, killing 128 Americans and turning American opinion against Germany. The ship was carrying munitions, including rifle cartridges and shrapnel shells listed right on its cargo manifest. And Griffin points to a letter Churchill wrote as First Lord of the Admiralty in early 1915, saying it was important to attract neutral shipping to British shores, especially in the hope of embroiling the United States with Germany. For Griffin, that letter is a window into how great powers and their financiers set up the conditions for war.

Griffin also took seriously *Report from Iron Mountain* (1967), a document presented as a secret government study concluding that war is necessary for the stability of modern societies. Its author, Leonard Lewin, later said he wrote it as satire. Griffin argued that, whatever its origins, it captured the logic of the "war system" with uncomfortable accuracy.

### World War II through Griffin's eyes

Griffin's book focuses more on the founding of the Fed and World War I than on World War II, but his framework fits the second war remarkably well, and the financial record backs up a lot of it. American federal debt exploded from roughly $43 billion in 1940 to around $260 billion by 1945. Starting in 1942, the Federal Reserve committed to holding interest rates on government debt at very low fixed levels, buying whatever bonds it took to keep them there, an arrangement that lasted until the Treasury–Federal Reserve Accord of 1951. In other words, the central bank stood behind the war with an unlimited checkbook. Wartime wage and price controls kept much of the inflation hidden, until 1945, when prices shot up.

Through Griffin's lens, World War II was the biggest demonstration of the Mandrake Mechanism the world had ever seen: a war of unprecedented scale, fought on money created by the banking system, paid for by the public through debt and inflation, and followed by a brand-new international financial order, built at Bretton Woods in 1944, with the dollar and American finance at the center of the world. The business ties from the previous section, Standard Oil, GM, the Bank for International Settlements, slot right into the same picture: financial interests operating across the front lines.

## Part Twelve: Darryl Cooper: Churchill and the Myth of the Good War

And then there's Darryl Cooper, who brought all of these questions roaring back into public view. In September 2024, the historian and podcaster behind *Martyrmade* sat down for a long interview with Tucker Carlson, and it set off one of the biggest history debates in years. Cooper's case overlaps with Buchanan's but pushes further, and it's as much about how we remember the war as about the war itself.

### Churchill as the chief villain

The line everyone remembers is Cooper calling Winston Churchill the "chief villain" of the Second World War. His point wasn't that Churchill killed the most people; he didn't argue that. It was that Churchill bore the most responsibility for the war becoming what it became: not a regional war over Poland, but a world war of annihilation.

Cooper argued that the war wasn't inevitable and that almost nobody outside Churchill's faction in Britain wanted it. In his telling, Hitler wanted a settlement in the West after Poland and made repeated peace proposals in 1939 and 1940. Chamberlain and much of the British establishment were open to ending the war. Churchill, Cooper argued, was the leader most determined to keep it going and escalate it, even though Britain on its own had no realistic way to beat Germany. His only route to victory was to pull in the United States and the Soviet Union, and Cooper argued that Churchill pursued it through media and propaganda operations aimed at American public opinion.

And that last point connects to something that really happened. Starting in 1940, British intelligence ran a covert organization out of New York called British Security Coordination, headed by the Canadian businessman William Stephenson. Its job was to break American isolationism. It planted stories in the American press, backed interventionist groups, monitored and worked to undermine antiwar organizations like America First, and in some cases spread forged documents. The full scale of the operation only came out decades later.

### Breaking the old limits of war

Cooper also argued that Churchill shattered a long-standing restraint in European warfare. For about two and a half centuries, he said, European powers had largely avoided deliberately targeting each other's civilian populations through measures like mass starvation, and pre-war Europe marked a high point of civilization in that respect. Churchill, in Cooper's account, reversed that tradition. People who share this view point to the British hunger blockade of Germany in World War I, when Churchill was a leading figure at the Admiralty, and to the area bombing campaign of World War II, which deliberately targeted residential neighborhoods and killed hundreds of thousands of civilians in cities like Hamburg and Dresden.

### The war in the East

Cooper's most controversial remarks were about the Eastern Front. He argued that when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, it captured millions of prisoners and suddenly controlled enormous civilian populations with no real plan for feeding or housing them, and that this lack of preparation, more than a premeditated design, explains much of the mass death that followed. This is the part of the interview that drew the fiercest reaction, because it touches on the origins of the Holocaust. Cooper's response was that the intensity of the outrage proved his larger point about how sacred the story of World War II has become.

### The founding myth

And that larger point may be the most thought-provoking thing Cooper has to say. He argues that the standard story of World War II isn't just history. It's the founding mythology of the current global order, the story that justifies the institutions, alliances, and moral framework built after 1945. Because it plays that role, he says, questioning it gets you treated like a heretic. He has also argued that after Nuremberg, it became effectively impossible in the West to be genuinely right-wing, because the war's legacy is used to push certain political views outside the bounds of acceptable debate. Interestingly, some of his fiercest critics, including the Churchill biographer Andrew Roberts, agreed that the war and Churchill do serve as a kind of founding story for the modern world. They just rejected Cooper's conclusions about it.

For Cooper, this is about today, not just the 1940s. The "lesson of Munich," that any compromise with a hostile power is appeasement, has been used to justify war after war since 1945. Questioning the myth, in his view, means questioning the logic behind all those later wars too.

## Part Thirteen: How the Three Fit Together

Buchanan, Griffin, and Cooper often get lumped together, but each brings something different to the table, and seeing how they fit is half the fun.

**Common ground.** All three take Churchill as a pivotal figure who deserves far more scrutiny than he usually gets: for Buchanan, the architect of Britain's ruin; for Cooper, the leader most responsible for the war's escalation; for Griffin, a statesman willing to draw America into war through incidents like the *Lusitania*. All three doubt that the war was simply forced on the democracies. And all three look at how the war ended, Eastern Europe under Stalin, Britain bankrupt and stripped of its empire, American finance and military power astride the world, and ask whether the war really served the purposes it was supposed to serve.

**Three different angles.** Buchanan's lens is statecraft: statesmen who misjudged their nation's interests and blundered into catastrophe. Griffin's lens is structure: a financial system that profits from war and makes it easier to wage, with bankers and governments working hand in hand. Cooper's lens is memory: Churchill's choices in 1939–41, and how the story of the war became the moral foundation of the postwar world. Buchanan is a conservative nationalist who mourns the British Empire. Griffin distrusts concentrated financial power in any form. Cooper is most interested in how the story of the war shapes politics right now. Their timelines differ too: Buchanan starts with World War I and Versailles, Griffin with the founding of the Federal Reserve in 1913, and Cooper zooms in on 1939 to 1941.

**Where the facts are solid.** A lot of what these three point to is a matter of record. Many mainstream historians also see the Stresa collapse, the Anglo-German naval agreement, and the unenforceable guarantee to Poland as mistakes. The Jekyll Island meeting, the Fed's wartime role, and the debt explosion are documented. So are the British influence operation in America, the McCollum memo, and the business ties between Western firms and Nazi Germany. The big debates are about what those facts add up to.

## Part Fourteen: The Pushback

Any good exploration of a debate has to give the other side a full hearing, and historians who defend the standard account have some powerful responses.

**Hitler's aims went far beyond Versailles.** The biggest objection to Buchanan's case concerns Hitler himself. His goals, laid out in *Mein Kampf* and his unpublished second book, and confirmed in the Hossbach Memorandum, a record of a November 1937 meeting where he told his top commanders to prepare for war, went far beyond reversing Versailles. He wanted to conquer vast territories in the East and remove or destroy the people living there. Nazi planning documents like the Hunger Plan and Generalplan Ost envisioned tens of millions of Slavs dying through starvation and deportation. A Germany that dominated Europe, critics argue, wouldn't have been a stable partner for Britain but a genocidal empire stretching from the Atlantic to the Urals, with the industrial base to come after Britain later.

**Prague changed the equation.** Critics argue the guarantee to Poland wasn't a reckless gamble but a response to proven bad faith. At Munich, Hitler said the Sudetenland was his last territorial demand in Europe. Six months later he took Prague. After that, British leaders concluded, reasonably in the critics' view, that no deal with Hitler could be trusted and that Danzig wouldn't be the end.

**The peace offers were on Hitler's terms.** Hitler's offers of 1939 and 1940 assumed that Germany would dominate the continent, keep occupying Poland, France, and much of Western Europe, and that Britain would accept it. Churchill's defenders argue that such a peace would have left Britain at the mercy of a Nazi-run Europe, and that refusing it kept the hope of liberation alive.

**Germany and Japan made the decisive choices.** Hitler chose to invade Poland, then Western Europe, then the Soviet Union, and then declared war on the United States, which nobody forced him to do. Japan chose to attack Pearl Harbor. Most historians accept that Roosevelt was working to aid Britain and confront Japan. But the claim that he knew about Pearl Harbor in advance and let it happen has been examined many times, including by congressional investigations, and most historians reject it. The leading explanation, set out in Roberta Wohlstetter's *Pearl Harbor: Warning and Decision*, is that the warnings got lost in noise and misjudgment.

**On Griffin.** Economists broadly agree that central banks make it easier for governments to finance wars, and the Fed's wartime role is on the record. Most reject Griffin's bigger claim that the Fed is a cartel run for bankers, noting that governments fought huge wars long before modern central banks, from Napoleon's campaigns to the American Civil War, using taxes, borrowing, and printed paper money. Historians who have studied the *Lusitania* and the J.P. Morgan loans for a century mostly conclude that German submarine warfare, culminating in the return to unrestricted attacks in 1917 and the Zimmermann Telegram, did more to bring America into World War I than the bank loans did. *Report from Iron Mountain* is regarded as a hoax. And because the "Rothschild Formula" uses a Jewish banking family as its emblem, the phrase has been picked up in antisemitic circles in ways that go far beyond Griffin's argument, which is worth knowing when it comes up.

**On Cooper.** Critics, including historians at the Churchill Project, point out that Churchill held none of the decisions that started the war: he was out of office when Britain guaranteed Poland, Chamberlain was the one who declared war, and Hitler had ordered the invasion of France before Churchill became Prime Minister. On the Eastern Front, historians point to extensive evidence that the mass death was planned rather than improvised. The Hunger Plan, drawn up before the invasion, anticipated the starvation of tens of millions of Soviet civilians to feed the German army and the Reich. Of roughly 5.7 million Soviet prisoners Germany captured, more than 3 million died, a death rate historians attribute to deliberate policy. The Einsatzgruppen began mass shootings of Jews in the first weeks of the invasion, before any supply crisis. On civilian targeting, historians note that Germany bombed Warsaw, Rotterdam, and London before Britain's area bombing campaign began. Cooper's defenders respond that his critics have answered his narrower claims about Churchill's decisions in 1940 while dodging his bigger questions about the postwar order.

**The war and the Holocaust.** Historians agree that the war radicalized Nazi policy and made mass killing possible on a vast scale. But they stress that the persecution of Jews started long before the war, and that the drive to destroy European Jewry came from Nazi ideology and was carried out by deliberate decision. Arguments that treat the Holocaust as a by-product of Allied choices are widely seen as shifting responsibility away from the people who carried it out.

**Keeping the debate clean.** Historians also note that some earlier challenges to the official account of the war's origins became a gateway to something much worse: a few writers of the 1950s and 1960s went on to deny or minimize the Holocaust itself. Questioning strategic decisions and questioning documented atrocities are two completely different things, and the strongest version of this debate keeps them separate.

## Conclusion

So, was it an unnecessary war?

Take these three at their strongest, and a lot holds up. Versailles really did leave millions of Germans under foreign rule and breed deep resentment. British diplomacy in the 1930s really was often incoherent. The guarantee to Poland really couldn't be enforced, and the war Britain entered for Poland's sake ended with Poland under Stalin. Britain came out of the war victorious but bankrupt, dependent on America, and soon without its empire. Roosevelt really did maneuver the country toward war while promising peace. Western companies really did do business with Nazi Germany. A central bank really did stand behind the war with a blank check. And British intelligence really did run covert operations to steer American opinion toward war.

Where the debate gets heated is the conclusion. Buchanan argues that a wiser Britain could have stayed out of a German-Soviet clash in the East and kept both its empire and peace in Western Europe. His critics argue that the nature of Hitler's regime made that impossible.

Griffin and Cooper push the conversation even further. Griffin asks who paid for the war and who profited from the way it was paid for, pointing to a financial system that made war on this scale affordable. Cooper asks why the standard story of the war is treated as beyond question, and what that story has been used to justify ever since. Whatever you decide about their answers, those questions reach far beyond 1945.

And that might be the best thing about digging into this history. The questions Buchanan, Griffin, and Cooper raise apply to every war: were the decisions that led to it truly unavoidable, who made them, who paid, who profited, and did the outcome serve the purposes it was fought for?
