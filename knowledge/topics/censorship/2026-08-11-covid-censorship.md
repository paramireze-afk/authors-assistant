# The Realignment Nobody Saw Coming: Notes from a Conversation Between Scott Horton and Glenn Greenwald

American politics has a habit of humiliating its own experts. That was the starting point for a recent conversation between libertarian broadcaster Scott Horton and journalist Glenn Greenwald, who used two "impossible" presidential wins as a lens for understanding a much bigger shift now underway in both parties.

## Two Elections the Pundits Got Wrong

Greenwald pointed to Barack Obama's 2008 victory and Donald Trump's rise in 2016 as the clearest evidence that conventional wisdom about "electability" has been broken for nearly two decades. Both men were dismissed early as unelectable — one for his background and perceived liberalism, the other for having no political credentials at all — and both went on to win, in some cases in the same rust-belt counties. Greenwald argued the two had more in common than their politics suggested: personal charisma that pulled irregular voters into the process, and a shared willingness to present themselves as enemies of an establishment most Americans already despise. In his view, the old assumption that only careful centrists can win swing states no longer holds, and it's why a new wave of unapologetically left-wing candidates is doing better than the professional political class expected.

## Israel as the Democratic Establishment's Weak Point

A recurring theme was the string of primary losses suffered by Democratic incumbents seen as closely tied to Israel and AIPAC — Haley Stevens among them — while insurgent, further-left candidates unseated them. Horton framed Israel and war as the "Achilles heel" of centrist Democrats. Greenwald agreed but offered a twist: he doesn't think most voters are voting on Israel policy itself so much as using it as a proxy for a broader complaint — that their representatives are absorbed in distant conflicts while ignoring crumbling communities and economic hardship at home. A politician's posture toward Israel, in this reading, has become a shorthand test for whether they actually care about ordinary Americans.

The two also discussed a Michigan primary in which the winning candidate pushed back against accusations of anti-Jewish bias by making a simple case: American tax dollars should serve Americans first, not fund conflicts abroad — a position he applied evenhandedly, including to aid for Egypt, despite his own family's ties there. They noted a similar dynamic played out in the New York City mayoral race, where most candidates said their first foreign trip in office would be to Israel — except one candidate, who said his focus would stay on his own constituents.

## JD Vance, Trump, and a Cooling of the "Special Relationship"

The conversation turned to a notable moment where JD Vance appeared to downgrade Israel's status from a uniquely "special" ally to something closer to an ordinary one. Greenwald traced this back to an Oval Office exchange in which Trump firmly opposed Israeli annexation of the West Bank — a position Greenwald linked less to principle than to Trump's desire to expand the Abraham Accords, which Gulf states have made contingent on progress toward a two-state framework. Weeks later, while Vance was visiting Israel, the Knesset passed a symbolic measure endorsing annexation, timed in a way Greenwald read as a deliberate jab at Vance during his trip. Despite media speculation that Vance was freelancing, Greenwald noted that reporting from journalists covering the administration indicated Vance's comments were authorized by Trump himself — plausibly, in part, to counter the idea that Trump simply takes orders from Netanyahu. Greenwald also connected this shift to generational politics: with Vance eyeing a 2028 presidential run, he'll need enthusiasm from younger conservatives who are increasingly resistant to unconditional support for Israel.

## Cameras Changed the Story

Both hosts returned repeatedly to the role of unfiltered footage from Gaza in reshaping public opinion. Greenwald argued that past American wars were softened for the public because only large media outlets — historically cooperative with the national-security state — had the resources to cover them, and they typically did so through embedded, sanitized reporting. Phone cameras and platforms like TikTok changed that equation, giving ordinary people in conflict zones the ability to document and share what was happening directly. Greenwald suggested this explains the urgency behind efforts to restrict TikTok, since it became a primary channel through which younger Americans encountered images the traditional press had generally avoided.

## An Old Taboo: Talking About Blowback

One of the more striking threads was a discussion of an interview neoconservative writer Robert Kagan gave to Responsible Statecraft, in which he reportedly acknowledged the concept of "blowback" — the idea that military interference abroad generates retaliatory hostility — in relation to events including the Ukraine war. Greenwald called this remarkable given how forcefully that framework has historically been treated as fringe or even unpatriotic to raise, recalling how, after 9/11, commentators who suggested U.S. foreign policy played any role in generating resentment were treated as pariahs — pointing to the fate of an obscure academic who made a similar argument and was turned into a national villain by Fox News coverage and ultimately lost his job over it.

That history became directly relevant again, Greenwald argued, when a wave of young people on TikTok stumbled onto the letter Osama bin Laden wrote to the American public in 2002. The letter laid out grievances that were rarely discussed in mainstream terms — sanctions on Iraq that were tied to enormous civilian death tolls, U.S. military bases on land considered religiously sacred, and unconditional American backing for Israel's treatment of Palestinians. For many young viewers encountering this material for the first time, it upended a simpler story they'd absorbed growing up, that the 9/11 attacks were motivated by hostility to American freedom and culture rather than by specific, decades-old grievances about U.S. foreign policy in the region.

Greenwald described what happened next as a case study in how forcefully that narrative gets policed. The Guardian was essentially one of the only major outlets that still hosted the original letter, and as clips and links circulated on TikTok, the government and other institutions pressured the paper into taking it down, breaking the links that had been spreading. TikTok itself then moved to restrict discussion of the letter on the platform. To Greenwald, the speed and coordination of that response was itself revealing — evidence of just how sensitive the foreign policy establishment remains to Americans encountering the "blowback" argument in the source material's own words, rather than filtered through decades of official framing.

Kagan, for his part, reportedly rejected the "neoconservative" label altogether, describing himself instead as a lifelong liberal Democrat — a reminder, Greenwald noted, that many figures associated with the Iraq War era, including Richard Perle, never formally left the Democratic Party, and some, like Bill Kristol and Max Boot, have since returned to it.

## Tucker Carlson and the Case for a Real Third Party

Horton and Greenwald also discussed speculation around Tucker Carlson potentially building an independent political coalition, drawing together figures across the spectrum who share a distrust of both parties' establishments — comparing it to the populist energy that fueled both Occupy Wall Street and the early Tea Party after the 2008 financial crisis, movements Greenwald sees as expressions of the same underlying anger at concentrated financial and political power. He was skeptical that Carlson himself intends to run for office but saw the broader project as a sign that space now exists for a genuine third-party movement — not a billionaire vanity campaign, but something built on shared disgust with the political duopoly. Marjorie Taylor Greene came up as an example of a legislator whose critiques of Washington, in Greenwald's view, now echo arguments more commonly associated with the left.

## Can Left and Right Actually Work Together?

The conversation closed on a more personal note, with Horton describing the difficulty of getting genuine leftists and genuine right-wingers into the same room, even when they agree on opposing war and monetary policy — recalling a 2023 antiwar rally where organizers deliberately avoided mixing the two camps to keep the coalition from fracturing. Greenwald argued that old tribal taboos, like the idea that association with the left is automatically disqualifying for a conservative, are eroding as both parties' establishments lose credibility — pointing to his own history of being labeled a partisan extremist for years, by both sides, simply for engaging with people across the aisle.
