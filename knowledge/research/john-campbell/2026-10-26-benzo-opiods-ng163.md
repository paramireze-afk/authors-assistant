# Benzodiazepines, Opioids and NG163

## What John Campbell Calls a National Scandal

John Campbell opens by saying he wants to talk about what he considers **a national scandal that is being ignored**. He summarises the whole argument up front so viewers can decide whether to keep watching:

- On **18 March 2020**, the British government issued a **Drug Safety Update** titled, in effect, *"Benzodiazepines and opioids: reminder of risk of potentially fatal respiratory depression."* In plain terms: give these two together and it can kill you.
- **Sixteen days later**, on **3 April 2020**, NICE published the guideline Campbell calls *"infamous"*, **NG163**, which said these drugs **could** be combined when treating patients with COVID-19.

As Campbell puts it, on 18 March the guidance was essentially "do not mix opioids and benzodiazepines" (such as morphine sulfate with midazolam or lorazepam), except under very precise circumstances. Sixteen days later came what he calls a complete contradiction: if you had COVID-19, you could suddenly be given this potentially lethal combination.

He acknowledges these are "pretty incendiary" things to say and promises to give full evidence. He also gives a shout-out to **Andrew Bridgen**, whom he describes as a former pioneering Member of Parliament in Britain, for pointing it out to him. Campbell says he had missed it himself "because it was so obvious."

---

## The documents involved

### The Drug Safety Update (18 March 2020)

This is the government update Campbell shows on screen first, dated 18 March 2020.

### NG163 and its replacement

The guideline in question is the COVID-19 rapid guideline on **managing symptoms, including at the end of life, in the community**. According to Campbell:

- NG163 has since been **superseded** by a newer guideline (NG191), in which "they've corrected a few things."
- That replacement did not come out until much later, on **23 March 2021**.
- The current guideline was **last updated in 2025**, and Campbell says, "So things are good now. That's okay."

### Where NG163 went

Campbell says he looked in the NICE archives and **could not find NG163** there. He adds, with evident sarcasm, that there is "no doubt a perfectly innocent explanation," that it is not that anyone would want to cover their tracks, and that someone "probably just forgot to leave it on."

He notes, however, that **archived copies do exist**. He shows one, published 3 April 2020, says he will link it, and describes it as "pretty straightforward"; you could read the whole thing in half an hour or less. "Good job it's archived," he says, otherwise it would not be available, despite the NHS publishing it in 2020.

## Why the combination is dangerous

### Something every UK clinician "just knows"

Campbell says that anyone in clinical practice in the UK simply knows this:

- **Benzodiazepines can cause respiratory depression.**
- **Midazolam is particularly prone** to it, for various reasons, including that it tends to have a much quicker onset.
- You don't mix these drugs, because you get respiratory depression.

### A case Campbell witnessed

He recalls working on a **coronary care unit**, where a colleague gave **5 mg of intravenous morphine** to an older lady and **she simply stopped breathing**. The emergency buzzer sounded and the team went in.

It wasn't a disaster, he explains, because:

- they ventilated her,
- they gave the antidote, **naloxone (Narcan)**,
- they could keep ventilating her while waiting for the naloxone to work, and
- an expert team was on hand.

His point is that patients **can just stop breathing**. If they are not closely supervised and are given morphine together with midazolam or another benzodiazepine, they can stop breathing and "then of course they simply die. It's that simple. This really happens. I've seen it."

### The physiology

Campbell explains the mechanism simply:

- Breathing is controlled by the **respiratory centre in the brain**.
- If drugs switch that centre off, **nothing makes you breathe**.
- The respiratory muscles do not work on their own; they depend on impulses through the **intercostal nerves** and **phrenic nerves**.
- "Switch off the power supply, if you like," and you just stop breathing. "Terrible, but that simple."

## What the 18 March 2020 warning actually said

Campbell goes through the Drug Safety Update's advice for healthcare professionals:

- **Benzodiazepines (and benzodiazepine-like drugs) and opioids can both cause respiratory depression.** Separately, he notes, you would have to give a lot of benzodiazepine to cause it, but the two **act together**.
- They have **additive effects on the central nervous system**, increasing the risk of:
  - sedation,
  - respiratory depression,
  - coma (in which, Campbell adds, people can vomit and choke on their own vomit), and
  - **death**.

He calls the warning "completely unambiguous. No question about it."

The update's instructions, as he relays them:

1. **Only prescribe benzodiazepines and opioids together if there is no alternative.** Campbell comments that in the COVID situation, he believes there *were* alternatives, so this contradicts the later guidelines.
2. If a decision is made to co-prescribe, **use the lowest dose possible for the shortest duration**, and **carefully monitor** patients for signs of respiratory depression.
   - Campbell says this is fine in an acute unit where patients are closely watched, as in his coronary care example: a medical emergency, but manageable, with an Ambu bag and naloxone to hand.
3. If there is any change in prescribing, such as a new interacting drug or a dose adjustment, **reintroduce close monitoring**. In other words, if you alter the dose, go back to watching the patient closely.
4. **Advise patients** about respiratory depression and sedation and the need to **seek immediate medical attention** if they occur.
   - Campbell remarks this is "rather difficult if you're in a cubicle, isolated in an old person's or a care home facility."

His summary of the message: mix these two drugs and there is a real risk you will kill people by switching off the respiratory centre. **Don't do it.** Clinicians had known this for a long time, he says, but the government reinforced it in terms that were "utterly unambiguous."

## What NG163 said 16 days later

### Context: "end of life" and how COVID patients were treated

Campbell notes that the guideline covers managing symptoms **including at the end of life**, and argues that "end of life" is subjective:

- In his view, COVID on its own killed very few people because it has a **very low infection fatality rate**.
- Yet many people who caught COVID were **immediately assumed to be dying**.
- Healthcare professionals were advised **not to go and see them**. Nurses he has spoken to described propping up their own mobile phones at the end of the bed so the GP could interview the patient remotely.
- Looking back, he calls this "unacceptable" and "terrible."

### The period it was in force

NG163 was published 3 April 2020 and, although updated, **remained in force until 2021**. Campbell states that between **March 2020 and March 2021** there were **about 127,000 deaths from COVID in England and Wales**. He adds his view that the majority of these deaths probably were not from COVID but from other things.

During that period, he says, NICE was **actively advising** that benzodiazepines such as midazolam and lorazepam could be mixed with opioids such as morphine sulfate, **directly contradicting** the Drug Safety Update of 16 days earlier. "You couldn't make this up."

### Managing fever

Campbell notes that NG163 advised patients with fever to take **paracetamol** (acetaminophen, Tylenol). To be fair, he says, it also said not to give paracetamol *purely* for fever. Still, he is uncomfortable with it:

- Lowering temperature, he argues, **reduces the efficiency of the immune system**, which works best at a **pyrexial (feverish) temperature** during infection.
- Fever exists for a real reason: to help the immune system work more effectively and to make the environment uncomfortable for viruses and any bacteria.
- He says this mistake is still commonly made throughout healthcare, "after all these years." He then sets the point aside: "we'll let that pass for now."

He also mentions that the guideline discussed other symptoms that antibiotics would help treat.

### Managing breathlessness: the morphine doses

The guideline's morphine recommendations, as Campbell reads them:

| Patient group | Dose in NG163 |
|---|---|
| Not already on opioids (oral) | Morphine sulfate immediate release **2.5 to 5 mg every 2 to 4 hours** |
| Already on morphine (oral) | Morphine sulfate **5 to 10 mg every 2 to 4 hours** |
| Subcutaneous (often via syringe driver) | **1 to 2 mg every 2 to 4 hours** |

Campbell concedes these doses don't *sound* like much. In A&E, he says, someone in severe pain might happily be given 10 mg of morphine. But in the **elderly**, 2.5 or 5 mg "can zonk them for ages," because they are much more sensitive to it. People who are elderly, already ill, and already have a respiratory infection are **much more prone** to the adverse effects.

### "Consider an opioid and benzodiazepine in combination"

Campbell quotes NG163 directly: **consider an opioid and benzodiazepine in combination**. (He briefly fumbles how many days separated this from the government warning, wondering aloud whether it was 13 or 9, before settling on "less than a couple of weeks." The figure he gives at the start is 16 days.)

According to the guideline, this applied to patients with COVID-19 who:

- **are at the end of life** — Campbell says most wouldn't have been, but were wrongly assumed to be;
- **have moderate to severe breathlessness** — as you very often would with a respiratory infection; and
- **are distressed** — as you would be with a respiratory infection causing dyspnoea (difficulty breathing).

His conclusion: this casts "a pretty wide net" for combining a contraindicated opioid with a benzodiazepine, precisely what the government had just advised against.

### Lorazepam

The guideline advised adding a benzodiazepine if required, alongside morphine, for breathlessness and anxiety:

- **Lorazepam 0.5 mg sublingually** (under the tongue) when required, **maximum 4 mg a day**.
- **Reduce the dose to 0.25 to 0.5 mg** in elderly or debilitated patients. Campbell says "Good" to this, but stresses it is still being **added to** the morphine, **as well as, not instead of**.

He explains why 4 mg is more than it sounds. His rule of thumb is that **lorazepam is about eight times more potent than diazepam (Valium)**, so 4 mg of lorazepam equals roughly **32 mg of diazepam**, which he calls quite a big dose. Lorazepam is a very potent, short-acting benzodiazepine and should be used with caution.

### Midazolam

For associated **agitation or distress**, NG163 advised:

- **Midazolam 2.5 to 5 mg subcutaneously** when required.

Campbell reminds viewers that midazolam is **more prone to cause respiratory depression** because it is quicker acting, among other reasons, and that this too was **in addition to morphine**. "So here the guidelines are patently saying: give morphine, and if you want, add midazolam."

### The sentence Campbell objects to most

NG163 then states, he says: **sedation and opioids should not be withheld because of the fear of causing respiratory depression.**

His reaction: "Yes, it flipping well should. Of course it should."

His paraphrase of what that line means: sedation (benzodiazepines) and opioids **should not be withheld just because there's a risk it will kill your patient**. "Well, I would have thought it should. I would have thought it exactly should." He emphasises that this comes directly from NG163, further down the document, and encourages viewers to check it for themselves. He says it sits in the sections on managing fever and managing breathlessness, and that the quotes he shows are direct.

## Campbell's position on the drugs themselves

Campbell is careful to say this is **not anti-pharmacy**:

- Morphine and midazolam are both **"wonderful drugs."** He has been present many times when midazolam was used by anaesthetists and in A&E departments.
- Even combining them is **not out of the question** if there are **really careful monitoring facilities** and the ability to **bag the patient and reverse** the drugs. Even then, he says, he still wouldn't do it if he could possibly avoid it.
- Used incorrectly, though, they can kill, because they turn off the respiratory centre. And the guideline, as he reads it, was effectively saying: don't let that put you off, just give both together.

## His conclusions

### What explains the contradiction?

Campbell poses three possibilities:

1. **Incompetence?**
2. **Professional negligence?**
3. **Or something else?**

"I couldn't possibly comment," he says, inviting viewers to give their views in the comments.

### The human cost, as he sees it

Whatever the explanation, Campbell states his belief that:

- **"A lot of people probably died"** from the combination of midazolam and morphine, or lorazepam and morphine, **who otherwise would not have died.** "That simple."
- He asks how many **thousands, or tens of thousands**, could have died from this **iatrogenic** (treatment-induced) use of a contraindicated combination in patients who were often **poorly supervised**.
- He says he has spoken to **nurses in care homes** who did this. They described cupboards **"packed out with midazolam and morphine,"** given to all sorts of people. People were being put on end-of-life care, sometimes **simply on being diagnosed with COVID**, when they could have made a very good recovery.

He calls it **"a dark chapter in English medical history," basically completely ignored.**

### What he wants

Campbell says this should be **investigated** and brought to **public attention**, for two reasons:

1. **The dead need justice.** They need to know what went on.
2. **The bereaved** deserve answers: did their relative die because someone injected them with midazolam and morphine?

He closes: "Thank you for watching."
