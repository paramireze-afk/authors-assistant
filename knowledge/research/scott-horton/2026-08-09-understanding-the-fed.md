# Understanding the Fed, Fractional Reserve Banking, and the Boom-Bust Cycle
### Notes from a conversation between Scott Horton and economist Bob Murphy (Mises Institute)

*A framework-first explainer, organized around Murphy's Austrian School economics and Horton's political-economy interpretation.*

---

## Why This Conversation Matters

This is a discussion between Scott Horton (a libertarian foreign-policy commentator who also follows monetary policy closely) and Bob Murphy (an Austrian School economist affiliated with the Mises Institute). The goal here isn't to fact-check them or weigh their views against mainstream economics — it's to understand *their* internal logic: how they believe money, banking, and government power are connected, and why they think the pieces fit together the way they do.

The throughline of the whole conversation is this: **the ability of governments and banks to create new money "out of nothing" is the hidden engine behind a huge range of phenomena** — the Great Depression, the 2008 crash, the housing affordability crisis, wage stagnation, inequality, and even (they argue) immigration policy and generational conflict. Murphy provides the technical/economic explanation; Horton keeps pushing him to connect the dots to lived political and historical reality.

---

## Part 1: What "Inflation" Actually Means (Austrian Definition)

Murphy opens by making a point that Austrians consider foundational: **the word "inflation" used to mean something different than it does today.**

- **Old/Austrian definition:** Inflation = an expansion of the money supply (and bank credit) itself.
- **Modern/popular definition:** Inflation = rising prices (what you'd see in the Consumer Price Index, or CPI).

Why does this distinction matter to Murphy? Because if you define inflation only as "rising prices," you're describing a *symptom*, not a *cause*. This lets central bankers rhetorically position themselves as inflation-fighters even while their own money-printing is the actual source of the problem. Murphy's line for this: it's like **"the arsonist promising to put out the fire."**

This isn't a minor semantic quibble in the Austrian framework — it's the key that unlocks the rest of their argument. If you think of inflation only as visible price increases, you'll miss periods where the money supply is expanding rapidly but prices *look* stable — which, as we'll see below, is exactly what Murphy argues happened in the 1920s, right before the Great Depression.

### The Keynesian Contrast (as Murphy frames it)

Murphy contrasts the Austrian view with what he calls the Keynesian framework:

- **Keynesian view (per Murphy):** The economy needs enough aggregate demand (total spending) to sustain full employment. Gently rising prices are actually seen as *healthy*, because they indicate enough money is circulating.
- **Austrian view (per Murphy, especially the Murray Rothbard tradition — sometimes called "hard money" advocates):** Money's real function is to enable *economic calculation* — that is, to let people and businesses compare prices and make rational decisions. Once you have a functioning money supply, adding more of it doesn't create real wealth. As Murphy puts it, you don't get more farms, tractors, drill presses, or scientists just by printing more money. So central banks tinkering with the money supply is, in this view, more likely to cause problems than solve them.

## Part 2: The Case Against the Federal Reserve as "Lender of Last Resort"

Horton raises the standard justification for central banking: *if we get rid of the Fed, who bails out banks in a crisis?*

Murphy's Austrian counter-argument has two parts:

### 1. The empirical record
Murphy points out that since the Fed's creation in 1913, the dollar has lost **over 99% of its value**. By contrast, he argues that under the classical gold standard — when the dollar was defined as a fixed weight of gold — the U.S. went through roughly century-long stretches (e.g., early 1800s to early 1900s) where prices were "basically steady." He also notes that the Great Depression and the 2008 financial crisis both happened *on the Fed's watch*, which he uses to argue the Fed hasn't actually delivered the stability it was created to provide.

*(Note: this is Murphy's historical framing/argument, not something the transcript treats as requiring outside verification — it's presented here as his case, not as a neutral fact-check.)*

### 2. The "moral hazard" argument
This is the more theoretical — and, according to Murphy, more distinctly Austrian — point: **having a lender of last resort standing by paradoxically makes private banks *more* reckless, not less.**

The logic: if a bank knows that a central institution will step in and inject liquidity ("cash") whenever there's a run or a shortfall, the bank has less incentive to be conservative in the first place. Without that safety net, a bank that overextends itself (lends out too much relative to what it's holding in reserve) risks going out of business — so the discipline comes from actual consequences. Add a lender of last resort, and you remove that discipline while keeping the risky behavior.

## Part 3: How Money Is Actually Created — Fractional Reserve Banking Explained

This is the technical heart of the conversation, and it's worth slowing down for, because Horton and Murphy revisit it twice: once in the abstract, and once through the lens of a viral, badly-explained example (the Epstein/Bannon interview — see Part 3B below).

### The building blocks

- **M1**: A basic measure of "how much money is in the economy." It's not just physical cash — it also includes the balances in everyone's checking accounts, because for practical purposes, $100 in your checking account functions the same as a $100 bill (you can swipe a card or hand over cash, and the store doesn't care which).
- **Fractional reserve banking**: The system where banks are only required to keep a *fraction* of their total deposits on hand as actual reserves (historically, this was often around 10%, though the exact rule varied). The rest can be lent out.

### Why banks want to do this

When someone deposits, say, $1,000, the bank doesn't just let it sit. It might keep $100 in reserve and loan out $900 to someone else — while the original depositor *still thinks* they have their full $1,000 available. That $900 loan then earns interest for the bank. Murphy calls this "pyramiding on other people's deposits" — the bank earns extra income on money that, from the depositor's point of view, is still just sitting safely in their account.

### The single-bank-vs-whole-system distinction (a key nuance)

Horton brings up a real-world example: in an interview with Steve Bannon, Jeffrey Epstein claimed that if you deposit a dollar, "they can loan out $9" — a 9x multiplier. A critic on social media called this wrong, saying the real ratio is: **deposit $10, and banks can loan out $9** (i.e., a 90% loan-out rate, not a 9x multiplication).

Murphy's answer is genuinely interesting because he says **both framings contain some truth, depending on what level you're looking at:**

1. **From a single bank's point of view, in an idealized/technical sense:** If reserve requirements are 10%, then in principle a bank receiving a $100 deposit *could* immediately make $900 in new loans, since a bank with $1,000 in liabilities (the original $100 checking balance plus the $900 in newly created checking balances for borrowers) only needs $100 in reserves — which it already has. So, technically, Epstein's version isn't "demonstrably false" at the level of a single transaction.

2. **But in practice, no single bank actually does this**, because once loans are made, people spend the money — and if any of that spending goes to people who bank elsewhere, this bank has to send real reserves to the *other* bank to settle up. So in practice, a bank is conservative and only lends out roughly 90% of a new deposit (i.e., $90 on a $100 deposit) to make sure it can survive those interbank settlements.

3. **From the perspective of the banking system as a whole, over time**, both numbers can be reconciled: Bank A takes in $100, lends out $90 (conservatively). That $90 gets spent and deposited into Bank B, which then lends out $81 (90% of $90). That process repeats through the whole banking system — a "chain all the way down," as Horton puts it — and if you add up all the new money created across every link in that chain, the total *new* money created does approach $900. So the multiplier only manifests across the whole system over time, not in a single bank's single transaction.

This is presented in the conversation as a genuine, careful economic point — not a "gotcha" against either the popularized Epstein version or the critic's correction. Murphy essentially says: **they're both saying true things, depending on which level of the system you're describing.**

*(Side note on the transcript: Murphy separately criticizes Epstein's overall credibility and describes him as "a pathological liar" whose claimed timeline of events didn't add up — but this is Murphy's aside about Epstein's general trustworthiness, separate from his assessment of the banking mechanics Epstein described, which he treats as "not demonstrably false," if imprecise.)*

### A historical wrinkle: reserve requirements were eliminated in 2020

Murphy notes that the Federal Reserve actually **eliminated reserve requirements entirely** around March 2020, in the middle of the COVID crisis — announced somewhat quietly, in a Sunday release, buried in a footnote referencing another document. This is presented as a historical/factual claim in the conversation, marking a real shift away from the fractional-reserve framework as classically understood, even though banks still operate with similar practical caution around lending.

## Part 4: Who Actually Creates New Money — The Fed, or the Banks?

One subtlety Murphy stresses: **the Federal Reserve does not have direct, total control over the money supply**, because a large chunk of the money supply (checking account balances) depends on how much private banks choose to lend. If banks issue more loans, more money (in the M1 sense) effectively comes into existence, because borrowers now have spendable balances that didn't exist before.

This is why Murphy connects central banking to private banking as a *partnership* — referencing the framework from G. Edward Griffin's book *The Creature from Jekyll Island* (called "Jackal Island" throughout the conversation, apparently a mishearing/mispronunciation preserved from the audio). Murphy's summary of that argument, which he generally endorses (with caveats):

- The government grants banks a privileged, cartel-like position — the legal ability to create money through fractional-reserve lending.
- In exchange, banks are expected to reliably buy government debt, especially during wartime when governments need to borrow more than the public would voluntarily lend.
- This arrangement is *not*, in Murphy's telling, a wild "conspiracy theory" — he frames it as fairly plain, traceable history: governments running into fiscal trouble (especially due to war) is historically the *actual reason* central banks like the Bank of England or the Federal Reserve were formed.

### Murphy's caveat about Griffin/the "Birchers"

Horton raises the John Birch Society ("Birchers") — a mid-20th-century anti-communist political movement known for conspiratorial narratives centered on figures like the Rockefellers and Morgans. Murphy's interesting aside: he says that when he first read Murray Rothbard's *Wall Street, Banks, and American Foreign Policy*, it read "exactly like the Bircher history of the 20th century, only without the kind of weird right-wing stuff" — meaning Rothbard covered similar ground (the power of banking dynasties like the Rockefellers and Morgans) but, in Murphy's view, did so more rigorously and without invoking groups like the Illuminati. Murphy also notes, as a point of interest, that Griffin's book cites Rothbard repeatedly, and that the Birchers were historically aligned with "hard money" (Mises/Rothbard-style) positions on monetary policy — distinguishing them from other populist critics of banks who wanted the *government* to issue more free money directly (e.g., "Lincoln greenbacks" or "Kennedy greenbacks").

**This is presented as Murphy's own historical interpretation and personal reading experience — not a claim the conversation treats as settled fact.**

## Part 5: The 1920s Boom and the Road to the Great Depression (Their Historical Narrative)

This is one of the most detailed causal stories in the conversation, and it's worth walking through step by step, because it's the backbone of the Austrian "boom-bust" theory as Murphy presents it.

### Step 1: World War I broke the gold standard internationally
Before WWI, major countries had their currencies tied to gold. During the war, every major belligerent *except the United States* formally abandoned the gold standard and printed money to finance the war. This left currencies like the British pound weaker relative to gold (and the dollar) than before the war.

### Step 2: Britain tried to return to its pre-war gold value
After the war, Britain pledged to bring the pound back to its pre-war parity with gold. Doing so required *deflation* — falling prices and wages — which caused real economic pain, including conflicts with labor unions who resisted wage cuts.

### Step 3: The U.S. inflated the dollar to help Britain
Around 1927, the head of the Bank of England (Montagu Norman) leaned on his American counterpart and personal friend, Benjamin Strong, head of the New York Federal Reserve (described in the conversation as effectively acting like a modern Fed chairman). Strong agreed to expand the U.S. money supply to relieve pressure on the pound — reducing the incentive for people to dump pounds for dollars.

### Step 4: This monetary expansion fueled a stock market and real estate boom
Murphy and Horton both point to the timing here as significant: right when the U.S. "opened the monetary spigots" to help the British, U.S. stock and real estate markets began booming — a boom that would eventually crash in 1929.

### Step 5: The boom looked deceptively "stable" because of the inflation-definition issue from Part 1
Here's where the earlier point about the definition of inflation becomes crucial to their argument: because U.S. productivity was *also* rising sharply in the 1920s, prices for everyday goods stayed roughly flat, even as the money supply expanded. Prominent economist Irving Fisher famously (and, in hindsight, disastrously) declared stocks had reached "a permanently high plateau" shortly before the crash.

Murphy's Austrian argument: **the "correct" outcome, absent Fed intervention, would have been gently *falling* prices**, reflecting the productivity gains. Because inflation offset that natural deflation, prices only *looked* stable — masking the underlying monetary expansion. Murphy notes that both Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek reportedly saw the crash coming in the 1920s specifically because they were tracking credit expansion rather than consumer prices — including an anecdote (which Murphy says he believes is true) that Mises turned down a prestigious bank economist position because he foresaw a "great crash coming" and didn't want his name associated with it.

### Step 6: Under a truly free market, the argument goes, the crash would have been short
Murphy states the standard Austrian claim: unsustainable booms lead to crashes, but if the government stays out of the way and lets prices and wages fall to find their natural market level, the correction is typically over within about two years — and he says this is what happened historically in earlier, less-interventionist crashes (referencing the 1920–21 depression as a comparison case, where the recovery was fast).

### Step 7: What (they argue) made the 1930s different
This is the crux of their explanation for why the Great Depression was so uniquely severe and prolonged:

- After the 1929 crash, President Hoover called in labor union leaders and big business owners and pressured them **not to cut wages** — even as prices were collapsing.
- Historically, wages had fallen quickly in earlier depressions (allowing markets to "clear" — i.e., for supply and demand to find a new equilibrium). In the early 1930s, wages fell much more slowly because of this political pressure.
- The result, in Murphy's telling: businesses couldn't afford to keep as many workers at those artificially high wage levels, driving unemployment up to roughly 25%.
- Murphy's analogy for why simply "printing more money" wouldn't have fixed the underlying problem: it's like advising the mafia (the "Corleone family") to stop shooting people — the family might reasonably say "we're at war, if we stop we'll be killed" — but the deeper point is that they only got into that situation *because* of their own earlier choices. Similarly, Murphy argues 1930s policymakers had backed themselves into a corner where "just print more money" seemed like the only option, but that option only seemed necessary because of the earlier interventions (like the wage-fixing policy) that had made the crisis worse in the first place.

### A related, more speculative claim: David Stockman's view
Horton raises a claim from Reagan-era budget director David Stockman: that the depression was economically "over" — i.e., bad debts had already been cleared out of the system — by the time FDR was inaugurated in March 1933, and that it was the New Deal itself that prolonged the downturn for another decade.

Murphy's response is measured — he says he trusts that Stockman has "receipts" for his specific claims, but adds an important qualifier: the money supply (M1/M2) actually **shrank by about one-third between 1929 and 1933** due to bank runs (people pulling cash out of banks, forcing banks to call in loans, which contracts the money supply in fractional-reserve systems — the reverse of the money-creation process described in Part 3). Because of this, Murphy suggests that even if bad debts had been cleared, the Hoover-era wage-fixing policy meant the economy still hadn't found its footing by 1933 — so "everything was already fixing itself" is, in Murphy's assessment, an incomplete picture rather than something he simply confirms.

**This is flagged in the conversation itself as an area of partial uncertainty for Murphy** — he doesn't fully endorse or reject Stockman's framing, but qualifies it.

## Part 6: The Chicago School / Friedman Contrast — Where Austrians and Monetarists Disagree

This section matters because Horton pushes Murphy to clarify that Austrians are *not* the same as other free-market-adjacent economists — specifically the "Chicago School," associated with Milton Friedman (and later Fed chair Ben Bernanke, described as someone who studied and was influenced by Friedman's work).

- **Friedman/Chicago School view (per Murphy):** The core mistake of the early 1930s was that the Fed was too *passive* — when the public started pulling money out of banks (causing the money supply to shrink), the Fed should have "offset" that by pumping in more money "from the top." Friedman attributes the Fed's failure to act partly to circumstance: Benjamin Strong (the New York Fed head mentioned earlier) had died, leaving what Murphy describes as a "power vacuum" — nobody with the authority or nerve to act decisively. In this view, the problem wasn't too much monetary expansion in the 1920s so much as too little in the early 1930s.

- **Austrian/Rothbard view (per Murphy):** The problem was the *opposite* — the Fed *had* pumped too much monetary inflation into the economy during the 1920s boom (as described in Part 5), creating an unsustainable bubble in the first place. If that expansion hadn't happened, the argument goes, there wouldn't have been nearly as severe a "boom-bust" cycle to correct in the 1930s.

Murphy frames this as a genuinely important internal distinction Austrians care about: **don't lump Austrians in with all "free market economists."** Both camps agree the Fed made the Depression much worse than it needed to be, but they disagree sharply about *when* the Fed's real mistake occurred and in *which direction* it erred.

## Part 7: Does Inflation Help "The Little Guy"? A Nuanced Disagreement

Horton raises a common argument he says he was taught (framed as "conventional wisdom" from a junior-college class): that inflation is *good* for ordinary people, because it lets them borrow dollars and pay back the loan later in cheaper, less valuable dollars — for example, paying off a 30-year fixed mortgage with wages that have risen (in dollar terms) due to inflation, even though the mortgage payment itself stays the same.

Murphy's answer distinguishes between short-run and long-run effects — a good example of the "don't flatten this into inflation-is-simply-bad" nuance:

### The short-run, "one-shot" effect
Murphy agrees this is *technically true* on its own terms: unanticipated inflation transfers wealth from creditors (lenders) to debtors (borrowers), because debts get repaid in currency that's worth less than when the loan was made.

**But** this cuts both ways: inflation also hurts people on relatively fixed incomes when it's *not* matched by quick wage increases — if prices rise 10% but your employer doesn't immediately give you a 10% raise, you're worse off in the meantime. Murphy notes this hits younger, less-established people hardest — those without a mortgage or a car loan to "benefit" from being repaid in cheaper dollars, but who still have to pay more at the grocery store.

### The long-run, systemic effect (this is the more important Austrian point)
Murphy's more significant argument is about what happens once **everyone knows** inflation is coming and adjusts their behavior accordingly:

1. If it's common knowledge that a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage is a great inflation hedge (because your payment stays flat in dollar terms while rents and wages rise), then **wealthy people with capital rush to buy real estate** to lock in that advantage.
2. This creates more buying pressure on a limited supply of housing, which **bids up home prices** until the advantage is priced in — i.e., prices rise to the point where even accounting for the inflation-hedge benefit, buying no longer looks obviously profitable at the margin.
3. Over decades, this repeated pattern (people expecting inflation → buying real estate as a hedge → home prices rising further) becomes what Murphy calls a "reinforcing cycle."
4. **The people who got in early (the current older generation of homeowners) already captured the benefit.** But now that home prices are so elevated, younger people — who don't have prior assets to leverage — are priced out and cannot access the same "cheap debt" advantage. Murphy's blunt conclusion: this is "partly why right now young couples can't buy a house."

### Extending the argument: institutional real estate buying
Horton and Murphy connect this directly to public anger about companies like Blackstone buying up large numbers of single-family homes to rent out. Murphy frames this not as an isolated corporate villain story, but as the *predictable result* of the incentive structure described above: if wealthy actors expect the dollar to be debased (lose value) in the coming years, real estate is an obvious place to park money — pushing prices higher and further squeezing out ordinary buyers.

### The political dimension: why housing prices are "protected"
Horton raises a claim (attributed to a reported/quoted statement from President Trump in a "gaggle" with reporters a few weeks before this conversation) that older Americans' home equity is often their primary source of wealth — and if home prices were allowed to fall to make housing more affordable for younger buyers, it would financially devastate many older, non-wealthy homeowners.

Murphy's response connects this to a broader historical shift: he notes that mid-20th-century economists (citing Mises's writing from the 1940s–50s) described average households saving through things like bonds and life insurance — not stock portfolios or real estate. Murphy argues that the **unanticipated inflation of the 1970s destroyed the reliability of those older, fixed-dollar savings vehicles**, which is what pushed households toward assets that could "respond to inflation" — first mutual funds and diversified stock portfolios, and increasingly, home equity. This shift, in Murphy's telling, is why housing wound up occupying such an outsized and politically sensitive role in retirement security — which in turn explains why there's enormous political pressure (from both political factions, in their telling) to prevent home prices from falling significantly.

*(This entire section blends Murphy's economic mechanism, both speakers' interpretation of political incentives, and a claim about a public political statement — presented in the conversation as their read on current events, not as a neutral summary.)*

## Part 8: Wages, Inequality, and Who Benefits First From New Money — Cantillon-Style Reasoning

Horton connects the inflation discussion to wage stagnation and inequality, citing charts (often used, he notes, by left-leaning organizations to criticize tax policy) showing that **wages stopped tracking productivity growth starting in the early 1970s** — not, as those same organizations often imply, because of 1980s Reagan-era tax cuts.

Murphy's explanation for the timing: **1971 is when Richard Nixon took the U.S. off the gold standard entirely** (ending the last formal link between the dollar and gold). Murphy's argument is that this shift explains the wage/productivity divergence because it enabled sustained monetary inflation from that point forward.

The mechanism Murphy describes for *why* this creates inequality (rather than just general price increases) is important and specific:

> New money created by the Fed is **not distributed uniformly** to everyone at once (it's not as if everyone simply wakes up with a bigger bank balance). It enters the economy **through specific channels** — financial markets, large institutions, asset purchases — and the people and institutions closest to those channels benefit first and most. People "downstream" (ordinary wage earners) benefit later, if at all, and by the time higher prices reach them, their wages haven't caught up.

Murphy connects this directly to what he calls a "financier class" — people positioned to see monetary and inflation trends coming and shift their portfolios accordingly (e.g., into real estate, gold, or other assets that rise with inflation) — profiting from being early, in a way ordinary wage earners structurally cannot.

### Horton's extension: immigration as a wage-suppression tool
Horton connects this monetary framework to a political argument he says JD Vance made on the Joe Rogan podcast: that big business supports not just legal immigration but *illegal* immigration specifically because it creates downward pressure on wages, particularly in industries like agriculture, hotels, and food processing (Horton names Tyson Chicken as an example) — and, per Horton, tech companies pursuing this dynamic with skilled workers from countries like India, and a similar effect on the trucking industry.

Horton's framing: this is not simply about labor supply and demand in the abstract — he argues it's businesses "using the government to wage a class war against their own employees," specifically to suppress the wage increases that workers would otherwise be able to demand as compensation for inflation-driven cost-of-living increases.

**This section is clearly Horton's political interpretation** (built on Murphy's monetary framework, but extending it into immigration policy, which Murphy does not independently comment on in this part of the transcript).

### A related observation: the unemployment rate doesn't reflect the reality
Murphy adds a data-based point: the official U.S. unemployment rate cited in the conversation (around 4.2%) is historically very low — which should theoretically mean workers have lots of leverage to demand raises or switch jobs freely. But Murphy argues that doesn't match how workers actually seem to feel or behave, and floats this as evidence that "the official numbers don't make sense" relative to the Fed's own decision to hold interest rates steady even though (per Murphy) the Fed's own measures of price inflation are running hotter than its stated target.

## Part 9: Where Are We Now? The Yield Curve, Real Estate, and Predictions About a Coming Crash

This final section is explicitly speculative — both speakers frame it as forecasting, not settled fact, and Murphy is notably candid about the limits of his own track record here.

### The inverted yield curve, explained simply
Murphy explains a key Austrian-endorsed recession indicator:

- The "yield curve" plots interest rates (yields) on U.S. government bonds across different loan durations — 3 months, 1 year, 10 years, 30 years, etc.
- **Normally**, longer-duration loans carry *higher* interest rates than short-term ones (lending the government money for 30 years is riskier/less liquid than lending for 3 months, so you demand more return).
- When the curve **"inverts,"** short-term rates rise *above* long-term rates — an unusual, historically reliable warning sign.
- Murphy's explanation for *why* this happens, tied back to Austrian theory: during a monetary boom, people develop expectations of ongoing inflation, so long-term rates stay elevated to reflect that. When the central bank gets worried and "slams on the brakes" (raising short-term rates sharply, since the Fed has more direct control over short-term rates than long-term ones), short-term rates can shoot up above long-term rates — producing the inversion.
- Historically, Murphy says, every time the yield curve has inverted and then returned to normal since World War II, a recession has followed.

### Murphy's own track record and candor about uncertainty
This is a good example of Murphy expressing genuine uncertainty rather than overconfidence:

- The yield curve inverted in 2019, and Murphy expected a crash. Then COVID hit, causing an obvious (if externally triggered) economic collapse. Murphy acknowledges this is an ambiguous "vindication" — critics could reasonably say he "got lucky" because of COVID, since it's unclear what would have happened to the economy without the pandemic.
- The curve inverted again more recently and, at the time of this conversation, has been un-inverting — which by the historical pattern should mean a recession is coming within about 12 months. But Murphy explicitly admits: **"I'm surprised it's lasted this long."** He acknowledges that if another year passes without a significant crash, "that will be hard to explain" — a direct, unhedged statement of uncertainty about whether his framework's predictive track record will hold up this time.

### Is AI propping up the economy?
Horton raises the idea that the U.S. may effectively already be in a recession, masked by speculative enthusiasm around AI companies (with some concern about "circular" contracts between AI firms propping up reported growth), possibly echoing the dot-com bubble.

Murphy's view, stated plainly: he does think AI reflects genuine, substantial productivity improvements likely to unfold over the coming decade (he mentions using Claude at his own company and being impressed by it) — but he's careful to separate that belief from his recession forecasting. He explicitly says AI **can't be his "get out of jail free card"** for why his predicted crash hasn't materialized yet, acknowledging that something else must be delaying the pattern he expects.

### Real estate specifically
Murphy adds a real-estate-specific data point: the Case-Shiller national home price index (a common measure of average U.S. home prices) is at all-time highs even after adjusting for inflation — higher than the peak of the mid-2000s housing bubble — though it has been roughly flat/plateauing recently. He also notes the Fed made a choice during COVID to buy large amounts of mortgage-backed securities (a way of supporting the housing market) and is now letting those holdings run off its balance sheet faster than it's reducing its Treasury bond holdings — which Murphy interprets as the Fed prioritizing support for the Treasury/government debt market over the housing market, suggesting (in his view) that real estate specifically may be more vulnerable than the broader economy.

### A political timing observation
Both speakers note that the Fed began raising interest rates in 2022–23 (with cuts starting around September 2024) while Biden was still in office — which Horton finds notable, since higher rates risk triggering the kind of economic downturn that would typically be politically damaging to the sitting administration. Murphy confirms the basic timeline (rates rising through '22–'23, the Fed's asset holdings peaking and starting to shrink around May 2022) without offering a strong political explanation of his own — this observation is presented more as raw historical/timeline information than a fully developed causal argument.

## Summary: How the Pieces Fit Together (Their Framework, in One Picture)

If you strip away the historical detail, the underlying causal chain Horton and Murphy build across the conversation looks like this:

1. **Money creation is not neutral.** New money enters the economy through banks and financial channels, not evenly across the population — so it benefits those closest to the source first (Part 8).
2. **Fractional reserve banking lets the banking system as a whole expand credit far beyond its cash reserves**, through a chain of deposits and re-lending across many banks (Part 3).
3. **A central bank acting as "lender of last resort" removes the natural discipline** that would otherwise keep this credit expansion in check, because banks know they'll be bailed out (Part 2).
4. **This tends to produce cycles of unsustainable booms followed by crashes** — as in the 1920s (Part 5) — because artificially cheap credit encourages malinvestment that has to eventually be corrected.
5. **Government intervention after a crash (like wage-fixing in the early 1930s) can turn a short, sharp correction into a prolonged depression**, by preventing prices and wages from adjusting to their new market-clearing levels (Part 5).
6. **Because people learn to expect ongoing inflation, they shift their savings into inflation-hedging assets like real estate**, which over decades drives up home prices faster than incomes — locking out younger generations who don't already own assets (Part 7).
7. **This creates enormous political pressure to prevent asset prices (especially housing) from falling**, since so much of the population's wealth and retirement security is now tied up in those inflated asset values (Part 7).
8. **Wage earners, meanwhile, are structurally last in line** to benefit from new money and slowest to see their income keep pace with rising prices — which Murphy and Horton connect to widening inequality since the early 1970s, when the dollar's last link to gold was severed (Part 8).

The conversation ends without a confident resolution about *when* the next major correction will happen — Murphy is explicit that his own predictive track record has had to be revised more than once, even while he maintains that the underlying theoretical mechanism (illustrated by tools like the yield curve) remains sound in his view.
