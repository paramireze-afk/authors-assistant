# PCR, Kary Mullis, and the Testing Debates

## What PCR is

The polymerase chain reaction is a way of copying a specific stretch of DNA over and over until there is enough to detect. Each cycle heats the sample to separate the DNA strands, cools it so short "primer" sequences can latch onto the target, and lets an enzyme build new copies. Every cycle roughly doubles the target, so 30 cycles can turn a single molecule into about a billion copies.

For RNA viruses like HIV and SARS-CoV-2, the RNA is first converted into DNA, a step called reverse transcription. That is why COVID tests were technically "RT-PCR."

In modern "real-time" PCR, the machine watches a fluorescent signal as copies build up. The **cycle threshold (Ct)** is the cycle at which the signal crosses the detection line. A low Ct means there was a lot of target to begin with; a high Ct means very little. About 3.3 extra cycles corresponds to roughly ten times less starting material.

PCR's great strength is also its main interpretive challenge: it is extraordinarily sensitive. It can find genetic fragments, but it cannot by itself tell you whether those fragments came from an active, contagious infection.

## Who Kary Mullis was

Mullis conceived PCR in 1983 while working at Cetus Corporation, a biotech company in Emeryville, California. He shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. Paul Rabinow's *Making PCR* tells the fuller story: Mullis had the key idea, but turning it into a reliable technique took a team at Cetus, and questions about how much credit belonged to whom were contested for years.

Mullis was openly unconventional, and he said so himself. His memoir, *Dancing Naked in the Mind Field*, is a candid and entertaining book in which he discusses his doubts about HIV alongside his skepticism of human-caused climate change, his interest in astrology, his LSD use, and a strange nighttime encounter with what he described as a glowing raccoon. Supporters see this as the mark of a fearless independent thinker. Critics see it as a reason not to treat his Nobel in chemistry as authority on virology or epidemiology. Both views are worth keeping in mind when weighing his statements.

## Mullis's criticism

### The HIV/AIDS question

Mullis's main dispute was not with PCR as a technology. It was with the claim that HIV causes AIDS. In his memoir, he describes searching for a single scientific paper that proved the link and being unable to find one that satisfied him. He became an ally of Berkeley molecular biologist Peter Duesberg, who argued that AIDS was caused by recreational drug use and by the antiviral drug AZT rather than by a virus. Mullis wrote the foreword to Duesberg's *Inventing the AIDS Virus* (1996).

Journalist Celia Farber, in *Serious Adverse Events*, gives the most sympathetic account of this movement. She portrays Mullis, Duesberg, and others as dissenters punished for challenging a powerful research establishment and pharmaceutical interests.

### His remarks about PCR

In interviews during the 1990s, Mullis made the point that PCR can detect small amounts of nearly anything in nearly anyone, and that finding a trace of a virus does not prove it is causing disease. He objected specifically to the use of PCR-based "viral load" measurements as evidence that HIV causes AIDS. He also spoke dismissively of Anthony Fauci's scientific understanding, remarks that Robert F. Kennedy Jr. later highlighted.

His general point about sensitivity is correct and is taught in every molecular diagnostics course. The question is what follows from it.

### What happened next

The scientific community did not accept Mullis's and Duesberg's position, and the evidence since then has moved further against it. Two books by critics of the dissent movement lay this out:

- **Seth Kalichman's *Denying AIDS*** examines the dissenters' arguments in detail. Kalichman, an HIV researcher, documents how combination antiretroviral therapy, which targets HIV specifically, turned AIDS from a near-certain death sentence into a manageable condition. If HIV were not the cause, drugs that suppress only HIV should not have worked.
- **Nicoli Nattrass's *The AIDS Conspiracy*** and her earlier *Mortal Combat* trace what happened when South Africa's government under President Thabo Mbeki took AIDS dissent seriously and delayed providing antiretroviral drugs. A 2008 Harvard study (Chigwedere et al.) estimated that the delay cost more than 330,000 lives and led to about 35,000 infants being born with HIV that could have been prevented.

PCR viral load testing also proved its worth in HIV care. The PARTNER studies followed couples in which one partner had HIV and found no sexual transmission when the partner with HIV had an undetectable viral load. This is the basis for "Undetectable = Untransmittable," and it depends directly on the PCR measurements Mullis criticized.

## The COVID-19 chapter

### Mullis and COVID

Kary Mullis died in August 2019, months before COVID-19 appeared. He never commented on COVID testing. During the pandemic, his older HIV-era remarks circulated widely as if they were about COVID, along with some quotes attributed to him that have no traceable original source, such as the claim that he said PCR "can't detect infection." Anyone citing Mullis on COVID should be clear that they are applying his 1990s comments to a situation he never addressed.

### Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s argument

In *The Real Anthony Fauci* (2021), Kennedy draws on Mullis's statements to argue that PCR was misused during the pandemic: that a tool suited to research was used for mass diagnosis, that high cycle thresholds inflated case counts, and that this inflated sense of crisis helped justify lockdowns and other policies. The book was a bestseller and remains the most widely read version of this critique.

It is worth noting that Kennedy has served as Secretary of Health and Human Services since February 2025, so he now oversees the CDC, FDA, and NIH. Readers who are wary of government sources may want to weigh his book as the work of an author written before he took office, and his current statements as those of an agency head.

### The cycle threshold controversy

This debate was real and involved serious researchers, not just outside critics.

**Where the concern had merit:**

- Many labs called a sample positive if virus was detected anywhere up to about 37 to 40 cycles. Analyses of testing data in 2020 suggested that a large share of positives in some datasets would have been negative with a cutoff around 30.
- Didier Raoult's group in Marseille, itself known for challenging official positions, found that the chance of growing live virus from a sample dropped steeply as Ct rose. Most low-Ct samples grew virus; very few above the mid-30s did.
- High-Ct positives often came from people at the tail end of an infection, sometimes weeks after they stopped being contagious. Counting them as new cases likely inflated case numbers somewhat and caused some unnecessary isolation.
- No test is perfect. When very few people in a population are infected, even rare false positives can make up a meaningful share of all positive results (see the box below).

**Why lowering the cutoff wasn't a simple fix:**

- A high-Ct result is not a false positive in the usual sense. The test found real viral RNA; the open question was whether the person was infectious.
- A high Ct can mean an infection that is clearing, but it can also mean one that is just beginning, or a poorly collected swab from someone with plenty of virus.
- Ct values are not standardized. A Ct of 30 on one test platform does not equal 30 on another.
- Lowering the cutoff would have increased false negatives, which were the bigger problem in practice. A 2020 analysis (Kucirka et al.) found PCR missed about two-thirds of infections the day before symptoms began, and still missed around one in five at its best point, about three days after symptoms started.

> **The base-rate problem, in numbers**
>
> Imagine testing 100,000 people when only 0.1% are infected, using a test that catches 80% of infections and wrongly flags 0.1% of uninfected people.
>
> - 100 people are infected → 80 test positive
> - 99,900 are not infected → about 100 test positive anyway
>
> More than half of the positives would be false, even though the test is "99.9% specific." If the false-positive rate is 0.01% instead, only about 10 false positives occur. PCR's real-world specificity was generally very high, but this arithmetic is why critics' concerns were strongest during periods of very low infection.

### Were asymptomatic carriers an artifact?

A common belief is that high cycle thresholds produced so many false positives that "asymptomatic carriers" were largely a testing illusion. The evidence does not support that, because asymptomatic infection was confirmed in ways that do not depend on Ct cutoffs:

- **Antibodies.** Blood studies found antibodies in many people who never had symptoms. Antibodies are produced only after a real infection.
- **Viral loads.** In Vo', Italy, nearly the entire town was tested in early 2020. About 40% of infected residents had no symptoms, and their viral loads were similar to those of people who were sick. A South Korean study found the same thing. If asymptomatic positives were mostly artifacts, they would have clustered at high Ct values, and they did not.
- **Live virus** was grown from samples taken from asymptomatic people.
- **Transmission chains** were traced back to people who had no symptoms, or had not yet developed them.

There is a fair point on the other side, though. Many people labeled "asymptomatic" when tested were actually presymptomatic and fell ill days later. Early estimates that 40 to 45% of infections were asymptomatic were probably too high. Later reviews that followed people over time put the truly asymptomatic share at roughly 20 to 35%.

### Legitimate criticism of government testing

Skepticism of agencies during COVID was not without grounds. In February 2020, the CDC's first test kits contained a faulty component that produced inconclusive results, and problems at the agency's lab delayed widespread U.S. testing for weeks during the period when the virus was spreading undetected. Michael Lewis's *The Premonition* and Scott Gottlieb's *Uncontrolled Spread* both document this in detail. Lewis in particular portrays the CDC as slow, cautious, and more focused on protecting itself than on responding. Notably, the main failure in both accounts was too little testing, too late, rather than too much.

## Takeaways

1. **PCR is a powerful, very sensitive tool.** Mullis was right that detecting genetic material is not the same as proving active disease. That caution applies to any PCR result.
2. **Mullis's dissent was about HIV causing AIDS, not about PCR being unreliable.** His position was rejected, and the real-world consequences of acting on it in South Africa were severe.
3. **Mullis never addressed COVID.** Applying his remarks to COVID testing is an interpretation by others.
4. **The cycle threshold debate was legitimate.** High Ct cutoffs likely led to some overcounting and to people isolating after they were no longer contagious.
5. **Asymptomatic infection was real.** It was confirmed by antibodies, viral culture, and transmission tracing, none of which depend on where labs set their Ct cutoffs.
6. **Government agencies did make serious testing mistakes**, and well-documented books by authors outside those agencies describe them. Those failures mostly ran in the direction of too little testing rather than too many false positives.
