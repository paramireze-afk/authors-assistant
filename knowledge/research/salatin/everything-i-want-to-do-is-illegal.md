# Everything I Want to Do Is Illegal: War Stories from the Local Food Front

> Reference note: no copy of this book was available to consult directly. This document was reconstructed from publisher materials, book-summary services, interviews with Joel Salatin, reviews, and secondary discussion of the book and its companion works. Direct quotations are kept short and attributed; most content below is paraphrase and synthesis. Treat chapter-level claims as reliable secondhand reconstruction, not verbatim text, and verify against the book itself before quoting Salatin directly in published writing.

## 1. Bibliographic Note

- **Full title:** *Everything I Want to Do Is Illegal: War Stories from the Local Food Front*
- **Author:** Joel Salatin, owner/operator of Polyface Farm, Swoope, Virginia (Shenandoah Valley)
- **Published:** 2007, self-published through Polyface Inc. (Salatin has historically distrusted mainstream publishing gatekeeping as much as regulatory gatekeeping)
- **Origin:** Grew out of a widely circulated essay/article in which Salatin listed the everyday farm activities he was legally barred from doing — the essay became the book's first chapter
- **Length/scope:** xvii + 358 pages, organized as a sequence of topic chapters, each pairing a specific regulatory battle with broader commentary
- **Chapter list (as documented):** The Original Essay · Raw Milk and Dairy · PL 90-492 · Custom Beef · Bacon · Salmonella · 1,000,000 Mile Chicken · Organic Certification · Government Grants: Best Management Practices · Government Grants: Conservation Easements · Restaurants · Predators and Endangered Species · Sawmills Are Out · Zoning · Labor · Housing · Insurance · Taxes · Avian Influenza · Bioterrorism · National Animal Identification System · Mad Cow · Animal Welfare · Options
- **Position in Salatin's body of work:** Marks his turn from technical farming manuals (*Salad Bar Beef*, *Pastured Poultry Profits*) toward social/political commentary, continued in *Folks, This Ain't Normal* (2012) and *The Marvelous Pigness of Pigs* (2016)

## 2. Author Context (relevant to reading the book correctly)

Salatin self-identifies as a **"Christian libertarian environmentalist capitalist lunatic farmer."** This label matters as an interpretive key: he is not writing from a conventional left/right, pro-business/pro-environment axis. His positions combine:

- Evangelical Christian theology (creation care, stewardship, a graduate of Bob Jones University)
- Libertarian/constitutionalist politics (right of contract, property rights, distrust of federal agencies)
- Environmentalist practice (regenerative, ecological, chemical-free land management)
- Entrepreneurial capitalism (direct marketing, value-added products, profitable small business)

Family background is directly relevant to his regulatory worldview: his parents lost a farm in Venezuela to political upheaval/expropriation before buying the Virginia land that became Polyface. Property insecurity and government overreach are not abstractions for Salatin — they are family history. His grandfather was an early follower of J. I. Rodale (founder of the organic gardening movement), which situates Salatin inside a multi-generational alternative-agriculture lineage that predates the word "organic" being coopted by USDA certification.

Salatin came to broader public attention through Michael Pollan's *The Omnivore's Dilemma* and the documentary *Food, Inc.*, which is worth noting as a **tension in his own story**: he became a national voice for local food partly through the very forms of mass media and centralized distribution his philosophy is skeptical of.

## 3. The Book's Governing Thesis

**Salatin's direct claim:** The American regulatory system — food safety law, zoning, building codes, labor law, environmental law, tax law, and USDA/FDA rule-making — was built around and for large-scale, centralized, industrial agriculture. Rules that sound neutral or protective in the abstract ("food safety," "worker safety," "environmental protection," "uniform building standards") in practice impose fixed compliance costs that industrial operations can absorb and small/diversified operations cannot. The result is not neutral regulation of a marketplace — it is a structural thumb on the scale that:

1. Makes small-scale, local, transparent food production illegal or economically impossible
2. Forces consumers into a narrower, more homogenized, less accountable food supply than they would choose freely
3. Disguises industry protectionism as public-health or safety concern
4. Removes the farmer-consumer relationship (trust, visibility, direct accountability) and replaces it with a bureaucratic seal of approval that consumers are told to substitute for actual knowledge of their food's origin

**His proposed remedy is not zero regulation in the abstract**, but *scale-appropriate* rules, radical reduction of federal involvement, and restoration of the individual's **right of contract** — the constitutional and common-law principle that two willing parties (farmer and customer) should be free to transact directly without a third-party bureaucratic veto, provided they aren't defrauding or coercing anyone.

## 4. Salatin's Vocabulary — A Working Glossary

These terms recur across the book and Salatin's broader work. They are reusable as standalone glossary entries or as vocabulary for future writing.

- **"Everything I want to do is illegal"** — the organizing complaint of the book: normal, historically unremarkable farm activities (on-farm slaughter, on-farm sales, hosting a farm tour for a fee, hiring a teenager, building an unconventional house) have been criminalized or regulated into impracticality.
- **"Beyond organic"** — Salatin's term for his own farming standard, deliberately positioned as *higher* than USDA Organic certification, not lower. Signals that certification is a floor, not a ceiling, and that a bureaucratic label is a poor proxy for actual practice.
- **"Pigness of the pig" / "chickenness of the chicken"** — animals should be raised in a way that allows them to express their inherent, God-given physiological and behavioral distinctiveness (rooting, foraging, scratching, moving) rather than being treated as interchangeable manufacturing units. Later expanded theologically in *The Marvelous Pigness of Pigs*: denying an animal's nature is a small-scale version of denying the Creator's design.
- **"Food police"** — Salatin's term for the regulatory apparatus (state agriculture departments, USDA, FDA field agents) enforcing food law against small producers, often used with the observation that consumers are told highly processed shelf-stable products are "safe" while raw milk, home-canned goods, and pastured meat are treated as hazardous.
- **"Alphabet agencies"** — shorthand for the proliferation of federal and state bodies (USDA, FDA, EPA, OSHA, etc.) whose overlapping jurisdiction multiplies compliance burden without necessarily improving outcomes.
- **"Farmadisney"** — his sarcastic name for the zoning classification risk of turning a working farm into something legally treated like a theme park the moment it charges for tours, education, or on-site experiences.
- **The "pet food loophole"** — a real workaround some farmers use: labeling human-grade meat as "pet food" to legally sell direct-to-consumer without USDA slaughter inspection, since pet food is regulated differently than human food. Cited by Salatin as an absurd but telling symptom of a system where the *label* matters more than the *actual quality* of the product.
- **"1,000,000-mile chicken"** — Salatin's ironic counter-image to the well-known "food travels 1,500 miles" statistic; used to illustrate how convoluted supply chains and regulatory routing can make a chicken's path from farm to plate absurdly indirect even when better options exist a few miles away.
- **"Manufactured product" reclassification** — his example of driving meat off-farm to a USDA facility and back: the *same meat*, having left and re-entered the property, becomes legally a "manufactured product" subject to a different, heavier regulatory regime than food sold directly off the farm where the animal was raised — a case study in how regulatory categories, not the substance of the product, determine legality.
- **"Salad bar beef"** — Salatin's branding for grass-finished beef raised via rotational/management-intensive grazing, cattle moved daily to fresh pasture ("mob grazing" style) so they eat the most nutritious plants at their peak ("the ice cream of the grass") without overgrazing.
- **"Stacking enterprises" / polyculture** — Polyface's core production model: multiple species used sequentially and symbiotically on the same land (cattle graze, chickens/turkeys follow days later to scratch through manure and eat fly larvae and spread it as fertilizer, pigs later root and aerate compost) — an ecological mimicry argument used throughout Salatin's work as evidence that diversified small farms can out-produce and out-resile monocultures per acre.
- **"Know your farmer"** — Salatin's alternative to certification-based trust: relationship and visibility (a consumer who can visit the farm) as the real guarantee of food quality and safety, versus a remote label or inspection stamp.
- **"Food Emancipation Proclamation"** — a phrase Salatin has used (including in later interviews) to frame food-choice deregulation in the language of a civil-rights-style liberation, casting current law as an unjust restriction on a basic personal freedom.
- **"Bacteriosapiens"** — a term Salatin has used (crediting raw-dairy advocate Mark McAfee) to describe humans as beings that live in symbiosis with bacteria rather than separate from them — used to argue that the "sterile equals safe" framing behind much food regulation misunderstands human biology.
- **PL 90-492** — the federal statute providing a *custom-exempt* slaughter option: an animal can be processed for the exclusive use of its owner(s) without full USDA inspection. Salatin treats this narrow legal exemption as one of the few functioning cracks in an otherwise closed system, and much of his practical guidance involves working within or expanding such exemptions.

## 5. Core Arguments, Organized by Theme

### 5.1 Regulation as Structural Favoritism, Not Neutral Safety Policy

Salatin's central move throughout the book is to take a regulation that sounds reasonable in isolation and show its *asymmetric* effect by scale:

- **Zoning** treats farms as sites of raw production only; anything beyond that (processing, direct sales, education, agritourism) triggers commercial/industrial classification, permitting requirements, and infrastructure mandates (paved parking, handicap access, septic capacity) sized for a business model the small farm never intended to run.
- **Building codes** are written around standardized, code-book construction assumptions; unconventional but structurally sound methods (his son's house project, an earth-sheltered dream-house concept with a greenhouse roof and methane digester) are rejected not because they're unsafe but because they don't match a bureaucratic checklist. A poultry building strong enough for its original purpose is disqualified from reuse as a meat-processing facility purely because of a change in its *classification*, not its actual structural adequacy.
- **Food safety law** (culminating, in Salatin's account, in the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2010) expands federal inspection authority — including warrantless on-farm access — under a "science-based" standard that, in his framing, actually means "industrial-model-based," since a diversified pasture operation and a single-commodity CAFO cannot be safely regulated by the same rulebook.
- **Environmental and endangered-species regulation** designed with large feedlot pollution in mind gets applied to midsized diversified farms whose actual ecological footprint is different in kind, not just degree.

**Recurring pattern to extract:** *A rule calibrated to control the excesses of concentrated industrial production, when applied uniformly, becomes a barrier to the decentralized alternative that would otherwise compete with and correct that industrial production.* This is arguably the single most reusable idea in the book — a general principle about regulatory capture and non-scalable compliance costs that extends well beyond agriculture.

### 5.2 Government Response to Crisis Protects Incumbents

Salatin's chapters on Salmonella, Avian Influenza, Bioterrorism, Mad Cow, and NAIS share a consistent argument: government responses to food-safety scares are reactive, politically driven, and structured to reassure the public and protect large industry rather than to address root causes.

- He attributes disease outbreaks (avian flu, foodborne pathogens) largely to the conditions of concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs) — high-density confinement, monoculture, long supply chains, centralized processing — rather than to small, pastured, transparent operations.
- New tracking/identification mandates (like NAIS, the proposed nationwide livestock tracking and premises-registration system) get proposed after a crisis, but the compliance burden falls disproportionately on small producers with few animals per unit of paperwork, while the *actual* disease risk is argued to originate upstream in industrial systems.
- Indemnification/bailout requests after outbreaks (e.g., avian flu compensation for confinement operations) socialize the cost of a business model's inherent fragility.

**Historical context:** NAIS was proposed by USDA/APHIS starting in 2004, met sustained grassroots opposition (constitutional, religious-liberty, and cost objections; documented resistance in Wisconsin, Texas, Kentucky, Vermont, and elsewhere), was declared "voluntary at the federal level" in 2006 after backlash, and was effectively abandoned in 2010. This is a case where the *outcome* of a multi-year, small-farmer-led resistance campaign against a federal program aligns with Salatin's central argument — worth treating as a documented, verifiable data point rather than just Salatin's opinion.

### 5.3 Organic Certification as Inadequate Proxy

Salatin is notably **not** a defender of USDA Organic certification, despite being widely (and inaccurately) assumed to be an "organic farmer." His argument:

- Certification is a paperwork and input standard, not a comprehensive guarantee of ecological or nutritional quality — it says nothing about scale, animal behavior, distance traveled, labor conditions, or diversity of operation.
- The same federal agency (USDA) he holds responsible for decades of poor nutrition guidance (promoting hydrogenated oils, discouraging traditional fats, endorsing feeding animal byproducts to livestock) is, in his view, an implausible guardian of the "organic" ideal.
- Large-scale "industrial organic" (e.g., certified organic operations selling into big-box retail, or imported organic grain of dubious provenance) can technically comply with the letter of certification while replicating many of the problems — long supply chains, confinement housing at legal minimums, absentee ownership — that the organic movement was originally a reaction against.
- His alternative is **relationship-based trust** ("know your farmer") over **label-based trust** (a certification stamp), arguing that a consumer who can visit a farm and see it directly gets a more reliable signal than a logo.

*Historical context worth preserving:* the word "organic" predates USDA certification by decades — J. I. Rodale popularized it in the late 1940s as a farming philosophy, not a legal label. USDA's National Organic Program didn't formalize certification until 2002. Salatin's generational connection to Rodale-era organic farming (via his grandfather) is part of why he treats the *federalization* of the term as a kind of capture or dilution of an idea that originated outside government.

### 5.4 Economic Reasoning: Fixed Costs, Scale, and Market Access

A recurring economic argument, made concrete in the zoning, building-code, insurance, and tax chapters: many regulations impose **fixed costs** (a certain size of processing facility, a certain septic system, a certain insurance policy, a certain paperwork/reporting burden) that do not scale down with farm size. A large operation spreads a fixed compliance cost over thousands of units of production; a small or diversified farm faces the same fixed cost over a tiny fraction as many units, making the *effective* per-unit regulatory cost far higher for the small producer. This is functionally a barrier to entry that entrenches incumbents regardless of the letter of any individual rule's intent — an argument compatible with, but arrived at independently of, mainstream economic critiques of regulatory capture and barriers to entry.

**Where this connects outward:** this is the same underlying mechanism economists describe when discussing minimum efficient scale, regulatory capture, and the compliance-cost advantage of incumbents in any heavily regulated industry (banking, pharmaceuticals, telecom) — a good bridge concept for writing that wants to generalize beyond agriculture.

### 5.5 Property Rights and the Right of Contract

Salatin frequently invokes the **constitutional right of contract** — that two willing, informed adults should be able to buy and sell directly without a licensed intermediary or government approval standing between them, so long as no fraud or coercion is involved. He treats most food regulation as a *de facto* suspension of this right in the name of protecting consumers from choices they are, in his view, fully capable of making for themselves (raw milk being the paradigmatic example). This pairs with a broader property-rights argument: what a farmer may build, sell, or do on their own land should be primarily a matter between the farmer and their direct customers, not a matter for zoning boards or distant regulatory agencies calibrated for industrial-scale operations.

### 5.6 Entrepreneurial Response: Work the Margins, Don't Wait for Permission

Salatin is not primarily a lobbyist for legislative change (though he supports policy reform); his practical posture is entrepreneurial defiance and creative compliance:

- Use existing legal exemptions fully (e.g., custom-exempt slaughter under PL 90-492) rather than assuming the whole system is closed
- Find legally available workarounds (the pet food loophole) even when they expose the absurdity of the rules
- Build direct, face-to-face markets (farmers markets, farm stores, CSA-style relationships, farm tours) that route around industrial distribution infrastructure entirely
- Treat regulatory friction itself as informational — a sign of where the system is most misaligned with genuine consumer demand, and therefore where entrepreneurial opportunity is greatest

## 7. Ethical and Theological Dimension

Though *Everything I Want to Do Is Illegal* is primarily a regulatory war-stories book, Salatin's later, more explicit theological argument (developed fully in *The Marvelous Pigness of Pigs*) is present here in embryonic form and is important context: he connects respecting the "pigness of the pig" to a broader claim that how a society treats the distinctiveness of creation reflects how it will treat people — that mechanizing and standardizing animals is a small-scale rehearsal for mechanizing and standardizing human beings and human choice. This is a **direct claim of Salatin's own**, not a broader implication he leaves implicit — he draws the animal-treatment/human-treatment analogy explicitly in his writing and interviews.

## 8. Historical Context Worth Preserving

- **Jeffersonian agrarianism** — Salatin repeatedly frames the independent small farmer as inheriting an American ideal (the "educated agrarian proletariat" he attributes to Jefferson) that current land-use and business law has turned into a legal "nuisance" — he cites real examples of subdivision covenants explicitly restricting "farming and other nuisances."
- **Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA), signed January 4, 2011** — expanded FDA authority to inspect farms without a warrant based on a "science-based" risk standard; Salatin's critique (echoed across his interviews around this period) is that the law was passed by well-intentioned advocates without adequately accounting for the difference between industrial and diversified small-farm risk profiles.
- **Organic movement's pre-federal history** — J. I. Rodale's 1940s coinage of "organic" as an ecological farming philosophy predates and is philosophically distinct from USDA's post-2002 legal certification regime.
- **NAIS timeline (2004–2010)** — proposed, resisted, declared voluntary, ultimately abandoned; a useful real-world case study of a centralization proposal defeated by decentralized, grassroots opposition.
- **Raw milk enforcement history** — a wave of state-level raids, sting operations, and prosecutions against small dairies through the 2000s and 2010s (documented at length in David Gumpert's *The Raw Milk Revolution*, for which Salatin wrote the foreword) forms the immediate backdrop for Salatin's dairy and "food police" chapters.

## 9. Connections to Other Thinkers and Works

- **Michael Pollan, *The Omnivore's Dilemma*** — the chapter following Salatin and Polyface Farm is what introduced Salatin to a mainstream audience; Pollan frames Salatin's local food chain as a *movement*, not merely a market — a framing Salatin himself embraces.
- **Robert Kenner, *Food, Inc.*** — documentary appearance that further mainstreamed Salatin's critique of industrial agriculture.
- **David E. Gumpert, *The Raw Milk Revolution*** — Salatin wrote the foreword; the book documents the regulatory crackdown on raw milk dairies in detail and is a natural companion source for the dairy chapters.
- **Weston A. Price Foundation** — frequent ally/venue for Salatin's arguments about traditional foods, raw dairy, and pastured poultry; useful for corroborating nutritional and food-tradition claims.
- **J. I. Rodale / *Organic Gardening and Farming*** — intellectual ancestor of the "organic" concept Salatin later distances himself from in its federalized form.
- **Thomas Jefferson (agrarian ideal)** — recurring rhetorical touchstone, not a source Salatin engages with in scholarly depth, but a symbol he returns to.

## 10. Where Other Sources Push Back

In the interest of accurate reconstruction (not just sympathetic summary), documented points of pushback and complication:

- **Accessibility/equity critique:** at least one published review notes that Salatin's opposition to accommodations like handicap access for on-farm retail does not have to reconcile with the needs of a broader public the way general commercial policy does — a tension between individual-farm autonomy and universal-access goals that Salatin's framework does not resolve.
- **Scope critique:** the same review flags that Salatin includes a section expressing his views on abortion within a book about food and farming regulation, which some readers found jarring and only loosely connected to the book's central argument — worth noting as a genuine feature of the book's structure (Salatin does explain his own reasoning for the inclusion) rather than an invented criticism.
- **Public-health counter-framing:** mainstream food-safety agencies (FDA, CDC) maintain that pasteurization prevents specific, well-documented pathogen risks (*E. coli*, *Listeria*, *Salmonella*) in raw milk, and that risk falls disproportionately on children, pregnant women, older adults, and immunocompromised people — a direct empirical counterpoint to Salatin's "sterile confused with safe" framing that a balanced treatment of the raw milk debate should include.
- **Regulatory-economics literature on NAIS** treats the program's failure as explainable by ordinary cost-benefit and adoption-economics factors (fixed technology costs, uneven distribution of costs vs. benefits between the cow-calf and feeding/packing sectors) rather than purely as centralization-vs-liberty; useful for a more technical/economic treatment of the same episode Salatin narrates politically.
