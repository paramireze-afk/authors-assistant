# 34 Days on Water: Michael on Fasting, Simplicity, Trust, and Rescuing Old Books

Broadcasting live from Japan at roughly 1:35 in the morning, Michael spent close to an hour talking with viewers about the water fast he had just finished, what fasting has taught him, why he refuses to overcomplicate it, and how the COVID era permanently changed the way he evaluates information. The stream fought connection problems throughout, but the audio held up, and Michael worked through a steady flow of viewer comments before signing off to prepare for early-morning interviews.

Michael repeated that he isn't paying attention to it until there are people dead in the streets, while warning that "they'll use it as reason to do lockdowns or something." He also pushed back on the idea that one reported death should lead to global measures:

## The Fast: 34 Days, Guided by "My Little Voice"

Michael had just ended a water fast partway into its 34th day. His previous fast, about two and a half years earlier, went a little past 40 days.

He stressed that he never set a target. He might have stopped after three days; instead he continued until an internal signal told him he was done.

> "I don't listen to advice, outside advice on when to stop my fast. I listen to my little voice. My little voice said to stop, so I stopped."

The moment came while he was lying down watching his wife, Masako, nurse their baby son, Kiro. When the midwife came in, he asked for a couple of eggs because he "could just feel like I should probably stop now." He says 40 days would have been easy, and that he believes he could have gone to 50 or 60, though 40 remains his longest.

### Where the hard part really is

According to Michael, the meaningful change happens early:

- **Days 1–4 or 5** are where the big shift occurs and where some people experience "turbulence." This time, he had none. Viewer Richard Zar, who was also fasting, reported the same.
- **Day 10 versus day 20** is "really not much" different, which is why, once past day 10, he tends to think he may as well continue.

### Water, and (almost) nothing else

Michael says this fast taught him he didn't need salt, contrary to what "everybody knows":

- **Days 1–14:** water only.
- **Roughly days 14–17:** some salt, plus fresh-squeezed lemon juice (he estimates six or seven calories' worth) for about three or four days. He didn't enjoy it.
- **Day 17 through day 34:** back to plain water.

He estimates his total salt intake for the entire fast at around three tablespoons, all consumed during that one block.

### Breaking the fast — no ritual

There was no ceremony. His first bite was half of a small avocado with salt ("that salt was great"), followed by two boiled eggs and food taken from Masako's plate. Later that day he had cashews and young coconuts. The next day, he simply ate normally.

He addressed the common advice that you should taper out of a fast for as long as half of the fasting period:

> "That's not how I've been doing my more recent fasts."

He acknowledged that he went into the fast abruptly, coming off a big meal whose final bite was matcha ice cream, and said he didn't baby himself on the way out either, though he didn't gorge.

> "I should have three molecules of avocado and up to six molecules of boiled egg. I was like, 'No.'"

He concedes there may well be better ways to enter and exit a fast, as worked out by true experts who have studied it deeply, but he prefers simplicity because he has other things on his mind.

### A side benefit: freedom from meals

Once you have fasted for five, ten, or twenty days, he says, skipping a few meals becomes trivial. That streamlines life because you no longer have to plan your day around eating:

> "I'll go 10 days without food without even blinking an eye."

Responding to a viewer, he joked that his gut biome is very happy the fast is over: "Who caused the famine down here?"

## The Spiritual Side of Fasting

Michael says that once you're past the first few days and into the 10-, 20-, or 30-day range, fasting has a genuine spiritual element.

> "There's something to it that is very valuable… I don't know how to explain it."

He describes people with a deep spiritual life as having "an inner power that comes from both outside of you and inside of you. It's not either-or. It's both." Fasting, he says, helps him connect with that more strongly. Silence and solitude can do the same; he recalled long, quiet walks far into the Himalaya, watching avalanches.

> "What value can you put on that? Because it's valueless."

His recommendation: if your health allows it, do it. For long fasts, he notes, you need to have some fat to draw on.

## Keep It Simple: Michael's Approach to Fasting

Simplicity was the central theme of the stream. Michael argues that fasting is "stunningly simple," and that keeping it that way makes people more likely to actually do it.

**For a three-day fast, what do you need to do?**

> "Drink water. And that's it. Now, people say, 'Yeah, but what supplements?' And I'll say, 'Drink water.' … I've had to say that like a thousand times."

His answers to common questions:

| Question | Michael's answer |
|---|---|
| When should I start? | When you feel like it. |
| What should I eat beforehand? | Whatever you want. |
| When should I break the fast? | When you want to. |
| Should I weigh myself? | If you want, or don't. He weighed himself every two or three days, but recommends staying away from the scale because "the scale will psych you out." |
| What ketone sticks should I use? | He didn't use any. No ketone sticks, no blood pressure checks, no special devices. |

He anticipates criticism ("I know people will say that guy doesn't know what he's talking about") and points to his own record: two long fasts done exactly this way.

### Why elaborate protocols don't work for him

He walked through the math of a fully "phased" 40-day fast: 20 days of special preparatory diet, 40 days of fasting, and 20 days of tapering out. That's 80 days, close to three months of constant attention.

> "When do I get a three-month block to do something like that? But I do have time blocks where I can go, 'Hey, I'm just going to start fasting right now.' And all I need is water."

### Advice for first-timers

To Cynthia Hillyard, who said she was inspired to try a three-day fast, Michael said she'll like it, and offered a key piece of advice: listen to the little voice. If it stops being enjoyable, stop right then.

> "When you give yourself that leeway, then you'll come back and do it again… You don't look back at the fast like, 'Oh, that was so terrible.' You just go until you're done and you're done and then come back and do it again. And then the next time you'll do it longer."

He also noted that it may take a few attempts before the first few days stop being a big deal, after which four or five days becomes "nothing."

To Roy Leech, who asked whether fasting at 73 is worth considering, Michael said it can be extremely beneficial, but that Roy needs to research it in relation to his own health, since Michael doesn't know what the age parameters are. He added that you have to "throw out about 95% of what you hear right off the top," because many people with no real knowledge of fasting will insist you need salt, potassium, magnesium, and so on.

## Mastery, Intuition, and the Japanese Love of Simplicity

Michael connected his preference for simplicity to Japanese culture, citing Japanese archers who shoot intuitively, without visibly aiming. He recalled one archer who said his sensei made him practice drawing the bow 100,000 times before he was allowed to shoot an arrow.

The same principle, he says, applies to shooting, slingshots, and professional sports: after enough repetition, you release and simply know where it will land.

> "Young people should always strive to get good at at least one thing in their life that's physical. Get really good. Not just a little bit good, but really, really good… That changes the way you look at the world."

Viewer Hans said he spent 10,000 hours becoming a very good swimmer, and Michael agreed that 10,000 hours is about where something stops being a skill and becomes "part of you." He said he wishes he had done more than listen to music in his own education, and that much of what he listened to was music he "should not have been listening to."

Simplicity carries over to daily life. He wears the same few shirts over and over. The brown shirt he has worn around the world is the one he wore while helping deliver Kiro, receiving his son and handing him to Masako. He plans to retire that shirt, have it pressed, and keep it.

A viewer recommended David Tame's *The Secret Power of Music*, which Michael noted. Discussing music's place in ancient myth, he recalled (without naming it) the story of a musician who persuaded the underworld to return his dead wife but was told never to look back, and linked it to the worldwide folk motif of "never look back," including Lot's wife turning into a pillar of salt while looking back at Sodom and Gomorrah.
