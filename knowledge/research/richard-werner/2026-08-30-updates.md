# Richard Werner on Iceland, the Euro, and Central Bank Power

Two days before Icelanders voted on whether to reopen negotiations to join the European Union, economist Richard Werner sat down for a long-form interview to make the case against membership. Werner is a German economist best known in academic and financial circles for coining the term "quantitative easing" in the 1990s and for decades of research into how commercial banks create money — work that sits outside mainstream central-bank orthodoxy but has a real following among critics of conventional monetary policy.

The conversation ranged from technical questions about credit creation to sweeping claims about the political nature of the EU itself. Below is a guide to the main threads of his argument, in his own terms, with context added for readers who aren't monetary-policy specialists — and with a clear line drawn between what Werner asserts and what is independently verifiable.

## The core thesis: sovereignty is the real question

Werner frames the referendum as being about two separate things that people often collapse into one. First, there's the specific question of what the EU *is* and what joining it would mean practically. Second, there's a more philosophical question: does a small country need to join a larger bloc to be viable at all, or can it thrive independently?

His answer to the second question is emphatic: small, independent countries not only *can* thrive, they have structural advantages — in his framing, an individual's voice and influence matter more in a nation of 400,000 than in a bloc of 350 million. He's skeptical of what he calls a "stampede" mentality, where countries join supranational unions simply because others have, without weighing what's actually given up.

## Why he opposes the euro specifically

Werner's central economic claim is that adopting the euro would mean surrendering monetary policy — which he calls "the most powerful of all economic policies" — to the European Central Bank (ECB). His reasoning: whoever controls a country's money supply effectively controls the country's economic destiny, because they decide how much credit is created and where it flows.

He says he has publicly warned about this since the 1990s, when he was chief economist at a British investment bank in Tokyo, citing his own letters published in the *Financial Times* at the time. This detail is checkable in principle (Werner has published extensively on Japanese monetary policy from that era), though the specific 1990s letters weren't independently verified for this summary.

### His critique of the ECB

Werner's harshest and most detailed claims center on the ECB's track record since its founding around 1999–2000. His argument, in sequence:

- **A 2003 forecast.** In his book *Princes of the Yen*, Werner says he analyzed the ECB's legal structure — under the Maastricht Treaty, later folded into the Lisbon Treaty — and concluded it had been given more unchecked power than any other central bank in the world, with no government, parliament, or public prosecutor able to hold it accountable. From this, he says he predicted the ECB would go on to generate credit-fueled asset bubbles, banking crises, and mass unemployment.
- **The eurozone periphery crisis.** He attributes the property bubbles and subsequent crashes in Ireland, Portugal, Spain, and Greece in the 2000s–2010s directly to ECB-driven policy: first pushing banks to expand property lending, then tightening credit and triggering the bust, sovereign debt crises, and — in Greece's case — a decade of high youth unemployment.
- **Germany's property bubble.** He argues the ECB then did the same thing to Germany from roughly 2009–2022, inflating a property bubble that he says is now in "slow collapse" and will produce a banking crisis in the coming years.
- **The 2021–22 inflation surge.** Werner rejects the popular explanation that inflation in that period was driven by the war in Ukraine or energy prices. Instead, he says he forecast the inflation spike in mid-2020 — 18 months in advance — based purely on the scale of money creation by the Federal Reserve and ECB starting in March 2020, and warns of a "second bout" of inflation in the next couple of years.
- **Bank consolidation.** He states that roughly 6,000 banks — mostly small, local, and community banks — have disappeared in the eurozone over the ECB's 26-year history, which he sees as evidence of a deliberate drive toward centralizing financial control.

**A note on verification:** These are Werner's own characterizations of a highly technical and genuinely contested area of economics. It's true that the eurozone periphery experienced severe property-credit bubbles and crises in the 2008–2013 period, and that European bank consolidation has been substantial; the causal story Werner tells — that this was a deliberate or foreseeable result of ECB incentive structures rather than a more conventional account involving global financial contagion, national fiscal policy, and private-sector risk-taking — is his own theoretical framework, not a consensus view among economists. Mainstream and ECB accounts of the same events differ significantly, attributing the crisis to a mix of factors including pre-crisis private lending booms, fiscal imbalances, the 2008 global financial crisis, and design flaws in the eurozone's fiscal (not just monetary) architecture.

### Central bank digital currency (CBDC)

Werner is sharply critical of the ECB's planned "digital euro," a form of central bank digital currency. His argument: a CBDC would let the ECB open accounts directly for the public, cutting commercial banks out of the picture. He predicts that if a banking crisis coincides with the CBDC's rollout, people would move their money out of banks and into central bank accounts, effectively making the ECB "the only bank in town" — a structure he compares to the Soviet Union's single state bank. He put the likelihood of this playing out at "90 to 95%," and said "close to 100%" given what he described as the EU's increasingly dictatorial character.

This is a prediction and a normative judgment, not a settled fact — plans for a digital euro are real and publicly documented, but their eventual design, adoption, and effects remain genuinely uncertain and are actively debated among economists, regulators, and privacy advocates.

## How Werner thinks money and banking actually work

A recurring theme is Werner's view — which is a real, published strand of monetary economics, though a minority one relative to how central banks and textbooks traditionally describe money creation — that commercial banks create new money each time they issue a loan, rather than simply lending out existing deposits. In this framework:

- **Where credit goes matters more than the interest rate.** Werner argues central banks have distracted the public by focusing on interest rates, when the real lever of economic power is the *quantity* and *allocation* of credit.
- **Credit for productive investment is good; credit for consumption or assets is bad.** Bank lending channeled into business investment, he argues, creates goods, jobs, and non-inflationary growth. Lending for consumption fuels inflation. Lending for property and financial assets fuels bubbles and instability — and, in his view, has never been necessary or beneficial to the wider economy.
- **Interest rates follow growth, not the other way around.** Werner cites his own 2018 paper in the journal *Ecological Economics* ("Reconsidering Monetary Policy," co-authored with an econometrician) as evidence that the standard assumption — lower rates cause higher growth, higher rates suppress it — has it backwards: growth drives interest rates, not vice versa. This is a genuine, peer-reviewed publication, though its conclusions are a minority position relative to mainstream monetary economics, which generally does find effects (albeit debated in size and timing) running from policy rates to economic activity.

Werner frames money creation as "a public privilege" that should be tied to adding real value to the economy — which for him means it should overwhelmingly go to productive business lending, not mortgages or consumer credit.

## His alternative for Iceland

Rather than adopting the euro, Werner recommends Iceland restructure its own central bank and banking sector along lines he associates with the postwar German Bundesbank and the high-growth economies of East Asia (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Thailand, and later China). His specific proposals:

1. **Make the central bank genuinely accountable** to parliament, with a mandate for growth and low inflation, not just an inflation target — modeled, he says, on how Germany's Bundesbank operated compared to its more independent, less accountable predecessor, the Reichsbank.
2. **Radically expand the number of banks.** Werner wants many more small, local, community, and cooperative banks — potentially one per town of around 10,000 people — with a fast, simple registration process instead of a slow licensing bureaucracy.
3. **Restrict what banks are allowed to lend for.** Under his proposal, banks would lend *only* for productive business investment — not mortgages, not consumption, not asset purchases.
4. **Move mortgage lending to non-bank institutions.** These would be private companies funded through bond issuance (not deposits, which would make them banks in substance), meaning they lend out *existing* money rather than create new money. Werner argues this breaks the link between money creation and property prices, which he says would stabilize — and eventually make more affordable — housing.
5. **Simplify banking regulation drastically**, cutting compliance costs so banks can lend more cheaply, and abandoning the EU's Basel-influenced framework, which he says wrongly encourages mortgage lending over business lending.
6. **Address Iceland's inflation-indexed mortgages by treating the cause, not the symptom.** Iceland is unusual in indexing outstanding mortgage principal to inflation, meaning borrowers can make every payment and still see their debt grow. Werner argues the fix isn't better indexation — it's eliminating the high inflation that makes indexation seem necessary in the first place, via the credit-allocation reforms above.
7. **Focus the export economy on high value-added goods and services** — citing Iceland's fisheries and tourism sectors as examples where capturing more of the value chain (e.g., higher-end products, direct sales to restaurants, premium tourism) could strengthen the currency through stronger export performance.

Werner claims that countries following a similar "high-growth" credit-allocation model achieved sustained growth rates around 15% for extended periods — doubling national income roughly every four and a half years. This is a strong empirical claim; sustained double-digit growth did occur in several East Asian economies during their postwar catch-up periods, though economists differ widely on how much credit is available at what rates should be assigned to credit-allocation policy specifically, versus factors like technology transfer, demographics, export access, and industrial policy more broadly.

## The more contested political claims

Werner's argument goes well beyond economics into a set of assertions about the EU's political character and history. These are presented here as his claims, clearly separated from established fact:

- **The EU is not a democracy, in his view**, because he argues the European Parliament cannot itself write or initiate legislation — that power sits with the European Commission. It is true that under EU treaties, the Commission holds the sole formal right of legislative initiative in most policy areas, with Parliament and the Council of the EU amending and approving proposals; whether this structure amounts to Werner's characterization of a "rubber stamp" system or a workable division of powers is a matter of ongoing political and legal debate, not something resolved by the structural fact alone.
- **He compares the EU's institutional design to the Soviet Union's**, arguing both used elected assemblies without real lawmaking power to claim democratic legitimacy. This is a rhetorical and historical analogy Werner draws himself; it is a contested framing, not a neutral description.
- **He cites EU sanctions against individuals** — naming a Swiss national, Jacques Baud, and referencing a Liechtenstein national in Iceland and unnamed German journalists — as evidence the EU punishes people for expressing opinions, particularly regarding Russia and Ukraine. The EU does maintain sanctions regimes, including asset freezes against certain individuals, and some of these have been publicly controversial and legally challenged; the specific characterization that these actions were purely about "uttering opinions" reflects Werner's interpretation of contested and, in some cases, individually disputed cases, not a verified universal description of EU sanctions policy.
- **He claims decision-making rules are being changed** to remove smaller countries' veto rights in response to Hungary's obstruction of consensus votes, reducing small-state influence. Discussions about moving from unanimity to qualified-majority voting in specific EU policy areas (like foreign policy) are real and ongoing, driven partly by frustration with individual countries blocking action — Hungary has indeed used vetoes on various issues. Whether this amounts to what Werner describes was not independently verified for this summary and should be checked against current EU institutional reporting if it matters to your decision.
- **He describes Brussels as dominated by corporate lobbying**, pointing to the length and complexity of EU financial regulations (which he says are often drafted with input from London-based lawyers and banks) and to the 2014 resignation of the entire European Commission over a corruption scandal. The full Commission (under Jacques Santer) did resign in 1999 amid a corruption and mismanagement scandal — a well-documented historical event, though it happened in 1999, not "not long ago" as framed, and predates the current Commission by over two decades. Claims about EU auditors declining to sign off on accounts, and lobbying's specific role in drafting legislation, are Werner's characterization and were not independently verified here.
- **His most striking claim is that the EU was founded as a CIA project.** Werner asserts that founding figures — he names Jean Monnet, Robert Schuman, and Paul-Henri Spaak — were "literally" CIA agents, citing declassified CIA documents, and that the goal was to create an undemocratic structure the United States could control top-down, quoting Henry Kissinger's apocryphal line about wanting a single phone number for Europe. **This is a real, longstanding claim in some historical and euroskeptic literature** — there is documented evidence that the CIA covertly funded pro-European-federalist organizations in the 1950s (including groups linked to Monnet) as part of Cold War strategy, which is sometimes summarized in shorthand as "CIA funding of European federalism." However, mainstream historians generally do not characterize Monnet, Schuman, or Spaak as CIA agents in the sense of being recruited operatives directing policy on the agency's behalf; the claim that they were personally "revealed... as having been CIA agents literally" is a stronger and more specific assertion than the historical record on covert Cold War funding of European federalist groups typically supports, and is disputed by many historians of European integration. Readers interested in this should look at both the declassified-documents literature on Cold War covert funding and mainstream histories of the Monnet/Schuman era, which offer differing interpretations of the same underlying facts.

## Werner's closing argument

Werner ends by urging Icelanders to reject EU membership on both economic and political grounds: economically, because he believes Iceland can achieve high growth and low inflation on its own by reforming its central bank and banking structure; politically, because he views the EU as an unaccountable, corruption-prone, and — in his most extreme framing — externally controlled institution incompatible with Iceland's long democratic tradition, tracing back to the historic Icelandic "thing" (assembly). He recommends further reading via his own book (*Princes of the Yen*), his website (professorw.org), and a paid newsletter on Substack where he tracks credit creation and policy globally.
