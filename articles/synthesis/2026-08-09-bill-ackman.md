# The Billion-Dollar Short: Bill Ackman's War on Herbalife

*A narrative history of the biggest bet of one hedge fund manager's career, the rival billionaire who took the other side, and the government investigation that landed somewhere neither of them predicted.*

---

## Part One: The Bet

### Who is Bill Ackman?

William "Bill" Ackman grew up in the wealthy New York suburb of Chappaqua, the son of a commercial real estate financier. After Harvard Business School, he co-founded a small investment partnership called Gotham Partners in the early 1990s, then in 2004 started over with his own firm, **Pershing Square Capital Management**, seeded with $54 million (about $50 million of it borrowed from a single backer). Pershing Square is what's known as an **activist hedge fund**: rather than passively buying stock and hoping it goes up, Ackman buys large stakes in companies, then agitates loudly — through letters, presentations, board fights, and media appearances — to force the company to change.

By the time Herbalife entered his sights, Ackman was already one of the most famous investors in America. His signature move had been a multi-year bet against the bond insurer MBIA, which he believed was concealing catastrophic risk on its books; he was mocked and investigated for years before the 2008 financial crisis proved him right and handed Pershing Square roughly $1.4 billion in profit. He'd also engineered a spectacular rescue of the bankrupt mall operator General Growth Properties. Ackman had a well-earned reputation: brilliant, combative, evangelical about his own convictions, and willing to spend years — and enormous sums of his investors' money — defending a thesis that started out looking crazy.

### What is Herbalife?

Herbalife is a Los Angeles-based company that sells nutrition shakes, protein powders, weight-loss products, and skin care — not in stores, but through a network of independent distributors, a structure known as **multi-level marketing (MLM)**. Distributors buy products at wholesale prices, sell them (in theory) to customers at retail prices, and can also earn commissions by recruiting other people to become distributors underneath them. Founded in 1980, Herbalife went public in 2004, and its stock rose roughly 1,000% between 2005 and 2012 under CEO Michael Johnson, a former Disney executive who took sales from about $1.2 billion in 2003 to more than $4 billion by 2012.

MLM companies occupy a legally contested middle ground. A legitimate direct-selling business earns its money from real product sales to real customers. A **pyramid scheme** earns its money mainly by charging new recruits to join and climb the ladder, with product sales serving as a kind of legal cover — meaning the business eventually runs out of new recruits and collapses, and the vast majority of participants lose money. American law has never drawn this line with a bright statutory test; courts and regulators look at the substance of how the compensation actually flows.

### Ackman becomes convinced

Ackman's team began digging into Herbalife's business quietly in 2011 and 2012, well before the stock became a Wall Street obsession — it had already drawn skeptical questions from short-seller David Einhorn on a May 2012 earnings call, which briefly rattled the shares. Ackman's investigators visited "nutrition clubs," interviewed former distributors, and combed through Herbalife's own disclosures about who was actually earning money. What they found, in Ackman's telling, was a familiar MLM pattern: a tiny fraction of "distributors" — often cited as roughly the top 1% — captured the overwhelming share of the compensation paid out, while the large majority earned little or nothing after accounting for the cost of the products and materials they were required to buy. Ackman became convinced Herbalife wasn't really selling nutrition products to the public; it was selling a business opportunity to its own distributors, most of whom would never make money and many of whom would lose their savings.

### The billion-dollar public short

On December 19, 2012, CNBC's Kate Kelly broke the news that Pershing Square had built a roughly **$1 billion short position** in Herbalife — about 20 million shares. The stock immediately fell 10% in six seconds, triggering a trading halt. The next day, at an investor conference in Manhattan, Ackman delivered a three-hour presentation titled, roughly, "Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?" — laying out, slide by slide, his case that Herbalife was, in his words, an illegal pyramid scheme that would eventually collapse to zero. By Christmas Eve, five days after the CNBC report, Herbalife's stock had fallen 42%, from around $41–42 to roughly $24, wiping out close to $2 billion of the company's market value.

**How a short sale actually works, in plain English:** When you short a stock, you borrow shares you don't own and sell them at today's price, with an obligation to buy them back later and return them. If the price falls before you buy them back, you pocket the difference. If the price rises instead, you still have to buy the shares back to close out the position — at a higher price than you sold them for — and you lose money. Unlike a normal stock purchase, where the most you can lose is what you paid, a short position has theoretically **unlimited downside**, because a stock's price can keep rising indefinitely. This is what made Ackman's bet so extraordinary: he wasn't risking a fixed sum. If Herbalife's stock rose and kept rising, Pershing Square's losses could keep growing right along with it — which is exactly what eventually happened.

Ackman didn't just place the bet privately; he announced it to the world, along with his reasoning, in what's sometimes called a "public short" or "activist short" — a much rarer and riskier move than an ordinary short sale. The logic was straightforward: if he could convince enough investors, journalists, and eventually regulators that his thesis was correct, the resulting pressure on the stock — through investor selling, business disruption, or outright government action — would help make the bet pay off. His financial interest and his public advocacy were, by design, the same project.

### The central tension

This is the question the rest of the story turns on. Ackman stood to make an enormous amount of money — potentially over a billion dollars — if he could persuade the public and the government that Herbalife was a fraud. That doesn't, by itself, mean his claims were wrong; a financial incentive to expose fraud isn't inherently corrupting, and plenty of legitimate whistleblowers and short-sellers are motivated by the prospect of profit. But it does mean that everything Ackman said, funded, and lobbied for over the next five years needs to be read with the awareness that the messenger had an enormous financial stake in the message being believed. As the story unfolds, keep both possibilities open at once: Ackman may have identified something real, and Ackman had every incentive to push the most damaging possible interpretation of it.

## Part Two: The Campaign

### Pershing Square's operation

What followed was not a normal short sale — it was a multi-year public campaign. Pershing Square built out a research and communications operation devoted almost entirely to Herbalife: hiring investigators, producing a running series of rebuttal presentations (Pershing eventually released dozens of these, including 18-plus videos by mid-2016), and building a website, FactsAboutHerbalife.com, to host its evidence. Who exactly was paid to build and run this operation, and how much, is detailed in Part Four.

### Building the public case

Ackman's core statistical claim — echoed across his presentations — was that Herbalife's distributor compensation was overwhelmingly concentrated at the very top of the pyramid, that most rank-and-file distributors earned little to nothing once their required purchases were subtracted, and that recruitment, not retail sales, was the real engine of the business. He and his team produced videos featuring former distributors describing debt, drained savings, and businesses that never became profitable. He argued that because roughly 60–80% of Herbalife's U.S. distributor base was Spanish-speaking Hispanic, the company was disproportionately harming an already vulnerable community — a framing that became central to Ackman's public-relations strategy and to a political strategy built around Hispanic advocacy organizations and lawmakers.

### The other investors

Ackman's public short attracted a crowd almost immediately — some betting he was right, many betting he was wrong. **Dan Loeb** of Third Point started buying Herbalife stock right after the December 2012 presentation and disclosed an 8.24% stake in January 2013, worth roughly $300 million. **Robert Chapman** of Chapman Capital put over a third of his fund's capital into Herbalife shares, betting on a short squeeze. **George Soros**'s fund and consumer-goods veteran **Bill Stiritz** also built long positions. Most consequentially, **Carl Icahn** began buying in mid-January 2013 and would eventually become Herbalife's largest shareholder.

### Carl Icahn takes the other side

Icahn and Ackman already had history. In 2003, they had clashed over a deal involving Hallwood Realty; the dispute became a lawsuit that dragged on for eight years and ended with a court ordering Icahn to pay Ackman several million dollars. When Ackman went public against Herbalife, Icahn saw both a business opportunity and a chance for a rematch. His stated view was that Herbalife was a legitimate, profitable business unfairly targeted, and that the stock was undervalued given the fear Ackman had created. He built his stake aggressively — disclosed at around 13% by mid-February 2013 and rising past 23–25% over the following months, with board seats and approval to buy up to a quarter of the company. Unlike Ackman, Icahn wasn't running a research operation trying to prove a thesis about fraud; he was making a straightforward wager that the company would survive — a bet that happened to put him on a collision course with one of his oldest rivals.

**Why their incentives ran in opposite directions:** Ackman made money only if Herbalife's stock fell — ideally to zero. Icahn made money only if it rose. Herbalife's own management had an existential interest in defeating the pyramid-scheme allegation entirely. Each side had a very simple and very large financial reason to want the public to believe a particular version of the truth.

### The Battle of the Billionaires

The fight became genuinely personal, and it exploded into public view on live television. On **January 25, 2013**, Ackman was on CNBC's *Fast Money Halftime Report*, defending himself by phone against criticism Icahn had made about him the day before. The show's producers, tipped off that Ackman would be on air, invited Icahn to call in. What followed was roughly a half-hour of unfiltered, personal invective — Icahn calling Ackman a liar and saying he had one of the worst reputations on Wall Street, both men dredging up the old Hallwood Realty grudge — broadcast live while traders on the floor of the NYSE watched. It is remembered as one of the most extraordinary moments in the history of financial television, and it turned a niche hedge-fund dispute into a mainstream news story. The two men would eventually make peace, shaking hands publicly about eighteen months later.

## Part Three: The Political and Regulatory Pipeline

### The narrower question

Set aside whether Ackman was right or wrong about Herbalife's business. The documented question is: **how closely connected were Ackman's financial position, his privately funded research campaign, the political pressure that followed, and the market's reaction to that pressure?** None of what follows implies Pershing Square did anything illegal by lobbying while short — that's a legal and common (if unusual in scale) activity for an activist investor. What the record shows is how tightly the pieces fit together in time.

### The lobbyists Pershing Square hired

Before 2013, Pershing Square had never employed a registered Washington lobbyist. That changed as the campaign escalated. Filings tracked by OpenSecrets show Pershing Square retaining two firms in 2013: **Wexler & Walker Public Policy Associates**, a long-established bipartisan lobbying shop founded in 1981 by Anne Wexler, a former Carter White House aide, and chaired by Robert S. Walker, a former Republican congressman from Pennsylvania — a generalist Washington-access firm with no particular prior connection to Hispanic-community politics; and **the Moffett Group**, run by **Toby Moffett**, a former Democratic congressman from Connecticut. According to Politico's contemporaneous reporting, the Moffett Group's specific assignment was to help gather signatures from members of the **Congressional Hispanic Caucus** on a letter urging the FTC to investigate Herbalife. (Exact figures paid to each firm are covered in Part Four.)

Beyond these registered lobbyists, the *New York Times* reported that two individuals with direct prior staff ties were separately engaged to work congressional offices: **Larry Rasky**, a former aide to Senator Ed Markey during his years in the House, and **Malcolm Grace**, a former aide to Representative Linda Sánchez — both lobbying, in effect, offices where they had once worked. This matters for the chronology below, since Markey and Sánchez became the two members of Congress most closely associated with formal letters to the FTC and SEC. Separately, Ackman engaged directly and personally: reporting describes him or his staff speaking with roughly 15 members of Congress over the life of the campaign, along with a scattering of state and local officials, most of whom made no formal public move on the issue. Only one member of the U.S. Senate, Markey, ultimately sent letters to federal regulators.

### The Linda Sánchez letter: a documented chronology

- **June 5, 2013**: Rep. Linda Sánchez (D-Calif.) sent a letter to FTC Chairwoman Edith Ramirez asking the agency to examine Herbalife's marketing practices, citing prior letters from the National Consumers League (March 12, 2013) and the Hispanic Federation (May 17, 2013).
- **The following day**, according to the *Times*'s reconstruction, Ackman hosted a dinner with several other hedge fund managers at a Manhattan steakhouse and told them Sánchez had sent the letter "the previous day" — before the FTC had even logged it as received and before it had been made public. Ackman, who had personally lobbied Sánchez, read from a copy of the letter on his phone.
- Sánchez's office has acknowledged sending a copy of the letter to Ackman's team roughly a month before issuing a public press release about it, and says the release — when it finally went out — was **dated to match the original June 5 send date rather than the actual release date**, after the *New York Post* reported the letter's existence about a week after Ackman's dinner.
- **Market reaction:** By most accounts, the Sánchez letter itself did not move Herbalife's stock in any measurable way when it eventually became public — a useful contrast with what happened eight months later.

What's documented: Ackman personally lobbied Sánchez's office; a copy of a not-yet-public congressional letter reached Ackman's team before it reached the FTC's own mailroom; the eventual public release carried a date that didn't match when it was actually released. What's inference rather than proof: exactly why Sánchez's office shared the letter early, and whether the backdating was intended to obscure the delay or was a more routine administrative choice — Sánchez's staff has offered its own explanation, and that explanation is part of the record too.

### The Ed Markey letters: a documented chronology, with clear price action

- **October 2013**: Markey's staff met with Ackman, one of roughly 15 congressional contacts Ackman's team made over the campaign.
- **January 23, 2014**: Markey sent detailed letters to the FTC, the SEC, and to Herbalife itself, citing complaints from Massachusetts constituents — including one family that said it had lost $130,000 and its entire 401(k) — and asking the FTC to examine whether Herbalife's practices amounted to a pyramid scheme. Markey wrote that he took "no position on the merits" of the allegations but wanted the practices examined.
- **Same day, market reaction:** Herbalife's stock fell roughly **10–14%** within hours of the letters becoming public — one of the sharpest single-day drops of the entire campaign.
- Markey later told the *Boston Globe* that his staff had not informed him Ackman stood to profit personally from the letters, and defended the letters as consumer protection, not favor-trading. A political rival, Hopkinton selectman Brian Herr, subsequently filed a Senate ethics complaint alleging Markey had acted to benefit a campaign donor. That complaint is an **allegation**, not an adjudicated finding — there is no public record of it resulting in a formal sanction.
- **March 12, 2014**: Herbalife disclosed that it had received a civil investigative demand — confirming the FTC had formally opened an investigation, an outcome Markey had explicitly urged in January.
- **Same day, market reaction:** Herbalife's stock fell a further **7–15%** (reports vary by measurement window) on confirmation of the formal FTC probe.

Taken together, the two Markey-related events produced the two largest, most clearly attributable stock-price reactions of the entire campaign, and both followed directly from a chain that ran from Ackman's team, through a congressional staff meeting, to a sitting senator's public letters, to a federal agency's public confirmation of an inquiry.

### Direct contact with regulators, and advocacy organizations

Contemporaneous legal commentary (April 2014) described Ackman's team as having made, alongside speeches and congressional outreach, "direct pitches to the SEC and FTC" — a **less precisely documented** claim than the congressional correspondence above, since there's no public record of specific meeting dates or content. What is clear is that Pershing Square's presentations were explicitly framed, from December 2012 onward, as evidence a regulator should act on, and that the FTC's eventual complaint tracked many of the same allegations.

Pershing Square's outreach also extended to civil-rights and consumer organizations serving Hispanic communities. The *Times* reported that letters urging FTC action came from groups including the Hispanic Federation and the National Consumers League, and that some individuals who wrote or signed such letters said they had done so after being contacted by Pershing Square representatives, while others said they didn't recall writing the letters credited to them. The one organization whose finances became directly, publicly entangled with Pershing Square — the League of United Latin American Citizens, which returned a $10,000 contribution — is covered in Part Four, alongside Herbalife's parallel funding of friendly advocacy groups.

### Reading the pipeline as a whole

Laid end to end, the documented sequence looks like this: a hedge fund with a billion-dollar financial interest in a negative outcome for Herbalife hires its first-ever Washington lobbyists in 2013, one of them tasked specifically with gathering Hispanic Caucus signatures; a sympathetic congresswoman's not-yet-public FTC letter reaches the fund's founder before it reaches the FTC; a senator whose staff met with that same fund four months earlier sends letters that immediately move the stock by double digits; the formal investigation those letters requested is confirmed seven weeks later and moves the stock again. None of that proves any individual letter or investigation was improperly obtained — lawmakers, advocacy groups, and even the FTC itself had access to the same publicly available evidence Ackman was citing, and multiple people independent of Pershing Square had raised similar concerns before Ackman ever shorted the stock. But the tightness of the timeline, and the fact that Ackman knew about at least one congressional letter before the agency it was addressed to did, is what makes this more than an ordinary case of a company facing regulatory scrutiny.

## Part Four: Follow the Money

This is the piece the person reading this article specifically wanted reconstructed: not just *that* both sides ran expensive campaigns, but *who was paid, for what, and how well that's actually documented.* The honest answer is that some of this is precisely sourced — disclosed lobbying filings, an ABC News investigation that produced a copy of a signed agreement — and some of it rests on one side's own, self-interested claims about what the other side spent. Both kinds of evidence are marked below.

### Pershing Square: what the ~$50 million reportedly bought

Ackman told reporters, and has repeated in interviews since, that Pershing Square spent roughly **$50 million** researching and publicizing its Herbalife thesis between 2012 and 2014 — separate from the cost of the trading position itself (Ackman told *Fortune* in 2016 that simply maintaining the short, through stock-borrow costs and hedging, was running at roughly **$100 million a year** by that point, an entirely different expense from the campaign spending discussed here). Herbalife's own spokesman offered a competing figure in 2015, telling reporters Ackman had "spent over $75 million orchestrating" the campaign — a number Herbalife had every incentive to inflate, just as Ackman had incentive to round his own figure down. Neither figure is an audited, itemized total; both are the self-reported claims of a party to the fight.

What the $50–75 million reportedly bought, to the extent it's documented:

- **Registered lobbyists**, for the first time in Pershing Square's history: **Wexler & Walker Public Policy Associates** (reported 2013 spending of roughly $90,000–$150,000, depending on the disclosure period counted) and **the Moffett Group** (roughly $48,000–$84,000 in 2013), per OpenSecrets-tracked federal lobbying disclosures.
- **A research and government-relations consultancy, Global Strategy Group**, which worked for Pershing Square gathering evidence and, per Ackman's own on-air acknowledgment in 2015, had staff who received subpoenas from the Department of Justice as part of a federal inquiry (discussed below) into whether people connected to the campaign made false statements to spur investigations.
- **Public-relations support**, reported as including **Rubenstein PR** (referred to in contemporaneous coverage as Pershing Square's press office) and, per secondary reporting citing *Vanity Fair*, **Edelman**. These relationships are documented in press coverage; specific fees paid are not public.
- **A paid whistleblower arrangement with a former Herbalife executive.** The most concretely documented single payment in this entire story: ABC News reported in April 2014, and Pershing Square itself subsequently confirmed and released a redacted copy of the agreement, that Ackman's firm had an indemnification arrangement with **Giovanni Bohorquez**, a former Herbalife senior executive who left the company in 2011 and had supplied information to the *New York Times* and to government investigators about alleged manufacturing problems. Under the agreement, Pershing Square had already paid Bohorquez **$80,000** and had committed to indemnify him for up to **$3.6 million over 10 years** in lost wages if he lost future employment because of his cooperation — conditioned on his continuing to testify truthfully and to actively seek other work. Pershing Square said this was one of several such indemnification arrangements with people cooperating as whistleblowers, though it said other arrangements were narrower, covering only legal fees and damages if Herbalife sued them, not lost income. Ackman defended the arrangement as "the right thing to do"; ABC News noted that neither Ackman's PR team nor Bohorquez's own account had disclosed the financial terms before the network's reporting surfaced them.
- **Travel and per-diem expenses for former distributors** who appeared in Pershing Square's videos and in the independent documentary *Betting on Zero* (which Pershing Square says it did not finance or control). Ackman stated publicly that participants were not paid appearance fees, only economy-class travel, moderately priced lodging, and a **$71-per-day** per diem — a much smaller and more conventional arrangement than the Bohorquez deal, and one Ackman volunteered unprompted, seemingly aware of the comparison the Bohorquez story invited.
- **A $10,000 contribution to LULAC** (the League of United Latin American Citizens) for a table at the group's legislative gala — later returned by LULAC once questions were raised about its advocacy against Herbalife appearing alongside Pershing Square money. LULAC's executive director, Brent Wilkes, said the group had separately spent more than $100,000 of its own funds on the Herbalife issue and returned the smaller Pershing Square contribution specifically to keep its campaign above suspicion.

### Herbalife: the counter-spend

Herbalife's own political and communications spending was, by every disclosed figure available, considerably larger than Pershing Square's — though, like Ackman's numbers, none of it has been consolidated into a single audited total either.

- **Federal lobbying**: OpenSecrets-tracked disclosures put Herbalife's 2013 federal lobbying spend at roughly **$1.34 million to $1.89 million**, depending on the source and reporting period — routed through the company's in-house lobbying entity, Herbalife International of America, plus outside firms including Downey McGrath Group, Ogilvy Government Relations, the Podesta Group, the Raben Group, and Glover Park Group.
- **A Hispanic-lobbyist roster assembled specifically to counter Ackman's framing**: prominent Latino lobbyists including Cristina Antelo at Podesta, Dean Aguillen and Moses Mercado at Ogilvy, and Robert Raben and Estuardo Rodriguez at the Raben Group — an explicit response, as a company representative said at the time, to Ackman's attempt to frame the fight as one of Hispanic exploitation.
- **Crisis communications**: **Sard Verbinnen & Co**, one of Wall Street's most established crisis-PR firms (founded by George Sard and Paul Verbinnen), was retained to run Herbalife's communications defense — a relationship Ackman himself acknowledged on air, telling CNBC the firm had done a "fabulous job" for Herbalife (while doing, in his view, a poor one for an unrelated Pershing Square target, Valeant). Specific fees are not public.
- **Litigation counsel**: reporting at the time named **Boies Schiller** and **Gibson Dunn** among Herbalife's legal team as government scrutiny intensified; fees are not public.
- **A paid senior advisor**: former Los Angeles mayor **Antonio Villaraigosa** joined as a senior advisor to CEO Michael Johnson and the board — a role that would ordinarily carry compensation, though no dollar figure for his arrangement has been publicly reported.
- **Grants to friendly Hispanic advocacy organizations**: Herbalife gave **$25,000 each** to the National Puerto Rican Coalition and the U.S. Hispanic Leadership Institute, according to *New York Times* reporting; both later joined groups that signed a "Friends of Herbalife" letter defending the company to lawmakers — the funded-advocacy mirror image of the LULAC episode above.
- **Political donations**: Herbalife's PAC and employees gave more than $20,000 to Rep. Xavier Becerra (D-Calif.) during the 2011–2012 cycle, making the company one of his largest donors, and the PAC made its first-ever contribution to the Congressional Hispanic Caucus's BOLD PAC in 2013 — the same year Pershing Square's lobbyists were trying to gather Hispanic Caucus signatures against the company. Becerra urged fellow Caucus members not to sign the Sánchez letter.
- **Direct market defense** (a different category from information spending, but part of the financial fight): more than **$160 million** in stock buybacks in January and February 2013 alone, part of a much larger multi-year buyback program that both signaled management confidence and worked directly against Ackman's short position.

### The government looked at the money too

The financial closeness of Ackman's campaign to the regulatory pressure it generated didn't go unnoticed by law enforcement. In March 2015, the *Wall Street Journal* reported that the FBI and federal prosecutors in Manhattan were examining whether individuals — including some hired by Pershing Square — had made false statements about Herbalife in order to trigger government investigations and depress the stock; Ackman confirmed on CNBC that people who worked for Global Strategy Group had received DOJ subpoenas, while maintaining that Pershing Square itself was "unaware of any statements that we have made that are untrue." Herbalife, separately, faced its own FBI and FTC scrutiny over its business practices during the same period. By early 2016, CNBC reported that both the inquiry into Herbalife's business and the inquiry into potential stock-price manipulation connected to Ackman's campaign had been closed without charges against either side — an outcome that is itself a documented fact, distinct from the underlying allegations, which were never proven or formally resolved one way or the other.

### What the money adds up to, cautiously

No public source ties every dollar on either side to a single, audited total, and both sides' headline figures ($50 million and $75 million on Ackman's side alone) come from parties with an interest in the number. What is solidly documented is the *shape* of the spending: Pershing Square's money flowed mainly into research, a first-ever lobbying operation, PR, and — most unusually — direct financial arrangements with individual sources, most notably a six-figure-and-rising payment to a single former-executive whistleblower. Herbalife's money flowed into a larger, more conventional Washington lobbying and legal operation, crisis PR, funded advocacy groups, and direct stock-market defense through buybacks. Both sides paid for advocacy-group goodwill in the same Hispanic-community space and had at least one of those payments become a public controversy — Pershing Square's LULAC contribution was returned; Herbalife's larger grants to friendlier groups were not.

## Part Five: The FTC's Verdict

### The settlement

On **July 15, 2016**, after roughly two years of formal investigation, Herbalife agreed to settle FTC charges of deceptive earnings claims and unfair business practices. The terms: Herbalife would pay **$200 million** in consumer redress — one of the largest such redress amounts the FTC had ever obtained — and would fundamentally restructure its U.S. compensation system, so that at least two-thirds of distributor rewards would have to be tied to *verified retail sales to real customers*, with no more than one-third tied to distributors' own personal consumption. The following January, the FTC began mailing redress checks to nearly 350,000 people who had lost money as Herbalife distributors.

### What the FTC actually found — versus what Ackman claimed

The FTC's complaint did describe practices that closely tracked much of what Ackman had alleged for years — reward payments driven by recruitment rather than retail sales, deceptive earnings claims, and substantial economic harm to distributors, particularly lower-income and Spanish-speaking ones. But the word **"pyramid"** does not appear in the FTC's complaint. When reporters pressed then-FTC Chairwoman Edith Ramirez directly on whether Herbalife had been cleared of being a pyramid scheme, she pointedly declined to say it had — "the word 'pyramid' does not appear in our complaint" was as far as she'd go, explicitly refusing to confirm the popular media takeaway that Herbalife had been fully vindicated.

In short: the FTC found serious, sanctionable misconduct and imposed one of its largest-ever settlements to fix it — but stopped well short of declaring Herbalife an illegal pyramid scheme that had to be shut down, which is what Ackman had predicted with total confidence since December 2012. Herbalife was allowed to continue operating, under restructured rules, rather than being dissolved. Pershing Square's own response claimed vindication anyway, arguing the FTC's factual findings amounted to a pyramid scheme "under the relevant court decisions," regardless of the label used, and predicting the restructuring would cause the business to collapse on its own. That prediction did not come true.

## Part Six: The Reckoning

### Ackman doubles down, then folds

Remarkably, the FTC settlement did not end Ackman's position. Pershing Square kept its short alive for another year and a half. In November 2017, Ackman restructured the bet, converting the short-stock position into put options (a way to keep a bearish bet alive with a capped, known-in-advance maximum loss). On **February 28, 2018** — five years and a month after his live-TV brawl with Icahn — Ackman told CNBC he had **exited the position entirely**. Herbalife's stock, which had been trading around $45 when he first disclosed the short in December 2012, closed that week near $92.

### How the trade actually performed

Estimates of Ackman's total losses vary depending on what's counted, but consistent figures across financial reporting put the loss at **roughly $500 million to $1 billion**, once trading costs and the tens of millions spent on the publicity and research campaign are included — one of the largest and most public losses of Ackman's career, arriving in the same rough period as a separate, unrelated multi-billion-dollar loss on Valeant Pharmaceuticals that badly dented Pershing Square's reputation and assets under management.

Carl Icahn's trade, by contrast, was a clear win. The day after Ackman's exit, Icahn told CNBC he had made about **$1 billion on paper**, still hadn't sold a single share, and said he enjoyed a good fight, especially when he won it — adding, without apparent irony, that Ackman had personally called to congratulate him.

### What ultimately happened to Herbalife

Herbalife survived — but not without further trouble of its own making. In 2020, separate from the Ackman fight entirely, Herbalife agreed to pay more than **$123 million** to the SEC and Department of Justice to resolve a Foreign Corrupt Practices Act case alleging a decade-long scheme of bribing Chinese officials to grow its business in China. As of 2026, Herbalife remains a public company generating roughly $5 billion in annual revenue, managing a heavy debt load recently refinanced in the range of $1.4–1.5 billion, and repositioning itself as a "health and wellness" brand. Its stock has recovered significantly over the past year, though it remains far below its 2013–2014 peaks. The company still operates on a multi-level, distributor-based sales model — the FTC's restructuring changed the *proportions* of how compensation is earned, not the fundamental business structure.

## Part Seven: Reading the Incentives

None of the participants in this saga were neutral, and it's worth laying their interests side by side rather than picking a hero:

- **Bill Ackman / Pershing Square** stood to gain roughly a billion dollars or more if Herbalife's stock collapsed, and financed that pursuit with a first-ever lobbying operation, a research consultancy whose staff drew federal subpoenas, and a six-figure-and-rising payment arrangement with an individual former-executive source — all in service of a case that ultimately proved half right.
- **Carl Icahn** stood to gain, and ultimately did gain, roughly a billion dollars if Herbalife's stock rose, and used his own considerable public platform and boardroom leverage to defend the company, without running a comparable political or financial-source operation of his own.
- **Herbalife's management** faced an existential threat and answered with a larger, more conventional Washington operation: lobbying spend several times Pershing Square's disclosed totals, a Hispanic-lobbyist roster built to order, a top-tier crisis-PR firm, funded advocacy groups, and over $160 million in stock buybacks in the campaign's opening months alone.
- **Members of Congress** who engaged — chiefly Sánchez and Markey — each said their motivation was constituent protection, not favor-trading with a hedge fund donor; both faced public questions about the closeness of their offices' contact with Ackman's team that neither fully resolved.
- **Advocacy organizations** split along lines that tracked, imperfectly, who was funding them — LULAC returned Pershing Square's money to protect its independence, while several Herbalife-funded Hispanic organizations later signed a letter defending the company.
- **Regulators**, ultimately, produced the only assessment in this story not funded by either billionaire — and their conclusion split the difference between the two men's narratives: real, sanctionable harm to real people, but not the "illegal pyramid scheme" that would have justified shutting Herbalife down entirely.

## Open Questions

- **What should the rules be** when a person with a massive financial stake in a regulatory outcome is also paying, directly and individually, the sources supplying evidence for that outcome — as in the Bohorquez arrangement — while simultaneously funding lobbyists to press the same case in Congress? Public disclosure of the financial interest happened here (eventually, and not always voluntarily); whether disclosure alone is a sufficient safeguard is a separate question.
- **Why did two of the largest single-day stock moves in the entire campaign** trace directly back to a congressional relationship a hedge fund's lobbyists had cultivated only months earlier? What does that say about how much regulatory attention, and how much market-moving credibility, a well-funded private campaign can generate on its own?
- **How should the public weigh a documented $80,000-and-rising payment to a whistleblower** against Herbalife's own, much larger and more diffuse spending on lobbyists, friendly advocacy grants, and a senior political advisor? Is a single, personal financial arrangement with a source more or less significant than a company spreading a comparable sum across dozens of institutional relationships?
- **What does it mean that both sides fought this out primarily through paid lobbyists, funded advocacy groups, and compensated sources**, rather than through litigation or pure market competition? Whether that's a healthy check on corporate misconduct or a worrying template for how contested facts get established in public life is a genuinely open question — and one this story raises without answering.
