# The Bank of England

## Why Griffin Keeps Returning to It

Griffin treats the Bank of England as the **historical prototype** for everything else in the book. It is not a foreign curiosity or a footnote in monetary history — it is, in his telling, the institution where the modern central-banking "trick" was first assembled, tested, legally protected, and proven profitable enough to be copied everywhere else on earth, including by the Federal Reserve System two centuries later. He calls the Bank of England "the world's first central bank," and every mechanism he later documents in the Federal Reserve — the creation of money out of nothing, the partnership between bankers and politicians, the legal insulation of banks from the consequences of their own failures, the financing of war through debt rather than taxation — he traces back to this single 1694 institution.

He states this connection explicitly in his chapter summary: the Bank of England was "formed in 1694 to institutionalize fractional-reserve banking," and it "introduced the concept of a partnership between bankers and politicians" that would be copied, in his account, by every major central bank that followed — including, ultimately, the one at the center of his entire book.

---

## Founding: 1694 and "The Cabal"

### The financial crisis that created the opening

Griffin sets the scene in the England of the 1690s: the country was financially exhausted after decades of war with France and internal civil conflict. King William III urgently needed new revenue for the War of the League of Augsburg (1693) but had no good options. Twenty years earlier, King Charles II had simply repudiated over a million pounds owed to a group of goldsmiths, wiping out roughly ten thousand depositors — an act of state betrayal still fresh in investors' memory. With the Crown's credit ruined and taxes already stretched thin, Parliament was, in Griffin's words, "desperate for some other way to obtain the money."

### The Cabal and its seven-point plan

Griffin identifies two groups who saw opportunity in this crisis: the "political scientists" within government, and the "monetary scientists" of the emerging banking trade, organized around a Scottish promoter named **William Paterson**. Griffin is deliberate about the word he uses for their alliance — not "partnership" or "agreement," but **the Cabal**, invoking the dictionary definition of "a conspiratorial group of plotters or intriguers." He notes the meeting that produced the Bank of England took place at Mercer's Chapel in London under a secrecy he compares directly to the 1910 Jekyll Island meeting that would produce the Federal Reserve two centuries later.

Griffin lays out the plan the Cabal hammered out as a seven-point scheme:

1. The government would grant a charter to the monetary scientists to form a bank.
2. The bank would be given a monopoly to issue banknotes, which would circulate as England's paper currency.
3. The bank would create money out of nothing, with only a fraction of its total currency backed by coin.
4. The monetary scientists would then loan the government all the money it needed.
5. The money created for government loans would be backed primarily by government IOUs.
6. Although this money would cost nothing to create, the government would pay "interest" on it at 8%.
7. Government IOUs would also count as "reserves" for creating *additional* loan money for private commerce — meaning the monetary scientists would collect double interest on the same nothing.

The Bank's own subscription circular, quoted by Griffin, stated its business model with startling candor:

> "The Bank hath benefit of interest on all the moneys which it, the Bank, creates out of nothing."

The charter was issued in **1694**. As Griffin puts it, "a strange creature took its initial breath of life. It was the world's first central bank." He notes the irony that King William himself, along with members of Parliament, rushed to become shareholders in the very money-creation scheme they had just chartered.

## The Secret Science: Quantifying the Original Fraud

Griffin walks through the Bank's actual first transaction to demonstrate exactly how the scheme worked in practice, not just in theory. The Bank pledged a capital investment of £1,200,000 to the government at 8% interest — but at the time the loan was made, only £720,000 had actually been paid in by investors. In other words, the Bank "loaned" the government 66% more than it actually possessed. On top of that, the Bank was also permitted to create and lend an *additional*, roughly equal amount to the private sector.

Griffin does the math: an honest loan of the actual £720,000 at 8% would have earned £57,600 in interest. But between the 8% collected on the full £1,200,000 "loaned" to the government and roughly 9% collected on the £720,000 simultaneously loaned to the public, the Bank's true return came to about £160,800 — over 22% on its actual investment. His conclusion is the same one he later applies to the Federal Reserve:

> "When money is created out of nothing, the true interest rate is not 8% or 9% or even 22%. It is infinity."

He is careful to draw out the deeper deception here: because the Bank's newly created notes were physically indistinguishable from notes backed by real coin, the public could not tell the difference — and neither, he argues, could they tell that what was being called a "loan" and "interest" was in fact the manufacture of currency and the collection of an unearned fee. As he puts it elsewhere, comparing this directly to the Federal Reserve: "we must not forget that the Federal Reserve System is such a creature" — a hidden money machine operating "under the guise of purchasing government bonds," activated whenever the political scientists need funds it doesn't have to tax or borrow honestly to get.

## The Legal Precedent: "The Pattern of Protection Was Set"

Griffin identifies a single legal event as the moment that determined the future of all central banking: within two years of the Bank's founding, the flood of newly created money had driven prices up 100%, and a run on the Bank left it unable to produce the gold coin its notes promised. Under normal commercial rules, Griffin argues, this should have meant bankruptcy and liquidation — the market's own corrective mechanism at work.

Instead, in **May 1696**, Parliament passed a law authorizing the Bank to **suspend payment in specie** — legally exempting it from having to honor its own contracts to redeem notes for gold. Griffin calls this "a fateful event in the history of money," because it established the precedent that has governed the banker-government relationship ever since: the Cabal is a partnership in which each side protects the other out of mutual self-interest, and the banks — at least the large, politically connected ones — are simply not allowed to fail. He draws a direct line from this 1696 law to the entire modern practice of central-bank and government bailouts documented elsewhere in the book (see "The Federal Reserve System").

---

## "Booms and Busts Now Guaranteed": A Recurring Pattern of Crisis

Once legally insulated from the consequences of its own fractional lending, Griffin argues, the Bank of England was "doomed to a nauseating roller-coaster ride of inflation, booms, and busts" — and he documents that ride as a recurring, centuries-long pattern rather than a one-time event:

| Year(s) | Event | Griffin's account |
|---|---|---|
| 1694 | Founding | Bank chartered; began creating fractional money almost immediately |
| 1696 | First bank run | Prices had doubled in two years; Bank couldn't redeem notes; Parliament legalized suspension of payment |
| Early 1700s | Speculative manias | Money "pumped" into wild schemes, including a company formed to drain the Red Sea to recover gold supposedly lost by the Egyptians, and roughly £150,000,000 "siphoned into vague and fruitless ventures" in South America and Mexico |
| 1810 | Bullion Committee | House of Commons' Select Committee on the High Price of Gold Bullion concluded that prices weren't rising — the *currency* was falling in value, because it was being created faster than goods were being produced |
| 1815–1821 | Post-Napoleonic depression | Prices doubled, then crashed; riots occurred through 1816; gold convertibility restored in 1821 under political pressure |
| 1825 | Crash | Collapse of Sir Peter Cole and Company triggered the failure of 63 other banks |
| 1839 | Renewed crisis | More bank failures prompted five years of parliamentary debate |
| 1844 | Peel's Bank Act | Sir Robert Peel's reform attempted to cap money creation near a gold-backed level — but Griffin notes it failed on three counts: it still allowed £14,000,000 in fractional lending, it didn't apply to checkbook money at all, and it left unchallenged the core premise that politicians could manage the money supply better than a free market in gold |
| 1847 | Crisis returns | The Bank, again near insolvency, was simply exempted from Peel's own reserve requirements |
| 1857 | Crisis | Caused by "excessive and unwise lending" tied to overoptimistic foreign trade speculation |
| 1866 | Overend, Gurney crash | Speculative credit expansion ended in the failure of the famous banking house of Overend, Gurney and Co.; the 1844 Act was suspended again |
| 1890 | Baring crisis | Triggered by the failure of Baring Brothers amid speculation in American and Argentine securities |

Griffin's summary judgment on this record: in spite of "the general and recurring failures of the Bank of England during these years," the model proved so attractive to political and monetary scientists elsewhere that it was copied throughout Europe and eventually in America regardless of its actual performance. As he puts it: "Who cares if the scheme is destructive? Here is the perfect tool for obtaining unlimited funding for politicians and endless profits for bankers."

## The Model Spreads: From London to the World

Griffin presents the Bank of England as patient zero for an institutional contagion:

- **The Bank of Prussia** became the Reichsbank.
- **Napoleon** established the Banque de France.
- **The First Bank of the United States (1791)** was, in Griffin's words, "modelled closely after the Bank of England" — down to sharing a president (Thomas Willing) with its American predecessor, the Bank of North America.
- **The Federal Reserve System (1913)**, "a few decades later," became what Griffin calls "the venerated model" of the same basic structure — a legally protected, fractional-reserve, government-partnered money creator.

He treats this lineage as more than coincidence — it is, in his framework, the same seven-point Cabal formula, updated for each new country and century, but structurally unchanged.

---

## The Bank of England and Colonial / Early America

Griffin documents that the Bank of England's influence on America predates the Revolution. He describes the Bank using its influence with the Crown to **forbid the American colonies from minting their own coins or establishing local banks**, a restriction intended to force the colonists to rely on Bank of England notes for any paper currency. When the colonies proved "inventive" enough to issue their own fiat paper money instead, Britain moved in 1751 to pressure the colonies into redeeming and withdrawing their currency — buying it back, ironically, at steep discounts because colonial fiat money had already depreciated so badly on its own.

Griffin frames this episode as "a blessing in disguise": once the colonies were forced back onto a gold-and-silver commodity-money system, prosperity actually returned, despite dire predictions that a shortage of paper money would ruin the economy. This is one of the few places in the book where Griffin credits an action of the Bank of England with an accidentally *positive* outcome for the public — though the Bank's motive, in his account, was market control, not colonial welfare.

## The Bank of England and the Financing of War

War finance is one of the threads Griffin returns to most insistently, and he treats the Bank of England's founding as the literal starting point of the modern era of war-by-debt.

He documents the Bank's role in financing England's wars against Napoleon: to defeat Napoleon, "the Bank of England created vast new amounts of fiat money to 'lend' to the government," with a steady stream of gold flowing out of the country to help finance the allied armies of Russia, Prussia, and Austria. This episode appears in the same chapter (**"The Rothschild Formula"**) where Griffin lays out his broader theory that fiat-money creation is what makes prolonged, large-scale war financially possible in the first place — see the related note on that chapter for the full five-point doctrine.

His most direct statement connecting the Bank of England to the entire subsequent history of warfare comes in his **Fifth Reason to Abolish the Federal Reserve**:

> "An overview of all wars since the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 suggests that most of them would have been greatly reduced in severity, or perhaps not even fought at all, without fiat money."

For Griffin, this is not rhetorical flourish — it is the thesis statement connecting the Bank of England directly to World War I, the Bolshevik Revolution, World War II, Korea, and Vietnam, all of which he argues were financed through the same basic mechanism the Bank of England pioneered in 1694.

## The Twentieth-Century Relationship: The Bank of England as "Ailing Parent" of the Fed

Griffin's most explicit statement of the Bank of England–Federal Reserve relationship comes in his account of the 1920s, where he describes an unusually close, largely secret personal alliance between **Benjamin Strong**, the first dominant Governor of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York (and one of the original seven at Jekyll Island), and **Montagu Norman**, Governor of the Bank of England from 1920 until Strong's death in 1928.

### A close and secretive partnership

Griffin describes Strong and Norman vacationing together, crossing the Atlantic repeatedly to consult, and exchanging weekly private cables — all conducted with a secrecy he quotes one biographer as calling "more secret than any ever held by Royal Arch Masons or by any Rosicrucian Order." By 1926, the heads of the German and French central banks were occasionally drawn into these meetings as well.

### The secret meeting of 1927

The culmination came in July 1927, when Norman and Hjalmar Schacht (head of the German Reichsbank) arrived in New York aboard the *Mauretania* under conditions of extreme secrecy — neither name appeared on the passenger list, and neither met the press. They met with Strong and Charles Rist, Deputy Governor of the Banque de France, to address what Griffin describes as the persistently weak reserve position of the Bank of England. The plan agreed upon: the Federal Reserve would ease American interest rates and expand credit, drawing gold away from the U.S. and toward London, while making British goods more competitive by inflating American prices. Schacht and Rist declined to commit their own countries to the scheme and left early; Strong and Norman worked out the details alone.

Griffin quotes Strong's own May 1924 letter to Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon, in which Strong acknowledged the goal directly — to spare Britain the political pain of cutting wages and welfare spending, conceding: "The burden of this readjustment must fall more largely upon us than upon them."

### The Fed as the Bank of England's offspring

Griffin narrates the resulting 1924–1929 monetary expansion as a series of "up" and "down" moves by the Fed, largely timed to rescue the pound sterling whenever it weakened on international markets. His description of the pivotal 1928 expansion states the family relationship outright:

> "The fledgling Creature came to the aid of the Bank of England, its ailing parent."

This is the clearest single sentence in the book establishing Griffin's view of the two institutions' relationship — not merely two similar organizations, but a lineage, with the Federal Reserve created in the Bank of England's image and, in its early decades, still operating substantially in service of it.

### The payoff: 1929

Griffin connects this entire chain of events directly to the Crash of 1929, presenting evidence he calls circumstantial that the Federal Reserve and the Bank of England agreed at a secret February 1929 meeting that a market collapse was inevitable, after which insider warnings quietly went to preferred customers while the public was reassured. On August 9, 1929, the Fed raised the bank-loan rate and began selling securities; the market collapsed on October 29. (See "The Federal Reserve System" for the full account of this episode.)

## How the Bank of England Connects to Griffin's Other Core Concepts

| Concept | The Bank of England's role, per Griffin |
|---|---|
| **Fractional-Reserve Banking** | The Bank of England didn't invent fractional lending (that began with goldsmiths), but it was the first institution to **institutionalize** it at a national level, backed by government charter and law. |
| **Fiat Money** | The Bank demonstrated, for the first time at national scale, that a government-chartered bank could create currency "out of nothing" and have it accepted as real money — the template for every fiat currency scheme Griffin documents afterward. |
| **The Mandrake Mechanism** | Griffin explicitly identifies the Federal Reserve's money-creation process as functionally identical to the Bank of England's original 1694 transaction: buying government debt with money that didn't exist until the purchase was made. |
| **Usury** | The Bank's founding circular — "benefit of interest on all the moneys which it creates out of nothing" — is, in essence, the founding document of what Griffin later redefines as usury: interest charged on a "pretended loan." |
| **Inflation** | The 100% price rise within two years of the Bank's founding is Griffin's first documented case study of fiat-money-driven inflation functioning as a hidden tax. |
| **The Federal Reserve System** | Described as the direct institutional descendant of the Bank of England, both structurally (a legally protected, privately-connected cartel operating in partnership with government) and personally, through the Strong–Norman alliance. |
| **Government Debt** | The Bank of England pioneered the practice of a nation financing itself through permanently rolled-over debt to a privately-connected banking monopoly rather than direct taxation — the same "perpetual debt" pattern Griffin later documents in America. |
| **War Finance** | The Bank of England's founding purpose was to fund a war (against France) that the Crown could not otherwise afford; Griffin treats 1694 as the moment war financing was decoupled from a government's actual ability to tax or save. |
| **The Rothschild Formula** | The Bank of England's financing of the wars against Napoleon is one of Griffin's central illustrations of the Formula in action — using fiat money to fund not just one's own war effort but the armies of allied nations as well. |

---

## Griffin's Conclusion

For Griffin, the Bank of England matters less as a piece of English history than as **proof of concept** — the demonstration that a government and a group of private financiers could form a legally protected partnership, create money without producing anything, extract permanent interest on that created money, survive its own repeated failures through legal exemption rather than market discipline, and export the entire model successfully to the rest of the world. Every element of his critique of the Federal Reserve — its cartel structure, its immunity from failure, its role in inflation, its financing of war, its partnership with politicians who get to spend without taxing — he traces back to a charter signed in 1694. The Federal Reserve, in his account, is not an American innovation; it is the Bank of England's oldest and most successful franchise.
