# America's Infrastructure Is Dying of Old Age — All at Once

There's a strange kind of tragedy hiding underneath American suburbs, and almost nobody's job is to talk about it. It isn't a scandal, a war, or a crisis with a press conference attached to it. It's a pipe. Actually, it's millions of them — along with sewer mains, pump stations, and thousands of dams — all built in the same few decades, all aging at the same rate, and all now approaching the end of their working lives at the same time.

That's the warning from John Dudlichek, a civil engineer from northwest Indiana who has spent more than 30 years installing and repairing water and sewer systems in the towns ringing Chicago. He runs 14 or 15 crews for a construction company that does public infrastructure work, and he says what he's seeing in the ground doesn't match what politicians are talking about.

## A Nation Built in a Hurry

The story starts, oddly enough, with America's own success. After World War II, the country experienced a construction boom unlike anything the world had seen before — new towns, new suburbs, and, buried beneath them, entirely new water and sewer systems built to serve the baby boom generation. It happened fast: open trenches, empty ground, no competing utility lines to work around, and effectively no debt ceiling standing in the way.

The problem is that infrastructure, like people, has a lifespan. And according to Dudlichek, nobody in his field knows of water mains that reliably last much past 80 or 90 years. Some of the older cast iron and wooden pipes have held up remarkably well, but the ductile iron installed in the 1960s, '70s, and '80s — the material that made up the bulk of the postwar boom — is failing now, all at once, because it was all installed within the same narrow window of time.

He describes it almost like watching a single generation age together. The suburb of Valparaiso, Indiana, had more than 100 water main breaks last year alone. His own town sees a break every two or three days. Parts of Gary, Indiana are past the point of repair. In Dixmoor, Illinois, a project he worked on for the Army Corps of Engineers found buildings physically sagging because the water infrastructure beneath them had failed.

And it isn't just pipes. The Army Corps of Engineers has identified roughly 2,500 of the country's 16,000 dams as being in serious need of repair — deficient enough that the agency considers them unacceptable in their current condition. Dudlichek has personally run across the top of one, the dam holding back Rough River Lake in Kentucky, and watched water seeping through it. That dam is now the subject of a $900 million reconstruction project, one of thousands of similar problems scattered across the country.

## The Sewage Sandwich Problem

Making matters worse, fixing any of this isn't as simple as digging a new trench the way crews did in 1955. American water and sewer systems were built in a specific order — sewage lines at the bottom, drinking water above that — a layout that, however inconvenient to repair, at least prevents sewage from contaminating drinking water during a leak. But in the decades since, fiber optic cable, gas lines, and telecom infrastructure have all been layered in above that original system. Replacing a single water main today often means excavating around, or moving, several other utility systems that didn't exist when the pipe went into the ground.

That complexity means projects can't just target water mains in isolation. If a town replaces its water main but leaves an aging sewer main sitting right below it untouched, it's setting itself up for the same expensive disruption again in another 20 years — just for a different pipe.

## The People Who Know Aren't in the Room

The most pointed part of Dudlichek's argument isn't really about engineering — it's about who gets to make decisions. He argues that funding priorities are set by whatever's administratively easiest to fund, not by which system is closest to failure. And the people who could actually answer that question — contractors and engineers doing the physical repair work — aren't part of the conversation. Decisions flow instead through state departments of transportation that are themselves boxed in by rigid federal rules, wetland regulations, and funding strings attached at the national level.

He's careful not to trash the state officials he works with directly — he says Indiana's transportation department is doing about as well as it can with the money and rules it's been handed. His frustration is aimed higher up, at a funding structure where money is filtered through federal agencies before it ever reaches a local water main. His prescription, consistent with his libertarian sympathies, is to push funding and decision-making back down to the state and local level, where it's closer to the people actually doing the work.

He's similarly blunt about mandates layered onto public works projects that, in his view, function as a tax without much benefit — a required share of contracts set aside for a "disadvantaged business enterprise" designation, or erosion-control measures he says exploded into a billion-dollar-a-year expense in Indiana alone after a lobbying push by manufacturers of silt fencing and similar products, not because of any actual erosion problem on the famously flat terrain where he works.

## Guns Before Butter — Or Pipes

Running underneath all of it is a broader argument familiar to listeners of antiwar and libertarian commentary: that a federal government spending itself into roughly $40 trillion of debt, and continuing to fund wars and foreign commitments, is the same government pleading poverty when it comes to fixing the pipes under American towns. Dudlichek connects the dots between currency debasement, an unaffordable national debt, and a state transportation department that "can't afford what needs to be done" — not because the country lacks the wealth or productive capacity, but because so much of it gets diverted elsewhere by decisions made far away from the people who'd actually pour the concrete.

His proposed fix isn't a grand federal program. It's decentralization — funding decisions made closer to the ground, and a louder voice for the tradespeople who already know which towns are in trouble and which aren't. Short of that, his bet is that the country doesn't confront the problem until something breaks badly enough to force the issue. As he put it, that's not an outcome he wants, but he isn't sure what else would get anyone's attention first.
