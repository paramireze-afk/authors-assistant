# Transnational Capital

> Capital ordinarily belongs somewhere: a household, a bank, a factory, a city, or a nation. Transnational capital begins when capital no longer needs a home.

In Jiang Xueqin's framework, **transnational capital** is capital that can move across borders without maintaining any durable loyalty to the society that protects it.

It uses nation-states, but does not belong to them.

It may depend on a country's laws, military, infrastructure, currency, and population, yet remain prepared to leave once another location offers greater security, influence, or opportunity. Jiang therefore describes the relationship between transnational capital and the nation-state through the metaphor of a **parasite and its host**.

The host supplies:

* labor,
* military protection,
* legal enforcement,
* financial guarantees,
* political legitimacy,
* natural resources,
* and a population capable of carrying the costs.

Capital supplies investment, credit, institutional access, and temporary prosperity. But because it can leave, it does not necessarily share the long-term fate of the society in which it operates.

## A Capitalism That Can Escape

Jiang distinguishes ordinary capitalism from transnational capital.

Capitalism, in some form, has existed throughout recorded history. Merchants traded between regions. Lenders financed rulers. Landowners accumulated property. Markets formed around grain, metals, shipping, and debt.

But historically, wealth was usually tied to a place.

A Roman aristocrat could accumulate land, slaves, gold, and political influence, but if Rome collapsed, the aristocrat could not instantly transfer the entire system of ownership somewhere else. Wealth depended on the continued existence of the surrounding political and social order.

Transnational capital changes that relationship.

Modern finance allows wealth to move rapidly between:

* currencies,
* corporations,
* jurisdictions,
* investment funds,
* banking systems,
* and political regimes.

Capital can therefore survive the failure of the society that created it. It can benefit from one country during its expansion, leave during its decline, and reappear inside another rising power.

This mobility is the defining feature of Jiang's concept.

## The Host and the Parasite

Jiang repeatedly describes transnational capital as parasitic.

The metaphor is not simply that wealthy actors exploit poor people. His argument is structural: capital attaches itself to a powerful nation, encourages that nation to expand, and draws energy from the resulting empire.

The host nation provides:

1. **Military force** to open markets and protect trade.
2. **Public credit** to guarantee private financial interests.
3. **Taxpayers** to absorb debt and losses.
4. **Workers** to produce goods and services.
5. **Institutions** to enforce contracts and ownership.
6. **Political narratives** that portray expansion as moral or necessary.

The relationship can initially benefit both sides. Investment increases. Industry expands. The currency gains influence. The state becomes more powerful.

Over time, however, imperial expansion exhausts the host.

The population bears:

* war,
* debt,
* taxation,
* inequality,
* institutional decay,
* political polarization,
* and the declining legitimacy of the state.

Once the host becomes unstable, transnational capital begins searching for another center of power.

The parasite does not necessarily intend to kill the host. It may simply lack any reason to preserve it once extraction elsewhere becomes more profitable.

## Jiang's Historical Genealogy

Jiang traces the emergence of transnational capital through a particular historical sequence:

```text
Protestant Reformation
        ↓
Calvinism and disciplined accumulation
        ↓
Dutch commercial wealth
        ↓
The Glorious Revolution
        ↓
The Bank of England
        ↓
The British Empire
        ↓
The Federal Reserve and American Empire
        ↓
A possible future imperial center
```

This is not presented merely as a history of banking. Jiang treats it as the development of a new relationship between wealth and political authority.

## Calvinism and Accumulation

Jiang begins with Calvinist theology, particularly the doctrine of predestination.

In his telling, believers could no longer rely on the Catholic Church to determine salvation. God had already decided who would be saved. Material success could therefore be interpreted as possible evidence of divine favor.

But wealth could not simply be consumed through luxury. It had to be disciplined, preserved, and reinvested.

Jiang contrasts this with earlier societies in which wealth was frequently converted into social status through generosity, feasts, gifts, patronage, and obligations to family or community.

Under the Calvinist model as Jiang describes it, accumulation becomes its own moral activity.

Money is not accumulated merely to purchase things. Accumulation itself becomes evidence of discipline, virtue, and possible divine favor.

This creates a culture especially suited to reinvestment and financial expansion.

## The Dutch Republic and England

Jiang argues that this culture contributed to the commercial rise of the Dutch Republic.

The Dutch accumulated enormous financial wealth but remained militarily vulnerable. Jiang presents the Glorious Revolution of 1688 as an arrangement through which Dutch capital found a more secure political host in England.

The establishment of the **Bank of England in 1694** becomes a decisive event in his history.

Previously, lending to a king or prince was personally risky. The ruler might:

* die,
* lose a war,
* repudiate the debt,
* confiscate the lender's property,
* or simply refuse repayment.

The Bank of England connected private capital to the permanent credit of the state.

Debt was no longer guaranteed only by an individual monarch. It was backed by Parliament, taxation, and the continuing existence of the English political system.

The state became the guarantor of capital.

Capital, in return, gained influence over the priorities of the state.

## From Public Credit to Empire

In Jiang's account, the British Empire grew from this alliance between state power and mobile finance.

Foreign and domestic capital flowed toward England because English institutions appeared capable of protecting it. The British state then used this accumulated financial power to fund:

* fleets,
* colonial expansion,
* commercial monopolies,
* wars,
* resource extraction,
* and control of foreign markets.

Empire was not merely territorial conquest. It was a mechanism for expanding the territory in which capital could operate.

The state conquered.

Capital entered.

Institutions converted conquest into durable economic relationships.

## From Britain to the United States

Jiang interprets the later rise of the United States as another migration of capital.

Wealth accumulated through European empire was increasingly invested in American:

* land,
* railroads,
* industry,
* infrastructure,
* banking,
* and government debt.

The United States eventually became a more energetic and productive host than Britain.

In Jiang's telling, the Federal Reserve represents an American continuation of the same state-finance structure embodied by the Bank of England. The United States supplied the military reach, industrial base, political stability, and currency needed for a new global financial order.

The result was **Pax Americana**: an international system in which American power protected the circulation and expansion of capital.

## Money Is Not the Final Objective

One of the more important aspects of Jiang's argument is that extreme capital accumulation cannot be explained by consumption.

A person can only own so many:

* houses,
* cars,
* yachts,
* aircraft,
* estates,
* and luxury goods.

Beyond a certain level, additional wealth adds little to personal comfort.

Jiang therefore argues that the true objective is not money but **power**.

Capital is valuable because it can organize human activity.

It can determine:

* which industries expand,
* which technologies receive investment,
* which wars are financed,
* which governments remain solvent,
* which populations gain employment,
* which resources are extracted,
* and which futures appear economically possible.

Capital becomes power when other people must organize their lives around gaining access to it.

## Wealth Destruction and Dependence

This leads to one of Jiang's darker claims: capital may benefit from the destruction of existing wealth.

A population that possesses:

* land,
* strong families,
* local production,
* community support,
* practical skills,
* and low dependence on formal institutions

is difficult to control through finance alone.

A population that has lost those things becomes dependent on:

* employment,
* loans,
* reconstruction funds,
* government assistance,
* outside investment,
* and institutional approval.

In Jiang's framework, war and economic crisis can therefore create new opportunities for capital.

Destruction removes old systems of ownership and social organization. Reconstruction introduces new credit, contracts, infrastructure, and dependencies.

The ruins become an investment opportunity.

## Crisis as Opportunity

Transnational capital does not necessarily need to create every crisis.

It only needs to be able to move faster than everyone else once a crisis begins.

This is where Jiang's concept overlaps with his recurring principle of the **path of least resistance**.

Large institutions may appear to follow a master plan when they are actually reacting to incentives:

* investors move toward the highest returns,
* governments choose indirect coercion over costly wars,
* corporations enter markets opened by instability,
* financial institutions purchase distressed assets,
* and elites position themselves to benefit from outcomes they did not fully create.

This produces coordination without requiring perfect central control.

The system can behave as though it has a single intention even when its participants are competing.

## Coordination and Competition

Jiang does not consistently describe transnational capital as a single unified organization.

At times, he emphasizes its enormous diversity:

* pension funds,
* sovereign wealth funds,
* hedge funds,
* family offices,
* banks,
* asset managers,
* corporations,
* and private investors.

These actors do not all share identical goals.

They compete.

But Jiang argues that those capable of coordinating across institutions and borders gain an advantage over those who act alone.

This creates a selection effect:

> Capital that coordinates successfully becomes more powerful than capital that does not.

In the July 2026 discussion, Jiang goes further and attributes some of this coordination to secret societies, Zionist political networks, religious eschatology, and Jewish diaspora connections.

He connects these claims to his prediction that global power may shift toward Israel under what he calls **Pax Judaica**.

These claims form one of the most controversial extensions of his framework. The transcript records Jiang asserting them, but does not independently establish the hidden coordination he describes.

For purposes of understanding his theory, the important structural point is that he believes **cohesive networks outperform isolated wealth**.

## Capital and Religion

Religion occupies an unusual position in Jiang's model.

On one hand, religion competes with capital.

A genuinely religious person may value:

* faith,
* family,
* sacrifice,
* community,
* honor,
* tradition,
* and spiritual purpose

above wealth.

Such values limit the ability of capital to organize all human behavior through financial incentives.

On the other hand, Jiang argues that religion can also make capital more powerful.

A group bound together only by profit may fracture when incentives change. A group bound by shared belief can coordinate across generations, institutions, and national boundaries.

Religion supplies:

* identity,
* loyalty,
* discipline,
* historical memory,
* sacrifice,
* and a shared vision of the future.

This produces one of the central contradictions in Jiang's account:

> Religion is simultaneously one of capital's strongest rivals and one of its most powerful organizing tools.

## Empire as a Temporary Home

Jiang's concept suggests that empires are not permanent political achievements. They are temporary homes for concentrated capital.

A successful host must provide:

* military reach,
* political stability,
* reliable courts,
* deep financial markets,
* an influential currency,
* access to resources,
* and a productive population.

When the host can no longer provide these conditions, capital begins to leave.

Jiang interprets the historical sequence as:

```text
Dutch commercial system
        ↓
British imperial system
        ↓
American imperial system
        ↓
Possible Israeli or alternative successor
```

He sometimes suggests Israel may become the next host.

Elsewhere in the same discussion, however, he predicts that transnational capital itself may be approaching collapse and that Israel is too small, unstable, or internally divided to inherit the American imperial role successfully.

The framework therefore contains two competing predictions:

1. Transnational capital will migrate to another host.
2. The entire system of mobile capital may be reaching its historical limit.

This tension remains unresolved.

## Maritime Choke Points and Indirect Control

Jiang applies the concept to maritime geography.

An empire does not need to occupy every country if it controls the routes connecting them.

Strategic chokepoints such as:

* the Strait of Gibraltar,
* the Strait of Malacca,
* Arctic shipping routes,
* major canals,
* ports,
* and energy corridors

allow a relatively small number of institutions and military assets to influence enormous volumes of trade.

Control of circulation may be more important than control of territory.

This reflects the logic of transnational capital itself.

Capital does not need to own the entire world. It needs to control the channels through which:

* goods,
* currency,
* energy,
* credit,
* and information

must pass.

## Migration as Leverage

Jiang also connects transnational capital to migration.

Within his framework, migrants are not only individuals seeking work or safety. Large movements of people can become instruments within geopolitical competition.

Migration can place pressure on:

* local services,
* national borders,
* electoral coalitions,
* labor markets,
* and relations between states.

A state capable of opening or closing migration routes gains leverage over neighboring societies.

Jiang's Ceuta analysis treats migration as a form of indirect coercion: cheaper and politically safer than conventional military action, but potentially capable of producing substantial political change.

Whether or not his specific causal claims are accepted, this application illustrates the broader logic of the concept:

> Power increasingly operates by controlling flows rather than directly ruling territory.

## Internal Tensions

Jiang's theory remains unresolved in several important areas.

### Plan or emergent system?

At times, transnational capital appears to be centrally coordinated.

Elsewhere, Jiang argues elites are divided, shortsighted, emotional, and often incompetent. Events emerge through competition and the path of least resistance rather than from a single master plan.

### Religion as servant or enemy?

Religion is described both as a powerful mechanism of elite coordination and as the force most capable of defeating capital.

### Israel as successor or failed host?

Jiang predicts a movement of capital toward Israel while simultaneously arguing that Israel lacks the population, stability, and internal cohesion required to become a global empire.

### Capital as immortal or collapsing?

If capital can always move, the system appears almost impossible to defeat.

Yet Jiang also compares the current period to the Bronze Age collapse and predicts a breakdown of transnational capital itself.

These contradictions do not necessarily make the concept useless. They show that it remains a developing framework rather than a completed theory.

## Analytical Value

The value of transnational capital as a concept is not that it explains every event.

Its value is that it asks a set of questions often obscured by conventional nation-centered analysis:

* Who can move, and who is trapped?
* Who receives the benefits of imperial expansion?
* Who absorbs the losses?
* What happens when capital has fewer obligations to a country than the country's citizens do?
* Can democratic governments control institutions capable of leaving their jurisdiction?
* Does mobile capital weaken the relationship between wealth and community?
* When does investment become political power?
* Can a nation remain sovereign when its economy depends on capital that owes it no loyalty?
* What happens when the interests of a financial system diverge from the survival of its host society?

These questions are broader than any single claim Jiang makes.

They are the enduring core of the concept.

## Working Definition

**Transnational capital** is capital sufficiently mobile, concentrated, and institutionally protected that it can operate across national boundaries, influence state policy, benefit from imperial power, and relocate when a host society becomes exhausted or unprofitable.

In Jiang Xueqin's framework, it is not merely an economic resource.

It is a political organism without a permanent country.
