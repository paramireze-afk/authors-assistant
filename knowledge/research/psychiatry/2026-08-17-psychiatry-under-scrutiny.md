# The Case Against the Pill: Inside a Psychiatrist's Break With Mainstream Psychiatry

Roughly one in five Americans may now be taking an antidepressant. That's Dr. Josef Witt-Doerring's estimate: he puts confirmed data at about 14 percent of the population as of 2014, and, extrapolating from trends he's tracked since — including a jump he attributes to the pandemic — puts the current figure at somewhere between 15 and 20 percent. By his recollection, that's roughly a five-fold increase since the early 1990s.

If those drugs were working, you'd expect a country that's calmer, happier, and more stable than it was thirty years ago. Instead, Witt-Doerring says, suicide rates are higher, disability linked to mental illness has grown, and teen suicide has climbed too.

That contradiction — as he frames it, more pills alongside worse outcomes — is the starting point for a wide-ranging conversation between Tucker Carlson and Dr. Josef Witt-Doerring, a psychiatrist with an unusual résumé: residency training, a fellowship inside the pharmaceutical industry at Janssen (Johnson & Johnson's drug arm), and a stint as a medical officer at the FDA overseeing the safety of psychiatric drugs already on the market. He now runs a practice built almost entirely around helping people get *off* the medications he was trained to prescribe.

What follows is his argument, in his own terms — how a class of drugs sold as a fix for a "chemical imbalance" became one of the most widely used and, in his telling, most poorly scrutinized treatments in modern medicine.

## The Chemical Imbalance Story That Wasn't

Every account of SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors — drugs like Prozac, Zoloft, and Lexapro) tends to begin the same way: depression, the story goes, is caused by low serotonin, and these drugs correct that imbalance the way insulin corrects a diabetic's blood sugar.

Witt-Doerring says that story was never actually demonstrated. Researchers have looked — autopsy studies of receptor levels, spinal fluid tests for serotonin metabolites, comparisons of depressed and non-depressed brains — and consistently failed to find any measurable biological difference between people who are depressed and people who aren't. That's precisely why, he notes, psychiatry doesn't use blood tests or brain scans to diagnose any condition; there's no physical marker to look for. Diagnosis instead comes from a checklist in the *DSM* — if a patient reports five out of nine symptoms, they qualify for a diagnosis of major depressive disorder.

So where did the "chemical imbalance" idea come from? According to Witt-Doerring, it was really an inference built backward from a drug effect. When people take serotonin-altering drugs, some become calmer or appear less depressed. Rather than treating that as a straightforward drug effect — the person is *sedated*, in other words — the field reasoned that the improvement must mean the person previously had a serotonin deficiency being corrected. He characterizes this as a convenient story that made both doctors and patients comfortable with prescribing and taking the drugs, rather than a proven mechanism.

The distinction matters, in his account, because of what it changed. Before SSRIs, psychiatry — dominated by Freudian talk therapy — treated distress as something rooted in a person's relationships, history, and circumstances, however imperfectly. The chemical-imbalance framing recast depression and anxiety as standalone medical illnesses with an organic cause, independent of a person's actual life. Once that reframing took hold, he argues, questioning it wasn't just scientifically debatable — it became something close to taboo, cast as "stigmatizing the mentally ill."

## Numbing, Not Fixing

If SSRIs don't correct a chemical deficiency, what are they actually doing? Witt-Doerring's answer: emotional blunting. The drugs suppress the emotional range broadly — dulling anxiety and sadness, but also dulling positive feeling, connection, and libido along with it.

For someone in acute distress, that numbing can genuinely feel like relief, even life-saving relief, in the moment. He doesn't dispute that people experience real short-term benefit. His concern is what that relief costs: it can mask the underlying reason a person is anxious or unhappy — a bad relationship, an unstable job, an unaddressed health problem, financial stress — instead of addressing it. He describes it as similar to disabling a smoke detector rather than dealing with the fire it's warning about. The underlying problem doesn't go away; it festers, often worsening while the medication makes it harder to notice.

He also points to a pattern he says is common in long-term patients: the initial "honeymoon" period of relief wears off over months as the body adapts to the drug, prompting a higher dose, then eventually another medication altogether. Patients can end up on five different psychiatric drugs, each one added because the last one stopped working — without anyone stepping back to ask whether the original approach was sound.

## PSSD: The Side Effect Few People Have Heard Of

The part of the conversation Witt-Doerring calls the biggest untold story in psychiatry today is PSSD — post-SSRI sexual dysfunction.

Sexual side effects while on these medications are well known and common; Witt-Doerring puts the figure at around 70 percent of users experiencing some degree of it — reduced libido, difficulty with arousal, or difficulty reaching climax — though he doesn't cite a specific study for that number in the conversation. Patients are typically told this is temporary and will resolve once they stop taking the drug.

What's alarming, in his account, is a smaller subset of patients for whom the dysfunction doesn't resolve after stopping the drug — it persists, in some cases for years, and he references case reports describing symptoms lasting decades, though he says most people who do recover do so within about three years. He describes these patients developing what he calls genital anesthesia — a loss of physical sensation so complete that patients describe touch in that area as feeling like touching the back of a hand. Alongside the physical symptoms, he says the same patients often report cognitive effects (trouble concentrating, brain fog) and profound emotional blunting that persists after stopping the drug — an inability to feel warmth toward a partner or child, or nostalgia from a familiar song. He characterizes this combination as devastating enough that he believes suicide is common in this specific population — a clinical impression from the cases he's encountered, not a cited statistic — because the loss of emotional and physical connection removes what makes life feel worth living.

He's careful to note this isn't a fringe claim he's making up: PSSD is a listed, official warning on antidepressant labels in the European Union, and has been recognized by regulators in Canada, Australia, and Hong Kong. The FDA, he says, is currently reviewing it in the US. His frustration is that despite this level of regulatory acknowledgment abroad, most American doctors have never heard of it and don't warn patients before prescribing.

Drawing on his own direct experience, he recounts speaking with roughly 20 to 30 people who developed PSSD, including cases — two of which he describes in detail — where patients who told their doctors what was happening were dismissed as delusional or anxious and were involuntarily hospitalized — their families told the patient's account of drug-induced symptoms was itself a sign of mental illness. He also raises a subtler pattern: some men who developed profound blunting reported questioning their sexual orientation because their attraction to partners had simply stopped registering — which he frames as evidence of how deep and disorienting the blunting can be, not as a claim about causation of sexual orientation generally.

## Withdrawal, Dependency, and "Protracted" Injury

A separate strand of the conversation concerns what happens when people try to stop taking these drugs — both SSRIs and benzodiazepines (Xanax, Klonopin, Valium), a related but distinct class used for anxiety and insomnia.

Benzodiazepines, Witt-Doerring says, provide almost immediate relief, which is part of what makes them so easy to lean on and so hard to quit. Stopping them can trigger severe rebound anxiety and insomnia, and in some cases withdrawal can be dangerous enough to be fatal. For a subset of long-term users, tapering too quickly can trigger something he calls **protracted withdrawal** — which he characterizes as a persistent, potentially long-lasting neurological injury rather than a temporary discomfort, though he doesn't present a formal diagnostic mechanism for it beyond his clinical observation. He describes symptoms including tinnitus, light sensitivity, cognitive impairment, burning sensations in the hands and feet, and akathisia — a state of physical restlessness so severe that patients pace in circles, unable to sit still. The 70 percent figure he cites is specific to his own caseload — he estimates that about 70 percent of the patients currently in his practice are dealing with this kind of injury from tapering off benzodiazepines or SSRIs too fast — not a claim about the broader population of people who've taken these drugs.

The prognosis, in his clinical experience, is usually recovery — but on a timescale of up to two years, during which a person can be effectively disabled. He says this condition typically develops after people have been on a drug for years and then try to stop abruptly, though he's also personally seen it happen after just a few months in less common cases.

He's also critical of the clinical evidence used to justify keeping patients on these drugs indefinitely. The studies that support long-term use, he explains, work like this: researchers put a group of patients on a drug for around six months, then split them — half stay on it, half are abruptly taken off. Whoever becomes "depressed again" is counted as evidence the drug was working and is needed long-term. His objection is that this design doesn't distinguish a genuine relapse from withdrawal — and rapidly stopping a drug like this predictably *causes* withdrawal symptoms that look like depression. He calls the resulting conclusion — that this proves the drug is preventing relapse — fundamentally flawed, and describes it as the shaky evidentiary basis for a practice, indefinite prescribing, that has never actually been studied in controlled trials beyond about 12 months, despite millions of people staying on these drugs for five years or more.

## Why the System Works This Way: Incentives at Every Level

A recurring theme is that the problems he describes aren't the result of individually careless doctors, but of a system where the incentives line up, at almost every level, against slowing down or asking hard questions.

- **The FDA's funding structure.** Witt-Doerring recalls, without citing an exact source, that the FDA receives roughly 70 percent of its funding for drug divisions through user fees paid by the pharmaceutical industry (via a law called PDUFA), which comes with built-in deadlines — a new drug application reviewed within nine months, a protocol reviewed within 30 days. He says this isn't inherently corrupt, but it does concentrate reviewer time and attention on approving new drugs rather than following up on safety signals like PSSD or withdrawal injury, which get comparatively little institutional bandwidth.
- **Academic capture.** He describes many of the academics who train doctors and staff federal agencies as having financial ties to pharmaceutical companies, and describes a professional culture in psychiatry that treats criticizing medications as a kind of moral failing — reluctance he attributes less to overt corruption than to genuine belief, absorbed during training, that defending the drugs is part of defending patients from stigma.
- **Guild interest.** Psychiatrists, unlike other mental health professionals such as therapists or counselors, can prescribe medication — which he suggests gives the profession an institutional incentive to keep prescribing central to its identity and authority.
- **The economics of a doctor's visit.** He points out that the insurance-driven system pays doctors per visit, so a psychiatrist who spends 45 minutes to an hour actually understanding a patient's life earns roughly half what a colleague earns seeing four patients in that same window. Structuring care around checklist diagnosis and a prescription is, financially, the path of least resistance.
- **Telehealth.** He describes personally testing an online prescription service (similar to well-known telehealth startups), where he was diagnosed with depression through what may have been an automated chat and received a prescription in the mail three days later — without ever speaking to a live clinician, and with side-effect information reduced to a hyperlink on the checkout page. His view is that this model strips away what little oversight existed in a traditional visit while making these drugs easier to obtain.

## ADHD Medications and the Question of Root Causes

The conversation extends the same logic to stimulant medications for ADHD. Citing a New York magazine piece summarizing existing research — rather than data he generated himself — Witt-Doerring says long-term studies don't show these drugs improving academic outcomes; what they reliably do is make children's behavior easier to manage — useful for a child struggling to sit through material they find boring, but not necessarily a treatment for an underlying problem.

He then raises two explanations of his own for what he thinks gets too little attention, and he's explicit that both are more interpretation than proven finding. First, a cultural one: in a society that ties a child's worth heavily to academic performance, some children who are simply mismatched with their environment (bored, or pushed into coursework that doesn't interest them) get diagnosed and medicated rather than redirected. Second, a physiological hypothesis: he suggests poor diet and insulin resistance — driven by refined carbohydrates and added sugar — impair the brain's ability to use energy, producing brain fog and attention problems that resemble ADHD but are, in his view, a metabolic issue with a metabolic fix. As support, he points to his own clinical observation that ketogenic diets have helped a range of mental health symptoms in patients by reversing insulin resistance, and references the informal concept — sometimes called "type 3 diabetes" — that blood sugar dysregulation and cognitive decline are linked, without presenting this as an established clinical diagnosis.

## Cannabis: A Second Gateway Into the System

Witt-Doerring is also critical of cannabis, describing it as underappreciated as a trigger for mania and psychosis, especially now that legal cannabis products are far more potent than what was available decades ago (he estimates a roughly 40-fold increase in potency, without citing a specific source). His concern is less about cannabis itself and more about what happens next: when a psychotic reaction occurs, he says doctors sometimes downplay the role the drug played and diagnose a standalone disorder like bipolar disorder or schizophrenia instead — leading to another round of long-term antipsychotic prescribing for what may have been a drug-triggered episode. He's careful to distinguish this from claiming cannabis simply *causes* schizophrenia; his more specific claim, based on patients he's personally worked with, is that it can cause psychosis that, in some cases, takes months or even a year or two to fully resolve even after the drug is stopped — which he interprets, as his own hypothesis rather than an established finding, as evidence of a persistent injury rather than an unmasked pre-existing illness.

## Guns, Aggression, and a Question He Says Is Taboo

One of the more contentious threads concerns rare but documented paradoxical reactions — aggression or mania triggered by psychiatric medications, which Witt-Doerring notes is already listed as a possible side effect on the labels of stimulants, antipsychotics, and antidepressants. He compares it to how a room full of people smoking the same amount of cannabis might have most people relaxed and one person become paranoid — an individual, rare reaction rather than the typical one.

He cites one specific historical legal case — a 1990s lawsuit (the *Tobin* case) in which a jury found that an antidepressant contributed to a murder-suicide, a verdict that survived appeal — as evidence that this kind of reaction, while rare, is real and has been recognized in court. He explicitly does not claim medications explain most or all school shootings, and he floats a "social contagion" element as a separate contributing factor. His argument is narrower: that the topic is difficult to research and effectively unspeakable in mainstream discussion, and that a recent Tennessee law requiring investigation of psychiatric medication use after school shootings is, in his view, the first real attempt by any state to actually gather the data needed to evaluate the question.

## What He Says Instead

For all of this criticism, Witt-Doerring doesn't argue that these drugs should never be used. He describes still prescribing SSRIs in some cases — for a patient who has genuinely tried non-drug approaches (diet, sleep, addressing relationship and work problems) and remains severely unwell, he says he'll prescribe with informed consent and close monitoring. His objection is to first-line, checklist-driven prescribing with minimal effort spent on non-drug alternatives first, not to the existence of the drugs.

His own practice now centers on helping patients slowly taper off these medications — a process he says often takes 18 to 24 months for people who've been on them for years, calibrated to avoid triggering the kind of protracted withdrawal injury described above. He says he has no reliable way to predict in advance who will taper easily and who won't; broadly, younger patients and those on a drug for a shorter time do better, but he's seen exceptions in both directions.

His closing advice to someone struggling with anxiety or depression is to audit three areas of life — relationships, sense of purpose, and physical health — and address root causes there rather than assume there's a "chemical imbalance" to be corrected. He's also openly skeptical of AI-based therapy tools, arguing that an AI chatbot tends to simply validate whatever a person tells it, when what people often need is something closer to being challenged or held accountable, not just affirmed.

## The Bottom Line, In His Words

Witt-Doerring frames his position as unpopular within his own profession — he describes himself as viewed as a "dangerous" figure for discouraging people from what are widely called life-saving medications. His response is that the label of "life-saving" deserves scrutiny given the outcomes he describes: rising rates of depression and suicide alongside rising rates of prescribing, a side-effect profile that, in his account, can include persistent — and in some cases apparently long-lasting — sexual and cognitive impacts, a withdrawal process that can itself cause persistent injury, and a regulatory and financial system that he argues is structurally better suited to approving and distributing drugs than to investigating the harm they cause once they're already on the market.

Whether or not you find every piece of that case persuasive, it's a coherent and pointed argument, delivered by someone who has worked on both sides of the prescription pad — as a treating psychiatrist, inside the pharmaceutical industry, and inside the FDA itself — and who now spends his working life trying to undo what he once helped prescribe.
