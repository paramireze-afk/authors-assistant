# Fool's Errand: Time to End the War in Afghanistan
### Scott Horton (The Libertarian Institute, 2017) — Knowledge Base Summary

## 1. What the Book Argues

Horton's central claim, stated plainly in the title, is that the US war in Afghanistan was a **"fool's errand"** from the outset — not a war that was winnable but poorly executed (the frame closer to Wissing's and SIGAR's institutional-failure accounts), but a war built on a **category error**: the belief that a foreign military occupation could remake Afghan society, defeat a rural insurgency rooted in local grievance, and permanently neutralize a transnational terrorist threat, all at once, through nation-building and counterinsurgency. Horton's argument is explicitly a case for withdrawal, and the book is organized to demonstrate that the war's failure was **predictable from the beginning**, not a late-stage deviation from an otherwise sound strategy.

This distinguishes Horton's framing from your other three notes: Wissing and SIGAR largely treat the war as a mismanaged but potentially legitimate undertaking; Horton treats the entire undertaking as misconceived at its foundation. Where Wissing asks "how did the money get diverted," and SIGAR asks "what oversight and institutional failures caused collapse," Horton's prior question is "why did anyone think this could work in the first place."

## 2. The Underlying Explanatory Model

### 2.1 Blowback as the master mechanism
Horton's most load-bearing concept — one he uses explicitly and repeatedly — is **[EXTRACT] blowback**: the idea that US interventions generate hostile reactions and future enemies as a direct, traceable consequence of the intervention itself, rather than hostility arising independently. He applies this at multiple historical layers:
- US and Saudi/Pakistani support for the anti-Soviet mujahideen in the 1980s (**[EXTRACT] Operation Cyclone**) is treated as the seedbed from which both al-Qaeda's networks and the later Taliban movement grew — i.e., the precursor to the enemies the US would fight after 2001.
- The post-2001 occupation itself is treated as generating the insurgency it was meant to suppress: night raids, civilian casualties, detention practices, and the empowerment of predatory warlords are each treated as recruiting mechanisms for the Taliban and allied insurgent networks, not merely unfortunate side effects of counterinsurgency.

This is Horton's core causal chain, and it recurs at every scale in the book: **intervention → local grievance/resentment → recruitment into resistance → intervention escalates to counter the resistance it helped create → further grievance.** He treats this as a loop, not a line — meaning that from his view, tactical "successes" (kill/capture operations, troop surges) plausibly deepen the very problem they're meant to solve, by regenerating the grievance that fuels recruitment.

### 2.2 Threat inflation and mischaracterization of the enemy
A second recurring mechanism is Horton's argument that US policymakers and military leadership consistently **conflated distinct actors and threats** in ways that justified continued, expanding intervention:
- He distinguishes the Taliban (a Pashtun, Afghanistan-focused movement with local and national aims) from al-Qaeda (a transnational jihadist organization with global aims), arguing that US policy and rhetoric repeatedly blurred this distinction to justify treating an entire insurgency as equivalent to a global terrorist threat requiring open-ended war.
- He argues the actual operational and ideological ties between the Taliban and al-Qaeda were narrower, more contingent, and more transactional (rooted in Pashtun hospitality codes and a specific historical relationship, rather than shared global jihadist aims) than official framing suggested.
- This mischaracterization, in his account, is what licensed the expansion from a narrow counterterrorism operation (targeting al-Qaeda specifically, in the immediate post-9/11 period) into an open-ended nation-building and counterinsurgency war against the Taliban broadly.

**[EXTRACT] Taliban–al-Qaeda distinction** — a recurring analytical point worth its own note given how much of Horton's justification-for-withdrawal argument depends on it.

### 2.3 The nation-building fallacy
Horton treats the shift toward nation-building — building a centralized Afghan state, army, and democratic institutions from a historically decentralized, tribally organized society — as a strategic error with deep structural causes, not just poor execution:
- He argues Afghanistan's political geography and history of resisting centralized rule (a theme he traces back through prior foreign interventions in the country) made the entire project of building a strong, legitimate central government from Kabul unlikely to succeed regardless of resourcing or competence.
- He treats "hearts and minds" counterinsurgency doctrine itself as internally contradictory when paired with military occupation: the presence of foreign troops undermines the very legitimacy the doctrine is trying to build, since occupation itself is a primary grievance.

### 2.4 Institutional and political incentives for continuation
Horton devotes attention to *why* a war he judges strategically hopeless nonetheless continued for so long, and this section of his model overlaps with — but is framed differently from — the institutional-failure accounts in your other notes:
- **Bureaucratic and career incentives**: military and intelligence institutions have organizational incentives to report progress and to avoid being associated with "losing" a war, producing optimistic reporting that outlives realistic assessment of conditions on the ground.
- **Political incentives**: elected officials face asymmetric political risk in ending a war (blamed for any subsequent instability or attack) versus continuing one (diffuse, less visible costs), which Horton argues biases the system toward continuation regardless of strategic merit.
- **Defense-industry and institutional stakeholders**: Horton also treats the war's continuation as serving the interests of a broader set of institutional stakeholders (contractors, the military bureaucracy, the intelligence community) for whom the war's continuation is not incidental but useful — echoing Horton's broader noninterventionist critique of the incentive structures surrounding US foreign policy generally. **[EXTRACT] Institutional incentives for war continuation]**

### 2.5 The "good war" narrative as a rhetorical device
Horton pays specific attention to how Afghanistan was rhetorically positioned as the "right war" or "necessary war," especially relative to the more openly contested Iraq War, and argues this framing shielded Afghanistan policy from the level of scrutiny and debate the invasion of Iraq received — even as, in his account, the underlying strategic problems were comparable. This is a recurring rhetorical/narrative mechanism in his explanatory model: **framing itself functions as a causal factor**, by insulating a policy from the kind of debate that might otherwise have changed its course.

## 3. Historical Scope and Structure

Horton's narrative reaches back further than most conventional accounts of the war, and this reach is central to his argument rather than incidental background:

1. **Pre-1979 and Soviet invasion era** — Afghanistan's history of resisting centralized and foreign rule, used to establish a pattern Horton argues repeats with the US occupation.
2. **The 1980s anti-Soviet jihad and Operation Cyclone** — US, Saudi, and Pakistani support for the mujahideen, treated as the direct historical seedbed of both al-Qaeda and the Taliban (this section covers similar ground to Coll's *Ghost Wars*, though Horton's treatment is explicitly framed around the blowback thesis rather than Coll's proxy-war/institutional-friction framing).
3. **The 1990s civil war and Taliban rise** — the chaos following Soviet withdrawal and Afghan factional fighting as the condition from which the Taliban emerged and consolidated control.
4. **9/11 and the initial invasion (2001)** — the narrow, initially successful counterterrorism operation against al-Qaeda and its Taliban hosts.
5. **[EXTRACT] Tora Bora** — treated as a specific, consequential moment (the failure to capture or kill bin Laden in late 2001), often cited by Horton and similar critics as a hinge point after which the war's character shifted from counterterrorism toward prolonged counterinsurgency and occupation.
6. **Mission expansion and nation-building (2002 onward)** — the shift from the narrow post-9/11 operation to open-ended state-building, paralleled (per Horton) by strategic drift and mounting local resentment.
7. **The Iraq War distraction (2003 onward)** — resources and attention diverted to Iraq, a theme that overlaps with Wissing's "Afterthought War" framing but which Horton folds into his broader argument about institutional and political incentives rather than treating primarily as a resourcing failure.
8. **The Obama-era surge** — the escalation of US troop levels and counterinsurgency operations, treated by Horton as an intensification of the same flawed premises rather than a course correction.
9. **Drone warfare and targeted killing** — extended treatment of drone strikes and their civilian-casualty and blowback effects, tying into his broader thesis that tactical counterterrorism successes can generate strategic-level costs. **[EXTRACT] Drone warfare and civilian casualties]**
10. **Warlordism and the Karzai-era government** — the empowerment of local strongmen and a centralized but weak and corrupt Kabul government, a theme that overlaps substantially with SIGAR's later findings on centralization and corruption, though Horton frames it as evidence the entire nation-building premise was flawed from the start rather than as an executional failure.

## 4. Key Terminology (Horton's Own Framing)

- **[EXTRACT] Blowback** — see §2.1; Horton's central causal mechanism, explicitly drawn from the broader noninterventionist/realist critique of US foreign policy.
- **"Fool's errand"** — the book's own title-concept: an undertaking doomed by its own premises, regardless of the skill or resources brought to it.
- **"Good war" narrative** — see §2.5; Horton's term for the rhetorical shielding of Afghanistan policy from scrutiny.
- **Nation-building** — used critically throughout, as the specific strategic overreach Horton identifies as the war's core error.
- **Hearts and minds** — invoked critically, as a doctrine Horton argues is self-undermining when paired with military occupation.

## 6. A Note on Horton's Method and Perspective

Horton writes from an explicitly noninterventionist/anti-imperialist political tradition (he is a longtime antiwar movement figure and radio host), and the book is openly argumentative rather than framed as neutral history — it's built to make the case for withdrawal, using historical narrative and policy analysis as evidence for that case. This is worth keeping in mind when cross-referencing his material against Coll (a journalistic, less polemical institutional history), Wissing (an investigative-journalism critique focused on financial mismanagement), and SIGAR (an official oversight body's institutional findings) — Horton's book operates at a different level of the argument, addressing the prior question of whether the war's basic premise was sound, rather than how well a premised-as-legitimate effort was executed.
