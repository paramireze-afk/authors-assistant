# Markets, Oil, and Empire: World War I as a Clash of Capitalist Powers

## Introduction

The standard story of how World War I began is a story of a single gunshot. On June 28, 1914, a Bosnian Serb nationalist assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo, and within six weeks a tangle of alliances had dragged every great power in Europe into the bloodiest war the world had yet seen. That account is true as far as it goes. But it raises an obvious question: why was Europe a powder keg in the first place? Why were the great powers so heavily armed, so suspicious of one another, and so ready to treat a regional crisis in the Balkans as a fight for survival?

One long-standing answer is that the war was, at bottom, a clash of capitalist empires. On this view, the decades before 1914 saw the great industrial powers competing ever more fiercely for markets, raw materials, investment opportunities, and trade routes. Britain, the reigning economic superpower, watched a younger and faster-growing Germany challenge its position in industry, in shipping, in finance, and eventually at sea. France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire each had their own economic stakes in the outcome. The assassination in Sarajevo was the spark, but the fuel had been piling up for a generation.

This article walks through that interpretation. It covers the theory behind it, the most famous example of economic rivalry, the Berlin–Baghdad Railway, and a series of other flashpoints where money, markets, and empire collided. It closes with the strongest objections to the theory, because any honest look at the question has to grapple with them.

## Part One: The Theory of Imperialist War

The idea that modern wars are driven by economic competition did not begin with the war itself. In 1902, the English economist J.A. Hobson published *Imperialism: A Study*, arguing that the drive for overseas empire came from a specific economic problem. Industrial economies, he said, produced more goods and more capital than their own populations could absorb, largely because wealth was so unequally distributed. Rather than raising wages at home, the owners of capital sought new outlets abroad: new markets to sell into and new territories to invest in. Empire, in Hobson's telling, was a project that benefited a narrow class of financiers and exporters while the costs, in taxes and in soldiers' lives, fell on everyone else.

The Austrian Marxist Rudolf Hilferding built on this in 1910 with *Finance Capital*, describing how big banks and industrial cartels had fused together and leaned on their governments to secure protected markets and spheres of influence. Then in 1916, in the middle of the war, Vladimir Lenin pulled these threads together in *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism*. Lenin argued that the world had by then been fully divided up among the great powers. There were no unclaimed territories left. Any power that wanted a bigger share, and Germany, as a latecomer, very much did, could get it only by taking it from someone else. War, in this framework, was not an accident or a failure of diplomacy. It was the logical result of the system.

You do not have to be a Marxist to find parts of this persuasive. Many non-Marxist historians and anti-war writers have made overlapping arguments, focusing on how commercial and financial interests shaped foreign policy, how arms manufacturers profited from the naval race, and how the rhetoric of national honor often covered for more material concerns. The question is how much of the war these forces actually explain.

## Part Two: The Rise of Germany and the Anxiety of Britain

To understand the rivalry, start with the numbers. In 1870, Germany was not yet even a unified country. By 1914, it had become the leading industrial power in Europe. German steel production had passed Britain's in the 1890s, and by 1913 it was more than double the British figure. Germany led the world in the new industries of the so-called Second Industrial Revolution: chemicals, dyes, pharmaceuticals, and electrical engineering. Firms like Siemens, AEG, BASF, and Bayer were world leaders. German merchant shipping lines competed directly with British ones on the world's oceans, and German banks were expanding their reach into Latin America, the Ottoman Empire, and beyond.

British reaction moved from admiration to alarm. In 1887, Parliament passed the Merchandise Marks Act, requiring imported goods to be labeled with their country of origin. It was intended to warn British consumers away from foreign products. Instead, the label "Made in Germany" became a mark of quality. In 1896, a journalist named Ernest Williams published a bestselling pamphlet titled *Made in Germany* that warned British readers their industrial supremacy was slipping away. The following year, the London magazine *The Saturday Review* ran an article that ended with a play on the old Roman call to destroy Carthage, arguing in effect that Germany must be destroyed, and that if it were wiped out, every Englishman would be richer for it. That was an extreme voice, but it captured a mood.

The anxiety was not only about factories. Britain's entire economic model depended on its position as the hub of world trade and finance. London was the world's banker, the pound sterling was the world's currency, and the Royal Navy guarded the sea lanes that brought food and raw materials into a country that could no longer feed itself. A rising Germany threatened that position on several fronts at once.

## Part Three: The Berlin–Baghdad Railway

Of all the economic rivalries before the war, none captures the imagination quite like the Berlin–Baghdad Railway.

### What it was

The project grew out of a German company, the Anatolian Railway Company, which won a concession from the Ottoman government in 1888 to build a line across Anatolia, the Asian part of modern Turkey. The real ambition, though, was much larger. In 1899, and then in a more detailed agreement in 1903, the Ottoman Sultan granted German interests, led by Deutsche Bank, the right to extend the line all the way to Baghdad and on toward Basra near the Persian Gulf. Combined with existing European rail lines, it would eventually link Berlin, through Vienna and Constantinople, to the heart of Mesopotamia.

The project came with valuable extras. The concession included mining rights in a zone along the route, which mattered enormously once geologists began to suspect that Mesopotamia sat on top of large oil deposits.

### Why it mattered to Germany

For Germany, the railway solved several problems at once. Germany had arrived late to the race for colonies and had ended up with relatively poor territories in Africa and the Pacific. It had no route to the riches of Asia that did not pass through sea lanes controlled by the Royal Navy. A railway running overland from central Europe to the Persian Gulf could not be blockaded by British warships. It would open the Ottoman Empire and the Middle East to German goods, German capital, and German engineers, and it would bind the Ottoman Empire more closely to Berlin as a political and military partner.

Kaiser Wilhelm II embraced this vision personally. On a tour of the Ottoman Empire in 1898, he visited Constantinople, Jerusalem, and Damascus, and in Damascus he publicly declared himself a friend to the world's Muslims, a pointed statement given that hundreds of millions of Muslims lived under British, French, and Russian rule. German strategists dreamed of a sphere of influence stretching from the North Sea to the Persian Gulf.

### Why it alarmed Britain

From London's perspective, the railway was a threat aimed at the jewel of the empire. India was the center of British imperial power, and the Persian Gulf and the routes around it were treated as the outer defenses of India. A German-backed railway ending at the Gulf could, in theory, let Germany project power and commerce into a region Britain regarded as its own. It could allow troops to move toward Egypt and the Suez Canal or toward Persia and India by land.

Britain moved to protect its position. In 1899, the same year as the first railway concession, Britain signed a secret agreement with the ruler of Kuwait, Sheikh Mubarak, placing Kuwait under British protection. Kuwait was the most natural deep-water terminus for the railway. By locking it up, Britain made sure that Germany could not easily reach the Gulf on its own terms.

### The oil factor

Oil raised the stakes further. In the years before the war, the Royal Navy began converting its warships from coal to oil. Oil-fired ships were faster, had longer range, and could refuel at sea, but Britain had plenty of coal and essentially no oil of its own. Winston Churchill, who became First Lord of the Admiralty in 1911, pushed hard for the conversion and for securing reliable supplies. That push led directly to the British government buying a controlling stake in the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, the ancestor of BP, which held oil rights in southwestern Persia near the head of the Gulf. Parliament approved the purchase in the summer of 1914, only weeks before the war.

Suddenly the Persian Gulf and Mesopotamia were not just about guarding the route to India. They were about the fuel supply of the most important navy in the world. A German presence in Mesopotamia, with mining rights along its railway, looked far more dangerous in that light.

### The surprising twist of 1914

Here the story takes a turn that complicates the simple version. By 1914, Britain and Germany had actually negotiated their way to a settlement over the railway and the oil. In the spring of 1914, the competing oil interests in Mesopotamia were combined into the Turkish Petroleum Company, with Anglo-Persian holding half, and Deutsche Bank and Royal Dutch Shell each holding a quarter. Then, in an Anglo-German agreement initialed in mid-June 1914, Germany agreed that the railway would not extend beyond Basra without British consent, effectively conceding British control of the route to the Gulf.

In other words, the specific dispute over the railway was largely settled when the war broke out. The railway itself was also far from finished. Difficult tunnels through the Taurus and Amanus mountains were still incomplete, and the full line to Baghdad would not be completed until 1940.

Critics of the economic interpretation point to this as evidence that the railway was not a cause of the war. Defenders reply that the settlement covered only one project and did not touch the deeper problem: Germany's economic expansion as a whole was seen as a long-term threat, and paper agreements did little to ease that. They also point to what happened once the fighting began. In November 1914, just days after the Ottoman Empire entered the war on Germany's side, British and Indian troops landed at the head of the Persian Gulf to secure the Anglo-Persian oil refinery at Abadan, then pushed on to take Basra. The Mesopotamian campaign that followed ended with Britain in control of Baghdad and, after the war, of the oil-rich Mosul region. Whatever caused the war, its outcome in the Middle East looked a great deal like an oil and empire settlement.

## Part Four: Other Arenas of Economic Conflict

The railway was not the only place where commerce and empire collided. The interpretation becomes stronger when you see the pattern across many regions.

### The naval race

Germany's decision to build a large battle fleet, driven by Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz and enshrined in the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900, was explicitly linked to trade. A great commercial power, the argument went, needed a navy to protect its merchant shipping and overseas interests. To Britain, any navy large enough to protect German trade was also large enough to threaten British trade and the food imports the British population depended on.

The result was an arms race, intensified after Britain launched the revolutionary battleship HMS *Dreadnought* in 1906, which made all existing battleships obsolete and effectively reset the competition. The race poisoned relations between the two countries more than almost any other issue. It also enriched shipbuilders and arms manufacturers in both countries, a fact later critics would highlight.

The historian Nicholas Lambert has shown that before the war, the British Admiralty developed plans for economic warfare against Germany, aiming to use Britain's control of shipping, insurance, communications, and finance to strangle the German economy quickly in the event of conflict. The plans were watered down in practice, but their existence shows that British planners thought about a war with Germany in economic terms.

### Morocco

Twice before 1914, Europe came close to war over Morocco. In 1905, the Kaiser visited Tangier and declared support for Moroccan independence, challenging French efforts to take control of the country. In 1911, Germany sent the gunboat *Panther* to the Moroccan port of Agadir, claiming to protect German commercial interests, including mining interests associated with the Mannesmann brothers. Britain sided firmly with France. The Chancellor of the Exchequer, David Lloyd George, gave a speech warning Germany that Britain would not be treated as if it did not count, a remarkable threat from a man known as a radical and a man of peace. Germany backed down in exchange for a slice of the French Congo.

Both crises were about who would control territory, markets, and resources in North Africa. Both pushed Britain and France closer together, and both left German leaders feeling encircled and humiliated.

### Austria-Hungary, Serbia, and the "Pig War"

The Balkan crisis that actually triggered the war had an economic dimension too. Serbia's economy depended heavily on exporting livestock, especially pigs, to Austria-Hungary. In 1906, when Serbia sought more independence, including buying artillery from the French firm Schneider rather than from the Austro-Hungarian Skoda works, Vienna responded by closing its border to Serbian livestock. The trade dispute became known as the "Pig War."

Serbia survived by finding new markets and new routes through the port of Salonika and new financial backing from France. The result was a Serbia less economically dependent on Vienna, more closely tied to France and Russia, and more defiant. Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia in 1908 deepened the hostility. By 1914, Vienna saw an independent and expanding Serbia as an existential threat to its multinational empire, a fear that had economic as well as nationalist roots.

### Russia and the Turkish Straits

Russia's economy depended on exporting grain, and much of that grain left the Black Sea through the Bosporus and the Dardanelles, the narrow straits controlled by the Ottoman Empire. When the straits were temporarily closed during the Italian-Turkish war of 1911–12 and the Balkan Wars that followed, Russian exports suffered and Russian leaders panicked. The historian Sean McMeekin has argued that Russian ambitions to control the straits, and anxiety about German influence in Constantinople, played a much larger role in Russia's road to war than traditional accounts allow.

The German military mission sent to reorganize the Ottoman army in 1913, under General Otto Liman von Sanders, caused a major diplomatic crisis precisely because it seemed to place German officers in command of the very waterway Russia's economy depended on. Here the Berlin–Baghdad vision and Russian economic vulnerability met head-on.

### Germany and Russia: tariffs and timetables

Germany and Russia also clashed directly over trade. Germany's powerful landed aristocracy, the Junkers, wanted high tariffs on imported grain, which hurt Russian farmers. A commercial treaty signed in 1904, when Russia was weakened by its war with Japan, favored Germany, and Russians resented it. It was due for renegotiation in 1917, and both sides expected a fight.

At the same time, French bankers were pouring money into Russia through huge government loans, some of which were tied explicitly to building strategic railways toward the German frontier. German military planners watched Russia's rapid industrial growth and railway construction with dread, calculating that within a few years Russia would be too strong to defeat. This sense of a closing window, rooted in economic and industrial trends, made some German leaders more willing to risk war in 1914 rather than later.

### France, Germany, and the iron of Lorraine

France and Germany had been enemies since the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, when Germany annexed Alsace and much of Lorraine. That territory was not just a matter of national pride. Lorraine contained some of the richest iron ore deposits in Europe, the foundation of German steel production. Just across the border, the French side held the Briey basin, another major iron field. When Germany drew up its war aims in September 1914, annexing the Briey ore fields was on the list.

### Colonies and the "place in the sun"

Finally, there was the scramble for colonies itself. In 1897, the future German chancellor Bernhard von Bülow declared that Germany did not want to put anyone in the shade but demanded its own place in the sun. Germany's policy of *Weltpolitik*, or world policy, sought colonies, naval bases, and spheres of influence comparable to those of Britain and France. Some German planners dreamed of a *Mittelafrika*, a band of German territory across the middle of the continent. Britain and Germany even negotiated, in 1898 and again in 1913–14, over how they might divide up Portugal's African colonies if Portugal ever let them go.

## Part Five: What the War Aims Revealed

One way to test the economic interpretation is to look at what the powers wanted once the war was underway. Here the evidence is striking.

In September 1914, German Chancellor Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg's office drafted what is now called the September Program. It called for a central European economic union under German leadership, with France weakened, Belgium reduced to a dependent state, the Briey iron fields annexed, and a German colonial empire in central Africa. The historian Fritz Fischer, who studied these documents in the 1950s and 1960s, argued that German war aims were deeply shaped by the interests of German industry and finance.

The Allies were no different. In 1916, the Allied powers held the Paris Economic Conference and agreed to plans for continuing economic warfare against the Central Powers even after the war ended, including restrictions on German trade and access to raw materials. The same year, Britain and France secretly agreed in the Sykes-Picot Agreement on how to divide the Ottoman Middle East between them. After the war, Germany's colonies were taken over by the victors under the League of Nations mandate system, and the 1920 San Remo agreement gave Britain control of Mesopotamia, now Iraq, while dividing up its oil. The former German share in the Turkish Petroleum Company was handed to France.

The United States had its own economic entanglement. Officially neutral from 1914 to 1917, the U.S. became the Allies' supplier and banker. American trade with Britain and France boomed, and American banks, led by J.P. Morgan, extended loans to the Allies that dwarfed anything lent to Germany. By the time the U.S. entered the war in 1917, American financial interests were heavily tied to an Allied victory. In the 1930s, the Senate's Nye Committee investigated the role of bankers and arms makers in drawing the country into war, and the decorated Marine general Smedley Butler famously described war as a racket. Their conclusions remain debated, but they reflect a widespread suspicion that money, not just principle, had steered American policy.

## Part Six: The Case Against the Theory

The economic interpretation has real force, but it faces serious objections, and a fair account has to take them seriously.

The first objection is that economic interdependence argued against war, not for it. Britain and Germany were among each other's largest trading partners. In 1909, the British writer Norman Angell published *The Great Illusion*, arguing that war between modern industrial economies would be economically ruinous for all sides, winners included. He was right about the ruin, and many businessmen agreed with him. When the July Crisis broke in 1914, the City of London panicked. Financial markets seized up, and the London Stock Exchange closed at the end of July. Many bankers and industrialists in both Britain and Germany were horrified by the prospect of war. If capitalists drove the war, critics ask, why were so many capitalists trying to prevent it?

The second objection is timing. As the railway story shows, Anglo-German economic and colonial disputes were easing in the years just before 1914. The two countries had reached agreements on the railway, on Mesopotamian oil, and in principle on the Portuguese colonies. The naval race had cooled as Britain clearly won it. If economic rivalry was the cause, why did war come just as that rivalry was being managed?

The third objection is that the actual decisions of July 1914 were made by a small number of monarchs, ministers, diplomats, and generals, and their documented concerns were mostly about alliances, prestige, military timetables, and national security, not markets. Historians like Christopher Clark emphasize miscalculation, mutual fear, and the rigid war plans of the general staffs, especially Germany's Schlieffen Plan, which required attacking France through Belgium the moment war with Russia looked likely. Others stress nationalism, which moved masses of people in a way that tariff schedules never could.

A middle position, held by many historians, is that economic rivalry was a background condition rather than a direct cause. It helped create the hostility, the arms races, and the alliance blocs that made the July Crisis so dangerous, but the decision to go to war came out of strategic and political calculations. Realist scholars of international relations, such as John Mearsheimer, frame it somewhat differently: Germany's growing economic power translated into military power, and Britain, as it had done for centuries, moved to balance against whatever power threatened to dominate the European continent. On that reading, economics mattered mainly because economic strength is the foundation of military strength.

## Conclusion

Was World War I a clash of capitalist powers? The answer depends on what you mean by "cause." If you are looking for the reason Europe went to war in August 1914 rather than some other time, the answer lies mostly in the Balkans, the alliances, and the choices of a few dozen men in a few capital cities. But if you are asking why those powers were arrayed against each other, why they were so heavily armed, and why they saw each other as existential threats, economic rivalry is a large part of the answer.

The Berlin–Baghdad Railway stands as a vivid symbol of that rivalry: a German project to reach the oil, markets, and strategic waters of the Middle East by land, met by a British empire determined to keep the Persian Gulf as its own. The dispute was patched up on paper just before the war, but the war itself ended with Britain in Baghdad, with Mosul's oil divided among the victors, and with the modern Middle East carved out of the ruins of the Ottoman Empire. Whatever started the war, the peace that followed was shaped in no small part by markets, money, and oil, and the consequences of that peace are still with us more than a century later.
