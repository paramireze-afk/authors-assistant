# Damn the Torpedoes: The Life of Admiral David Glasgow Farragut

On the morning of August 5, 1864, a 63-year-old naval officer climbed into the rigging of his flagship as it steamed toward the mouth of Mobile Bay, Alabama. A sailor tied him to the mast so he would not fall if he were wounded. Within the hour, the lead ironclad in his column had struck a mine and sunk in minutes, his line of ships had stalled under the guns of a Confederate fort, and the attack seemed close to falling apart. The officer's response, compressed by history into four words, made him one of the most famous figures of the Civil War: *"Damn the torpedoes!"*

That officer was David Glasgow Farragut, and the moment at Mobile Bay was the high point of a naval career that had started more than half a century earlier, when he was nine years old.

## An Unusual Beginning

Farragut was born James Glasgow Farragut on July 5, 1801, at Campbell's Station near Knoxville, Tennessee, then on the American frontier. His father, Jordi (George) Farragut, was born on the Mediterranean island of Menorca and had fought for the American side in the Revolutionary War. The family later moved to New Orleans, where his father served in the small naval force on the Mississippi.

Tragedy shaped the boy's future. In 1808 his mother died of yellow fever. Around the same time, the elder Farragut had cared for the dying father of naval officer David Porter. In gratitude, Porter offered to take young James into his own household and raise him as a ward. It was through Porter that the boy entered the navy, receiving a midshipman's warrant in 1810 at the age of nine. He later took the name David in honor of his guardian.

## A Child at War

The War of 1812 came quickly. Farragut served aboard USS *Essex*, commanded by Porter, during its raiding cruise in the Pacific against British whaling ships. When the *Essex* captured a prize vessel, the 12-year-old midshipman was placed in charge of sailing it to port, an extraordinary responsibility for a child, and one he handled when the ship's older captain balked at taking orders from him.

In 1814 the *Essex* was cornered and defeated by two British warships off Valparaíso, Chile. Farragut was wounded and taken prisoner, though he was soon exchanged. The battle gave him an early and unforgettable lesson in the cost of naval combat.

## Decades of Steady Service

Farragut's career between the War of 1812 and the Civil War was long, solid, and largely unglamorous. He served on anti-piracy patrols in the Caribbean during the 1820s, spent years on routine sea duty and shore assignments, and saw limited action during the Mexican–American War. In 1854 he was sent to California to establish the Mare Island Navy Yard near San Francisco, the Navy's first permanent base on the Pacific coast.

His personal life was marked by both devotion and loss. His first wife, Susan Marchant, suffered a long illness, and he nursed her for years until her death in 1840. In 1843 he married Virginia Loyall of Norfolk, Virginia, and their only child, Loyall Farragut, was born the following year.

By 1860, Farragut was a captain nearing 60, respected but hardly famous. Many observers assumed his career was winding down.

## A Southerner Chooses the Union

The secession crisis forced a painful choice. Farragut was Southern by birth, had married into a Virginia family, and lived in Norfolk. When Virginia left the Union in April 1861, many of his friends and neighbors expected him to follow. He refused. According to accounts from the time, he told acquaintances that they would regret their decision, and he moved his family north to Hastings-on-Hudson, New York.

His Southern roots made some Union officials wary of him at first. That wariness did not last long.

## New Orleans, 1862

New Orleans was the largest city in the Confederacy and the gateway to the Mississippi River. In early 1862 Farragut was given command of the West Gulf Blockading Squadron and ordered to take it.

The river approach was guarded by two strong masonry forts, Jackson and St. Philip, along with a barrier of chained hulks, fire rafts, and a small Confederate flotilla. After a mortar bombardment failed to reduce the forts, Farragut decided to simply run past them. In the early hours of April 24, 1862, his ships steamed upriver in darkness through heavy fire. His flagship, USS *Hartford*, was set ablaze by a fire raft and briefly ran aground, but the crew put out the flames and pushed on.

The fleet got through. New Orleans surrendered days later, and the forts, now cut off, soon followed. It was one of the most important Union victories of the war, and Congress rewarded Farragut by creating the rank of rear admiral, which he became the first to hold in July 1862.

## The Mississippi Campaign

Farragut then pushed up the Mississippi, supporting efforts against Vicksburg and Port Hudson, the two fortresses that kept a stretch of the river in Confederate hands. In March 1863 he attempted to run his ships past the batteries at Port Hudson. Only the *Hartford* and one gunboat lashed to its side made it through, but that was enough to let him patrol the river between the two strongholds and disrupt Confederate supply lines from the west. Both Vicksburg and Port Hudson fell that July, giving the Union control of the entire river.

## The Battle of Mobile Bay, 1864

By 1864, Mobile was one of the last major Gulf ports still open to Confederate blockade runners. The entrance to Mobile Bay was narrow and heavily defended by Fort Morgan and Fort Gaines, along with a field of underwater mines, then called "torpedoes." Inside the bay waited a small Confederate squadron led by the powerful ironclad CSS *Tennessee*, commanded by Admiral Franklin Buchanan.

Farragut's fleet of wooden ships and ironclad monitors began its run into the bay on August 5. The monitor USS *Tecumseh*, leading the way, struck a mine and sank almost instantly, taking most of its crew down with it. The ship ahead of the *Hartford*, USS *Brooklyn*, stopped and began backing up, throwing the column into confusion directly beneath the guns of Fort Morgan.

Farragut ordered the *Hartford* to go around the *Brooklyn* and steam straight through the minefield. It was here that he is said to have shouted his famous order. The exact wording was not recorded at the moment and was reported only later, with versions such as "Damn the torpedoes! Full speed ahead!" and "Damn the torpedoes! Four bells! Captain Drayton, go ahead!" Whatever he said, the meaning was clear, and the fleet followed. Several crew members later reported hearing mines bump against the hulls without exploding, likely because many had been damaged by long exposure to seawater.

Once inside the bay, the Union fleet fought a fierce battle with the *Tennessee*, which took on several ships alone before it was battered into surrender. Fort Gaines surrendered within days, and Fort Morgan fell on August 23 after a siege. The city of Mobile itself held out until April 1865, but with the bay closed, its value as a port was gone.

Coming shortly before the 1864 presidential election, the victory, together with the fall of Atlanta weeks later, raised Northern morale and helped secure Abraham Lincoln's reelection.

## The First Admiral

Farragut was promoted to vice admiral in December 1864, again the first officer to hold that rank. In July 1866 Congress created the rank of admiral for him, making him the first full admiral in the history of the US Navy.

From 1867 to 1868 he commanded the European Squadron on a goodwill cruise that turned into something of a triumphal tour. One of the most personal stops was Menorca, his father's birthplace, where crowds turned out to welcome the famous son of the island.

## Death and Legacy

Farragut died on August 14, 1870, in Portsmouth, New Hampshire, at the age of 69. He was buried in Woodlawn Cemetery in the Bronx, New York, following a funeral procession that drew thousands.

His legacy is visible across the country. Farragut Square in Washington, D.C., features a statue cast from metal taken from the propeller of the *Hartford*, sculpted by Vinnie Ream. A celebrated monument by Augustus Saint-Gaudens stands in New York's Madison Square Park. The town of Farragut, Tennessee, near his birthplace, bears his name, as have a long line of US Navy destroyers.

More than his monuments, though, his lasting influence is an idea. Farragut showed that bold, decisive action, carefully planned but executed without hesitation, could win battles that caution would lose. The phrase "Damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead" outlived its original circumstances to become a common American saying for pressing forward in spite of risk, and it remains tied to a quiet stretch of water at the mouth of Mobile Bay.
