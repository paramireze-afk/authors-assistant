# The Trillion-Dollar Bet: Steve Hsu on the AI Bubble, the US-China Race, and the End of Maritime Power

Steve Hsu is an unusually wide-ranging figure: a physicist who has worked in quantum theory, dark energy, and computational genomics, a serial startup founder in AI and information security, and a prominent commentator on his Substack, *Information Processing*, and his podcast, *Manifold*. In this episode of *Multipolarity Dialogues*, he sat down with the hosts to walk through two questions he thinks are central to the future of American power: can the US actually win the AI race against China, and how has the proliferation of cheap precision weapons changed warfare — and with it, the viability of the kind of maritime power the US and Britain have relied on for two centuries.

## An Inverted Pyramid: Is AI Investment a Bubble?

Hsu's starting point is a shift he's noticed in how AI spending is being scrutinized. For years, equity investors — who he says are moved by "feels and vibes" and can be swayed by narrative — were the ones setting the tone. Now bond investors are paying attention, and they care about hard numbers: forecasted cash flows and net present value. That shift, he argues, is forcing a more sober look at the AI buildout.

He frames the situation with three numbers, each roughly ten times smaller than the last:

- **A trillion dollars** — the scale of annual infrastructure investment going into data centers and GPUs.
- **A hundred billion dollars** — the aggregate revenue flowing mostly through OpenAI and Anthropic, companies that are still deeply unprofitable and have to keep raising capital.
- **Tens of billions of dollars** — Hsu's estimate of the "organic" demand within that hundred billion: real businesses productively using AI and paying out of pocket, rather than capital being recycled between venture funds, sovereign wealth funds, and big tech firms investing in one another.

A few years ago, companies like Microsoft, Google, and Meta were profitable enough to fund AI infrastructure entirely out of operating cash flow. That's no longer true — even the largest tech companies are now borrowing heavily to keep up, something previously more associated with a company like Oracle.

Whether this is a bubble, in Hsu's view, comes down to a growth-rate question. If real-world organic use of AI keeps doubling every year for the next four years, current infrastructure spending could be justified by future revenue. If it instead grows a still-fast 50% a year, the investments made over the past few years will, in hindsight, look unjustified. He picks a four-year horizon deliberately: it roughly matches how long GPUs retain most of their value for accounting purposes before depreciation eats into the investment case.

Hsu is careful to separate this financial question from his belief in AI's long-run value. He thinks the technology is already "unbelievably impressive" and will keep improving, but that businesses and economies take time to find the best uses for new technology. If that adoption lag is longer than markets are currently pricing in, he expects something like the outcome of the dot-com crash: painful short-term losses for investors, but genuine, large economic benefits emerging over a longer horizon of about a decade.

## Two Different AI Industries: The US and China

The hosts pushed Hsu to explain why, if American AI labs are burning cash at such a rate, Chinese firms — which mostly give their models away as open source — aren't facing the same problem. Hsu's answer is that the two countries have built structurally different AI industries.

Labs like DeepSeek and Moonshot (maker of the Kimi model) run with teams of roughly 200–300 people, a tenth the size of OpenAI or Anthropic, while still shipping models just behind the American frontier. Because Chinese firms have had constrained access to advanced chips, Hsu argues they've had to make their models radically more efficient, often training them for an order of magnitude less than US labs spend, and they're more disciplined about avoiding costly exploratory compute runs. Combined with cheaper Chinese data-center construction and electricity, and heavy state investment in domestic chip fabrication, Hsu's assessment is that the Chinese AI industry is "probably not running this as a speculative, currently loss-making enterprise" the way American labs are — it's closer to being profitable, which he suggests could give it more staying power than a model dependent on speculative excess.

He also lays out the competition as really two separate races running in parallel:

1. **A hardware/semiconductor race**, largely about Nvidia versus China's Huawei and its domestic fab champion SMIC (as opposed to Taiwan's TSMC, which fabricates Nvidia's chips). Huawei hasn't fully caught up, but it's closing the gap.
2. **A software/model race**, where the outcome for Nvidia could diverge from the outcome for OpenAI and Anthropic — for instance, Nvidia chips could remain dominant even while cheaper, nearly-as-good Chinese open-source models running on those chips squeeze American labs' margins.

Hsu raises the prospect of Chinese "token factories": large data centers combining cheap power, adequate chips, and good-enough open models, potentially exporting most of their output as inference tokens to businesses outside China — not just in the developing world, but potentially in more advanced economies too.

### Two Scenarios for How This Plays Out

Asked what this means for OpenAI, Anthropic, and other Western labs, Hsu sketches two competing narratives:

- **The optimistic case for US labs**: America's larger capital base and compute access let it stay ahead on raw model capability, letting American firms charge a premium and capture the lion's share of the value AI creates — new drugs, replacing the work of millions of white-collar workers and developers. Anyone investing in Anthropic's reported $2 trillion valuation, Hsu says, has to believe this story.
- **The commoditization case**: developers increasingly toggle between an expensive frontier model and cheaper Chinese alternatives depending on the task, allocating tokens judiciously. In this scenario, the only markets fully protected for US labs are the US itself and a handful of close allies; large parts of Asia, Latin America, and much of Europe end up served substantially by Chinese-model-powered token factories.

On the question of "lock-in" — whether switching away from a tool like Claude would be as hard as it was to leave the Microsoft ecosystem in the 1990s — Hsu is blunt: there is almost none. He describes how advanced developers already mix and match models by task, routing cheaper agentic subtasks to low-cost open models like DeepSeek while reserving a premium model such as Claude Opus for the "master coordinator" or software-architect role. This granular, per-task allocation is, in his view, already an emerging market structure, and it undercuts any assumption that a leading US lab could win the way Microsoft won the desktop-software wars through switching costs alone.

The hosts draw a parallel to earlier format wars — VHS beating the technically superior Betamax, the UK's round satellite dishes beating the "squarial," Blu-ray beating HD DVD — where adoption and critical mass mattered more than product quality. Hsu's point is that AI may not follow that pattern, precisely because there's so little lock-in.

## What's Actually at Stake: Productivity, Not Necessarily a Winner-Take-All Step Change

Asked to size up the long-run stakes, Hsu resists the idea of a dramatic "step change." He expects a substantial increase in productivity growth from AI, but sees no strong reason to assume only one country — the US or China — captures it; both could see comparable gains, alongside other economies that adopt the technology.

He reaches for the analogy of the Second Industrial Revolution — skyscrapers, the telephone, the automobile, the railroad, standardized machine tools — technologies that, taken together, transformed daily life so completely that modern people forget how gradual and messy the transition actually was. Contemporaries didn't experience "a step change"; they experienced productivity growth ticking up from something like 3% to 7%, compounded over decades. Only in retrospect does it look discontinuous. He expects AI to produce a similar retrospective step change, possibly compressed into one or one-and-a-half decades rather than several.

Crucially, Hsu warns that faster productivity growth doesn't imply broadly shared prosperity. He points out that the Second Industrial Revolution coincided with the Long Depression of roughly 1880–1900, and that today, the people getting rich fastest from AI are disproportionately people who were already wealthy, concentrated geographically in the San Francisco Bay Area. He notes that OpenAI's Sam Altman has argued for years that a universal basic income will likely be necessary because of AI's effects on labor markets. Whether the gains get redistributed, in Hsu's view, depends heavily on whether ordinary people can enforce redistribution through democratic politics — absent that, he doesn't expect it to happen on its own.

### The "Centaur" Era and the One-Person Billion-Dollar Company

One of the more striking parts of the conversation is Hsu's use of chess as an analogy for how AI is currently being used. After IBM's Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov, there was a period — sometimes called "centaur chess" — where a skilled human paired with a chess engine could beat the engine alone. That era lasted roughly a decade before ending completely. Notably, Hsu stresses, it was never an *average* player plus an engine beating a stronger engine — it was always an elite grandmaster who understood the engine's specific weaknesses.

He sees the same pattern already emerging in how people use AI models today: an average graduate student gets little value from AI in physics research because the models' errors are too subtle to catch, while a world-class researcher with 20 years of experience knows exactly where models fail and can correct for it, gaining a large productivity multiplier. The same holds for software development — an average developer may not help the models much, but a great software architect sees outsized gains.

Hsu predicts a similar "centaur" period for human-AI collaboration broadly: temporary, valuable, and available mainly to an exceptional minority who can add value on top of what the models produce rather than degrade it with their own mistakes. He connects this to a trope circulating in both Silicon Valley and China — the race to create the first "one-person company," a business run by a single human plus a fleet of AI agents that nonetheless reaches unicorn scale. Whether that phenomenon represents a democratizing shift in wealth, or simply a way for already-exceptional individuals to pull further ahead of everyone else, Hsu leaves as an open and, in his view, currently unresolved question. His longer-term expectation is that the centaur period will end, at which point the owners of capital — the compute and the models themselves — will likely be in a much stronger bargaining position than people who sell their labor.

## The Other Race: Precision Strike and the Decline of Maritime Power

The conversation's second half turns to a subject the *Multipolarity* hosts have written about extensively: how cheap drones and missiles are reshaping warfare. Hsu summarizes the core dynamic as a fundamental cost asymmetry that has been understood since the Reagan-era "Star Wars" missile-defense debate of the 1980s, when defense scientists without industry conflicts of interest concluded that a defender would always have to spend more to intercept a missile than an attacker spent to build it.

An offensive weapon — a drone or missile — only needs to travel reliably from point A to point B, and perhaps jitter or maneuver enough to complicate interception. A defensive interceptor, by contrast, needs to be more maneuverable than the incoming weapon, detect it with high precision, and often depends on expensive supporting infrastructure like large radar installations. That asymmetry, Hsu argues, has now been decisively exploited by Iran, whose missile and drone programs — and those of proxies it arms, such as the Houthis in Yemen — have crossed the threshold of reliability and precision that the Soviet Union once possessed. The consequence, in his telling, is that the US currently cannot fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz or the Red Sea despite facing what he describes as comparatively low-tech opposition.

### Attackers Gain, But Only Against Vulnerable Targets

Hsu is careful to qualify where this asymmetry applies: specifically to high-value targets that are also vulnerable — radar installations, desalination plants, nuclear power plants, ships at sea. Where a state has invested in hardening — building infrastructure underground, tunneling into mountains with multiple exits and even internal excavation equipment to dig out from collapsed entrances — that target may still be *high-value* but is no longer *vulnerable* in the same way. He cites Iranian tunnel complexes for missile and drone manufacturing as an example, and points to Israeli intelligence reports (via the *Jerusalem Post*) describing surprise at how quickly Iran reconstituted drone and missile production after earlier strikes on the visible entrances to these facilities — the irreplaceable manufacturing equipment inside was never actually reachable.

The hosts connect this to a US Marine Corps Gazette article titled "Digging is Winning," arguing that above-ground barracks and command posts are no longer viable given how cheap and proliferated precision strike has become, and to comments from King's College London war studies professor David Betts, who has suggested cities may need to revert to something resembling medieval walls and towers — potentially repurposing existing ring roads — equipped with modern point-defense systems like the laser weapons currently being developed by the UK's Royal Navy and reportedly by Israel.

### Why Maritime Power Is Especially Exposed

Hsu argues these dynamics are "extremely negative for maritime powers" specifically. A country doesn't need to be a top-tier military power to threaten a warship approaching its coast; it just needs to buy systems from China, Russia, or increasingly Iran. Munitions with onboard sensing can now identify a ship, maneuver toward it, and partially evade interceptors from hundreds of kilometers away, making it dangerous for naval forces to operate near a hostile coastline even when that coastline belongs to a comparatively weak state.

He argues that this capability may have quietly existed for a long time, but nobody tested it because attacking a US Navy ship seemed suicidal, given the "hegemon" reaction it would invite. What has changed, in his account, is that states have now tested that assumption and found it wanting: the Houthis' effective closure of the Red Sea over the past several years is, in his framing, proof that the US "can claim" to dominate the world's shipping lanes, "but it's not true." He suggests the pattern was visible well before the more recent conflict involving Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, and that planners should have drawn the lesson from the Red Sea earlier than they did.

On the specific question of missile defense against larger, more sophisticated threats, Hsu is unambiguous: the technical problem of shooting down a determined volley of ballistic or hypersonic missiles remains unsolved, just as it was during the Cold War, and this is precisely what underpins the logic of mutual assured destruction between major nuclear powers. Missile defense might work against a handful of missiles from a state like North Korea, but not against a sustained, high-volume attack — a limitation he says anyone with genuine technical understanding of the physics involved would readily admit.

## Closing Thoughts

Across both halves of the conversation, a common thread emerges: technological change is outpacing the assumptions — financial, strategic, and organizational — built up over the preceding decades. On the economic side, Hsu sees a real chance AI capital spending has outrun near-term organic demand, even as he remains confident in AI's long-run transformative value; the question is one of timing and distribution, not whether the technology matters. On the military side, he sees the same asymmetry that troubled Cold War-era defense planners now playing out in the Middle East and the Red Sea, with consequences that, in his assessment, fall hardest on the kind of expeditionary, ocean-going military power the United States and Britain have relied on for generations.

Hsu writes regularly on his Substack *Information Processing* and posts frequently on X; his own podcast, *Manifold*, was recommended by the hosts as a deeper dive into many of the technological and social themes touched on in this conversation.
