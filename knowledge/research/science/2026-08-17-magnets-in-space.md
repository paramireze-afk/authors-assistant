## The Question Nobody's Asking

Somewhere in the last hundred years, physics got extremely good at answering *how much* and *how fast*, and quietly stopped asking *why*.

That's the provocation at the heart of a Saturday-morning live stream from the podcast *Demystifying Sci*, where hosts Shiloh and Anastasia are walking listeners through their new book, *Paradox Lost: The Material Principles of Natural Philosophy*. Their claim is not that the equations of modern physics are wrong. Quite the opposite — they're at pains to say that the mathematical description of forces like electromagnetism is one of the most precise achievements in the history of science, "solved in some cases to like 13 decimal places, experimentally confirmed." The problem, as they see it, is that success at *describing* a phenomenon quantitatively has been mistaken for *explaining* it.

Their favorite illustration of the gap is the humble point particle. Modern physics, they argue, locates forces as emanating from a mathematical point — "a location without a body." And once you've defined a force as something that simply radiates from a dimensionless point in space, you've made it impossible, in principle, to ask what's generating it. The force just *is*. Fields become "a map of the forces that bodies will experience at different locations in space" — useful, precise, predictive — but not a material thing that could, in the ordinary physical sense, *push* or *pull* anything. As Shiloh puts it, quoting a common intuition from kinematics: any time you push a block across a table, "there is an actor at each end of the story." That intuition evaporates completely once you get below the scale of everyday observation, and modern physics has, in their view, simply made peace with that evaporation rather than treating it as a problem to be solved.

They trace this partly to history. Prior to about 1900, physicists were deeply preoccupied with finding a physical *medium* for phenomena like light and electromagnetism — the luminiferous ether, vortex theories, cog theories. But the ether had two fatal flaws: nobody could detect it directly, and nobody could figure out how to unify it with ordinary matter into one coherent picture. When experiments around the turn of the twentieth century failed to detect the ether the way physicists expected, the field didn't necessarily conclude the ether was the wrong *kind* of proposal — it largely gave up on the project of finding a material medium at all, and moved toward treating the mathematics itself as the final answer. Shiloh describes this as almost a relief for physicists exhausted by decades of failed mechanical theories: "we don't have to preoccupy ourselves with this question of what is the thing that waves when light transmits. We have the equations. We have the fields. It's good enough."

Paradox Lost is an attempt to go back and finish the job the ether theorists never completed — not by reviving a single homogeneous fluid ether, but by proposing that empty space is filled with *structured* material extensions of atoms themselves.

## Three Rules for Playing the Game

Rather than trying to explain literally everything down to some final, irreducible layer of reality — a project they're openly skeptical of, joking about physicists' fondness for "foundations of fundamentalism of fundamentals, especially ones that end in pixels" — Shiloh and Anastasia set themselves a narrower, more disciplined goal. If you're going to call something a *physical* explanation, they argue, it has to follow a small number of ground rules. Any science, they say, needs this kind of foundational contract; they draw a parallel to biology's ongoing struggle to even define what "life" is, which makes it hard to build coherent theories about it.

Their contract comes down to three axioms, printed on page 74 of the book:

1. **All physical phenomena are the result of interactions between material bodies.**
2. **A material body is that which has a surface, a volume, and a location** — an enclosed region of space with a boundary separating its inside from its outside.
3. **All phenomena come down to displacement.** Because material bodies are mutually exclusive (two things can't occupy the same space), any interaction between them ultimately reduces to pushing, or pulling (which they treat as push reversed, via interlocking or "hooked" structures).

Stated plainly, these axioms sound almost too obvious to need saying — which is exactly the point. Once you accept that a physical explanation has to involve actual bodies with volume, location, and boundaries, mutually shoving each other around, you can't be satisfied by an account of electricity or magnetism that bottoms out in a force radiating from a bodiless point. Something concrete has to be doing the pushing.

## The Electron That Isn't a Thing

The first place this framework gets applied is the electron — and the move is genuinely radical. Shiloh and Anastasia reject the idea that free electrons exist as discrete little particles that can be peeled off an atom and passed along a wire, through the air, or through water. There is, in their words, no "little bleb" of charge traveling from place to place.

Their inspiration comes from an older episode in physics history: heat. Not so long ago, heat was treated much like electricity is today — as the flow of a fluid-like substance called "caloric," with its own particles. Eventually, physicists realized heat was better understood not as a substance moving from place to place, but as the *literal motion* of atoms and molecules — the jiggling we now take for granted in the kinetic theory of heat, calorie and all. Shiloh and Anastasia want to do the same thing to the electron: reconceive it not as a particle that moves, but as a *kind of motion* — specifically, a circulation on the surface of the atom itself.

Concretely, their hypothesis is that atoms are built from countless small subunits they call **atomic fibers**. These fibers make up the atom's outer surface and are proposed to flow — not as a simple fluid, but with a tensile, interlocked quality, "very similar to the way that we think about bubbles and water as having interlocked subunits." Picture, loosely, a bubble's skin: it has surface tension, it can flex and deform, but it's still a continuous, connected material structure.

The specific pattern this fiber traces is what they call an **involuting torus** — imagine a donut shape where material continuously flows around the outer equator, dives inward through the central hole, and re-emerges. This shape isn't chosen arbitrarily. It's meant to reproduce something very specific and very strange that's already well established experimentally: the electron's intrinsic **spin-1/2**.

## Why Spin-1/2 Actually Matters Here

The Stern-Gerlach experiment is the classic demonstration that gave physicists this puzzle in the first place. Fire a stream of neutral atoms (each with a single valence electron) through a magnetic field and onto a photographic plate. With the magnet off, the atoms land in a simple spray across the plate. Turn the magnet on, and instead of spreading out smoothly, the spray **splits cleanly in two** — every atom deflects either up or down, nothing in between.

This told physicists that electrons carry some kind of intrinsic angular momentum distinct from their orbital motion around the nucleus — something later formalized mathematically by Paul Dirac as "spin." But it's a peculiar kind of spin. A basketball, or any object with ordinary "spin one," returns to its original orientation after a single 360° rotation. Spin-1/2 objects are stranger: they require a full **720°**, two complete rotations, before returning to their starting configuration. This is often demonstrated with party tricks — Anastasia mentions the classic "plate trick," where you rotate a coffee mug (minus the coffee) twice to bring your arm back to a comfortable position, or the "Dirac belt trick" with a looped belt.

What excites Shiloh and Anastasia is that an involuting torus does this naturally, with no extra assumptions bolted on. Track a single point of fiber starting on the outer equator: after one 360° pass, it has migrated to the center of the vortex and is now moving in the *opposite* direction (downward instead of upward). It takes a second full 360° revolution to bring that point back to its starting position and orientation. In other words, the shape they've chosen to explain the electron *automatically* reproduces the 720°-periodicity that experiments demand — which they treat as a strong point in favor of the model, and one of the earliest and most satisfying results in the whole project. As a bonus, they note, the roughly spherical torus also matches the general shape predicted for the hydrogen atom by the Schrödinger wave equation, and it does away with the need for some separate "magical force" binding the nucleus to the rest of the atom — in their picture, the nucleus is simply continuous with the atom's outer surface.

## Charge as a Change of Speed, Not a New Particle

If the electron isn't a particle but a pattern of circulating motion, what is *charge*? Here the hosts reach for another physical analogy: the **Chladni plate**, a simple demonstration where a metal plate covered in loose sand is placed on a speaker cone. With no tone playing, the sand sits randomly. As you play a pure tone and slowly raise its frequency, the plate vibrates unevenly — some regions move, others stay still — and the sand collects along the still lines, tracing out increasingly intricate geometric patterns as the frequency increases.

Shiloh and Anastasia propose that atomic orbitals work the same way. If the electron is really the atom's surface fiber circulating faster and faster, then as you drive that circulation harder — analogous to raising the Chladni plate's frequency — the surface has to reorganize into new shapes to accommodate the increased motion. This reorganization is what conventional physics sees as new "orbitals" appearing as electrons are added to an atom.

The mechanism they propose for *why* new shapes appear at all comes down to the nucleus acting as a **bottleneck**. All the atom's circulating fiber has to pass through this narrow central point on every cycle. There's a physical limit to how fast material can be pushed through that bottleneck — but the outer surface can still be driven faster. The way the system resolves this tension is by deforming: by increasing the *path length* a given bit of fiber has to travel per cycle, the surface can move at a higher velocity while the *time* to complete one full circulation through the nucleus stays fixed. That geometric deformation is what shows up, on the outside, as a new orbital shape.

This reframes charge itself: it isn't a discrete, free-floating quantity glued onto a particle. It's **a rate** — specifically, the rate of the atom's surface circulation relative to the nucleus's fixed throughput. **Positive** charge, in their model, corresponds to an atom whose surface motion is *underdriven* relative to that baseline — the surface pulls back and exposes more of the nucleus's polar structure. **Negative** charge is the reverse: the surface is *overdriven*, circulating faster than its comfortable, neutral baseline.

They illustrate this with helium, chosen deliberately because it's one of the few atoms small enough that adding a little negative charge visibly forces it into a new orbital shape (most heavier atoms have enough "wiggle room" in their existing orbital structure to absorb small changes without a dramatic shift).

They also address a subtlety here that they think conventional quantum mechanics glosses over: even though the *appearance* of a new orbital is a discrete, sudden event (an electron either "shows up" or it doesn't), the underlying process — the atom's surface accelerating — is continuous. The reason physics tends to treat charge as inherently quantized with "no underlying cause," in their telling, is that the acceleration is undetectable right up until it crosses the threshold needed to trigger a shape change. The discreteness is real, but it's an artifact of measurement, not evidence that there's no continuous process underneath it.

## What's Actually Moving Through a Wire

This is where the model gets tested against something very concrete: electric current. The conventional picture is that current is literally free electrons — little charged particles — drifting through a wire from the negative terminal of a battery to the positive terminal. Shiloh and Anastasia note a well-known tension in that picture: electrical signals propagate through a circuit at close to the speed of light, while the actual measured drift velocity of electrons through copper is, in their memorable phrase, "about the pace of a garden snail." Something is traveling fast; it isn't the particles themselves.

Their alternative: nothing material travels down the wire at all. What propagates is **motion** — a wave of surface circulation passed from atom to atom, the way a compression wave passes down a line of touching dominoes without any single domino traveling the whole distance.

They ground this in ordinary battery chemistry. In a standard 1.5-volt battery, a chamber at the negative terminal (the anode) hosts a spontaneous reaction — zinc reacting with an alkaline electrolyte — which is conventionally described as "producing free electrons." In their material re-reading, this reaction instead drives the zinc atoms into a state of **excess surface motion** that has nowhere to go as long as the circuit is open. The moment a wire connects the terminals, that excess motion is channeled atom-to-atom down the wire until it reaches a reservoir of manganese dioxide waiting at the positive terminal (the cathode), which absorbs the incoming motion and is thereby "overdriven" enough to react in turn, completing the chemical cycle. Voltage, in this picture, becomes a measure of *how much excess motion is being produced at one terminal relative to how much can be absorbed at the other*; current becomes a measure of *how much surface motion is transferred per unit time* — which naturally explains why a thicker wire, with more atoms available to relay the motion, carries more current.

Crucially, they argue that the standard bookkeeping of chemistry — "zinc loses electrons," "manganese dioxide gains electrons" — is exactly that: bookkeeping. Because the two reaction chambers are separated by a membrane the charge can't cross directly, and because the gains and losses happen in neat discrete units, it has long seemed natural to imagine little electron particles hopping through the wire to balance the books. But if "an electron" is really shorthand for a specific quantity of surface motion, the same accounting works without needing any particle to physically travel anywhere.

## Where Magnetism Comes From

This is arguably the cleverest piece of the model, because it turns a genuine, long-standing mystery — *why are electricity and magnetism at right angles to each other?* — into what they present as a fairly direct geometric consequence of their electron picture.

Recall the toroidal circulation that gives the electron its spin-1/2 behavior: fiber flows both *longitudinally* (through the central vortex and back out, tracing the donut shape) and, simultaneously, has an *equatorial* component circulating around the outside. Ampère's right-hand rule — point your thumb along the direction of conventional current, and your curled fingers trace the surrounding magnetic field — has always worked perfectly as a mathematical description. Shiloh and Anastasia argue it also has a literal mechanical reading in their model: the *longitudinal* flow of atomic fiber through the toroidal vortex is what constitutes conventional current, while the *equatorial* rotation of that same fiber, orthogonal to the longitudinal flow, is what constitutes the surrounding magnetic field. Electricity and magnetism are perpendicular, in other words, because they correspond to two geometrically perpendicular components of the very same underlying spin-1/2 motion.

They extend this to explain why parallel current-carrying wires attract or repel. When two wires carry current in the *same* direction, the equatorial (magnetic) rotations of their atoms are aligned and co-rotating; the surfaces effectively mesh together like gears, drawing the wires closer. When the currents run in *opposite* directions, the equatorial rotations counter-rotate and clash, pushing the wires apart. They offer a hands-on demonstration for listeners: curl your right hand into a loose fist with your friend's, thumbs pointing the same way, and bring your loosely curled fingers together — they interlock naturally, modeling attraction. Point the thumbs in opposite directions and try the same thing, and the fingers can't mesh; they just push each other away, modeling repulsion.

Permanent magnets, in this picture, are simply materials where a manufacturer has used an external electromagnet to forcibly align a large population of atomic shells during production, and where the material (a "hard ferromagnet") is capable of holding that alignment afterward rather than relaxing back to a random, unmagnetized state. This also explains, in their telling, why heating or dropping a magnet destroys its magnetism — either action disrupts the physical alignment of the electron shells that was underpinning the effect.

## The Hardest Sell: Filaments and Action at a Distance

Even granting all of the above, one enormous problem remains: two atoms exert magnetic (or electric, or gravitational) influence on each other across genuinely empty space, with no obvious physical bridge connecting them. If the electron is just a pattern of motion confined to an atom's surface, how does that motion get transmitted to a distant atom it never touches?

Their answer leans on a real, well-established feature of quantum mechanics: the **radial distribution function (RDF)**. This function, derived from the Schrödinger wave equation, gives the probability of detecting an electron at any given distance from the nucleus. For hydrogen in its ground state, the RDF peaks at 53 picometers — matching the atomic radius measured experimentally via X-ray diffraction. But the RDF never actually reaches zero. However far out you go, there remains some nonzero, if vanishingly small, probability of detecting the electron.

Shiloh and Anastasia treat this as more than a mathematical curiosity — for their model, they argue it as an implication. If the electron really is fiber circulating on the atom's surface, and if it can, in principle, be detected at any distance no matter how small the probability, then some physical extension of that surface has to reach out to those distances too. This is where they introduce their most speculative and, by their own admission, hardest-to-swallow proposal: **filaments**.

They picture every atom as covered in thin, radially projecting extensions of the same atomic fiber that makes up its main surface — like a furry halo surrounding the smooth toroidal body. These filaments are proposed to grow sparser with distance, thinning out in a way that reconciles the RDF's exponential falloff with the different (inverse-square) falloff observed for electromagnetic and gravitational fields: the RDF, they suggest, tells you where the *bulk* of an atom's surface is most likely to be found, while field effects describe the specific spatial organization of these much fainter, longer-range filamentary extensions.

Functionally, the filaments are meant to be the actual physical mechanism doing the pushing and pulling that fields *describe* mathematically. When two current-carrying wires' equatorial rotations align, it's specifically their filaments — not some abstract field — that mesh and draw the wires together; when the rotations oppose, it's the filaments clashing and pushing the wires apart. In this view, a field isn't a thing at all — it's a map of what the filaments are doing. As Anastasia puts it, physics has become "too physically comfortable" treating fields as though they were themselves material, when a field, properly understood, only describes dynamics — "there's something happening underneath a field."

To their credit, the hosts are explicit that this is the single largest hypothetical leap in the entire framework. As Shiloh puts it, unlike the toroidal circulation (which he sees as compatible with ideas already circulating in physics, around Hopf fibrations and knot theory), "the filaments are a pretty big step into the unknown." They don't claim direct detection of filaments; the argument is closer to an inference to the best explanation — *something* has to physically carry force across space, and the filament hypothesis is offered as a concrete, testable-in-principle candidate rather than a settled discovery. Their case for taking it seriously rests less on direct evidence and more on unification: if the same filamentary mechanism can, downstream, account for gravity, light, magnetism, and electricity without requiring a fundamentally different kind of explanation for each, that consilience is itself evidence the model is on the right track — a "grand simplification," in their words, relative to a physics that has to keep bolting on new conceptual machinery for each new phenomenon it encounters.

They're also candid about the model's stranger implications. If filaments really connect every atom to its neighbors, then, as Shiloh notes half-jokingly, a room is "absolutely filled with filaments" — from the floor, the walls, every object in it — and yet we move through it freely. Their response is that this isn't actually a contradiction: it requires ongoing active effort to move through that resistance (Shiloh jokes that if he passed out, gravity's filamentary "pull" would simply drop him to the ground uncontested), and the filaments' influence is proposed to fall off very quickly with distance — thinning out fast enough that only very nearby, structured accumulations of atoms (like the ground beneath your feet) exert a practically noticeable effect.

## What's Established, What's Interpreted, and What's Hypothesized

It's worth being explicit about the different epistemic status of the pieces in play here, since the hosts themselves are careful to draw this distinction:

- **Experimentally established, and not in dispute:** the Stern-Gerlach split showing electron spin-1/2 behavior; the mathematics of the Schrödinger wave equation and the radial distribution function; the empirical relationship between current and magnetic fields (Ampère, Faraday, Maxwell); the extremely precise quantitative predictions of quantum electrodynamics.
- **The conventional interpretation of that data**, which Shiloh and Anastasia accept as data but contest as explanation: that electrons are free, point-like particles; that fields are the final, fundamental description of force, with no further material cause; that the RDF's nonzero tail at large distances is simply a probability curiosity rather than evidence of missing physical structure.
- **Their own proposed, explicitly hypothetical model:** atomic fiber as the atom's basic material substrate; the toroidal, involuting shell as the structure that gives rise to the electron's spin-1/2 behavior; charge as a rate of surface circulation; current as transmitted motion rather than transmitted particles; and filaments as the literal material bridge that carries force across apparently empty space.

They're forthright that large parts of this remain unfinished. They don't yet have a proposed internal structure for the nucleus, beyond an openness (prompted by a listener's question during the stream) to the idea that it might be some kind of knotted internal circulation rather than a separate object. They don't fully resolve how filaments behave under alternating current, where the direction of flow constantly reverses — a listener's question they explicitly defer, noting they need to first work through gravity, inertia, and mass using the filament concept before they can properly tackle how filaments carry the reversing signal that produces radiated light. And they're candid that a full accounting of real, chemically complex conductors — alloys, multiple orbital roles within a single lattice, and so on — is a project well beyond what a simplified single-atom-thick wire model can capture.

## The Bet They're Making

Underneath all of the specific mechanics — the torus, the fiber, the filaments — sits a single methodological wager: that a theory earns the right to be called an *explanation*, rather than merely a *description*, only if it identifies actual material bodies doing actual physical work. Shiloh states the stakes plainly: "the quantification of the forces has been an incredibly successful project… but the question of where the forces come from has been completely set aside and the forces are treated as fundamental. And we're saying no, the forces cannot be fundamental because you cannot have a force without some kind of material body to generate it."

Whether or not any individual piece of the model survives further scrutiny — the toroidal shell, the fiber, the filaments — the through-line of the project is a deliberate return to a style of question that, in their telling, mainstream physics largely stopped asking around a century ago: not just *what does this equation predict*, but *what, physically, is doing this*.
