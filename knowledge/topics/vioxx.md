# Vioxx (Rofecoxib)

> **Method note:** This document reconstructs the case against Vioxx's approval, marketing, and regulatory oversight as built by [[Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]] and a defined set of physicians, scientists, journalists, attorneys, and a whistleblower who made that case in their own primary work — journal articles, congressional testimony, litigation documents, and depositions. Unlike the [[AZT (Zidovudine)]] and [[Thimerosal]] documents in this knowledge base, the central factual claims here are not a minority scientific position: they were substantially validated by Merck's own 2011 federal guilty plea, FDA's post-withdrawal regulatory actions, and multiple peer-reviewed "Expression of Concern" notices from the *New England Journal of Medicine*. The editorial note at the end is correspondingly brief.

## Overview

Rofecoxib (Vioxx), a selective COX-2 inhibitor developed by [[Merck & Co.]], was approved by the FDA on May 21, 1999, for osteoarthritis and acute pain, and was withdrawn voluntarily by Merck on September 30, 2004, after the APPROVe clinical trial showed it roughly doubled the risk of heart attack and stroke after 18 months of continuous use. More than 80 million people worldwide had taken the drug by the time of withdrawal, and annual sales had exceeded $2.5 billion. It remains, by sales volume at the time, the largest prescription-drug withdrawal in U.S. history. The dispute this document reconstructs is not primarily about whether Vioxx carried excess cardiovascular risk — that point was conceded by Merck's own withdrawal and confirmed in court and regulatory records — but about *how early the signal was known*, *how it was represented in the scientific literature and to regulators*, and *what the episode reveals about the incentive structure* connecting FDA, industry funding, medical journals, and physician marketing. Kennedy and others treat Vioxx as the paradigmatic case study for the [[Regulatory Capture]] framework Kennedy has applied across his career, from his environmental litigation writing through *[[The Real Anthony Fauci]]* to his tenure as HHS Secretary.

---

## The Central Thesis (Shared Across Sources)

1. The biological mechanism by which COX-2 selective inhibition could cause cardiovascular harm was published and available *before* Vioxx reached the market, not discovered only afterward.
2. Merck's own pivotal 2000 safety trial (VIGOR) showed a large, statistically significant excess of heart attacks in the Vioxx arm, which the company and trial authors attributed to a protective effect of the comparator drug (naproxen) rather than a harmful effect of rofecoxib — an interpretation critics dispute.
3. Data relevant to that interpretation (three additional heart attacks, and broader adverse-event data) were not included in the trial's initial publication in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, despite being known to at least two authors months before publication.
4. FDA's internal safety reviewers identified cardiovascular concerns years before the 2004 withdrawal, but agency structure and culture — including a drug-review apparatus substantially funded by the industry it regulates — delayed or blocked regulatory action.
5. Physicians and researchers who raised concerns publicly faced documented efforts by Merck to characterize, track, and in some cases contact their institutions or employers.
6. The eventual scale of harm, by the most-cited independent epidemiological estimate, was in the tens of thousands of excess serious cardiac events and associated deaths in the U.S. alone.

## The Scientific Warning Signs Before Approval and Before Withdrawal

### Garret FitzGerald's early mechanistic prediction (1999)
Before Vioxx and the competing COX-2 drug Celebrex were even approved, pharmacologist [[Garret FitzGerald]] and colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania published "Systemic Biosynthesis of Prostacyclin by Cyclooxygenase (COX)-2" (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 1999), showing that selective COX-2 inhibition suppresses prostacyclin — a vasodilatory, platelet-inhibiting compound — without correspondingly suppressing thromboxane, a pro-clotting compound produced via the COX-1 pathway that these drugs largely spare. FitzGerald and colleagues explicitly flagged this asymmetry as a plausible route to a prothrombotic (clot-promoting) cardiovascular hazard, publishing further mechanistic elaboration in *NEJM* (2001, with Carlo Patrono) and *Science* (2002, with Yan Cheng et al.). This body of work is cited by nearly every other source in this document as evidence that the cardiovascular risk was biologically foreseeable from the outset, not an unpredictable surprise.

### The VIGOR trial (2000) and the "naproxen hypothesis"
Merck's Vioxx Gastrointestinal Outcomes Research (VIGOR) trial, led by Claire Bombardier and published in *NEJM* in November 2000, was designed to demonstrate rofecoxib's reduced gastrointestinal toxicity compared with the older NSAID naproxen. It succeeded on that primary endpoint — but also found a myocardial infarction rate roughly five times higher in the rofecoxib arm. Merck and the trial's academic authors publicly attributed this difference to a cardioprotective effect of naproxen (analogous to aspirin) rather than a harmful effect of rofecoxib. Critics — including Topol, Psaty, and Furberg — argued this explanation was neither the only nor the most parsimonious reading of the data, particularly once combined with FitzGerald's mechanistic work.

### The Mukherjee-Nissen-Topol meta-analysis (2001)
Debabrata Mukherjee, Steven Nissen, and Eric Topol (Cleveland Clinic) published a meta-analysis in *JAMA* (August 2001) pooling cardiovascular event data across the available COX-2 inhibitor trials, concluding the existing evidence raised a signal for increased cardiovascular thrombotic events with rofecoxib that warranted a dedicated safety trial — a call that went unheeded for three more years, until APPROVe.

### The APPROVe trial and withdrawal (2004)
The Adenomatous Polyp Prevention on Vioxx (APPROVe) trial, designed to test rofecoxib for colorectal-polyp prevention, was stopped early after a planned interim analysis showed a relative risk of major cardiovascular events of approximately 1.92 (roughly double) in patients taking Vioxx continuously for more than 18 months. Merck withdrew the drug from the global market on September 30, 2004, four days after being informed of the result — a rapidity Merck's then-CEO [[Raymond Gilmartin]] characterized in later congressional testimony as consistent with the company's institutional ethic; critics note the same rapidity raises the question of why equivalent action was not taken on the VIGOR signal four years earlier.

## Corporate Conduct: Internal Documents, Publication, and Marketing

### The NEJM "Expression of Concern" (2005–2006)
In December 2005, *NEJM* editors Gregory Curfman, Stephen Morrissey, and Jeffrey Drazen published a formal "Expression of Concern" regarding the 2000 VIGOR article, stating that three myocardial infarctions in the rofecoxib group had not been included in the data submitted to the journal. The editors stated they learned this was not merely a late-arriving data issue but, per a July 5, 2000 internal Merck memorandum obtained via subpoena in Vioxx litigation, at least two of the paper's authors knew of the additional heart attacks more than four months before publication — "ample time," in the editors' words, to have included them. The VIGOR authors, in a formal published response (Bombardier et al., *NEJM*, 2006), maintained they had followed the trial's pre-specified cutoff dates and that including the additional events would not have materially changed the reported relative risk. *NEJM* reaffirmed its concern in a follow-up editorial in March 2006, maintaining the omission made the article's safety conclusions misleading.

### The "hit list" of dissenting doctors
Testimony in *Peterson v. Merck Sharp & Dohme (Aust.) Pty Ltd* (Federal Court of Australia, 2010) — an Australian class-action proceeding — surfaced internal Merck emails from 1999 in which employees compiled a list of physicians critical of Vioxx or the company, with individual names annotated "neutralise," "neutralised," or "discredit." One email, read into the court record, stated staff might need to seek out and destroy such doctors "where they live" (professionally). Separately, [[James Fries]], a Stanford University professor of medicine, wrote directly to Merck CEO Raymond Gilmartin in October 2000 describing what he called damage-control intimidation directed at researchers who had criticized the drug, stating he was aware of the practice affecting at least eight clinical investigators and that he himself had been mildly threatened despite not having spoken or written publicly on the issue.

### Ghostwriting and the fabricated journal
Litigation discovery also revealed that Merck had commissioned ghostwritten manuscripts favorable to Vioxx for publication under outside academic authors' names, and had sponsored a publication called the *Australasian Journal of Bone and Joint Medicine* — published by Elsevier and formatted to resemble a peer-reviewed journal — that in fact republished reprints of studies favorable to Merck products without disclosing the sponsorship arrangement.

### The withheld Alzheimer's-trial mortality data
[[Bruce Psaty]] and Richard Kronmal (University of Washington), reviewing internal Merck documents obtained through Vioxx litigation, published "Reporting Mortality Findings in Trials of Rofecoxib for Alzheimer Disease or Cognitive Impairment" (*JAMA*, 2008). They found that Merck's own internal analysis in April 2001 identified a threefold increase in total mortality among Alzheimer's-trial patients taking rofecoxib compared with placebo (34 deaths among 1,069 rofecoxib patients versus 12 deaths among 1,075 placebo patients), and that this mortality finding was not provided to the FDA or made public in a timely fashion, and was not accurately reflected in the trials' eventual publications in *Neurology* (2004) and *Neuropsychopharmacology* (2005).

## The FDA Whistleblower: David Graham

[[David Graham]], associate director for science in the FDA's Office of Drug Safety, led an internal case-control study using Kaiser Permanente data examining cardiovascular risk among rofecoxib users. He has described in his own subsequent interviews and public statements that senior FDA management attempted to prevent his findings from being published and from being heard by Congress — including, he says, a senior FDA official contacting *The Lancet*'s editor to suggest, without prior contact with Graham himself, that his manuscript involved scientific misconduct, and other officials contacting Senator [[Chuck Grassley]]'s staff in an attempt to keep Graham from testifying.

On November 18, 2004, Graham testified before the U.S. Senate Committee on Finance, telling the committee that Vioxx represented a serious regulatory failure and that the FDA, in his assessment, was "incapable of protecting America" from a comparable future drug safety crisis, and that the agency and its Center for Drug Evaluation and Research were, in his words, broken. He additionally identified five other marketed drugs (Accutane, Bextra, Crestor, Meridia, and Serevent) he believed carried under-recognized risks — a list that drew immediate pushback from FDA leadership at the same hearing.

Graham's peer-reviewed study, published in *The Lancet* in early 2005 (after the delay described above), estimated that Vioxx use in the U.S. was associated with an estimated 88,000 to 140,000 excess cases of serious coronary heart disease, of which Graham estimated 30,000 to 55,000 were fatal, figures he has repeated in subsequent congressional and public appearances. Graham has stated in interviews that he faced professional marginalization at FDA following his testimony, describing being excluded from further drug-safety evaluation work.

---

## Congressional Oversight

The November 18, 2004 Senate Finance Committee hearing, chaired by Senator Grassley, took testimony from Graham, Merck CEO Raymond Gilmartin, and FDA officials including [[Sandra Kweder]], who disputed Graham's characterization of agency conduct and declined to endorse his assessment of the five additional drugs as an official agency finding. Grassley's office subsequently pursued further inquiry into FDA's internal culture regarding drug-safety dissent and into the alleged efforts to discourage Graham's testimony, consistent with Grassley's broader record of investigating whistleblower retaliation at federal health agencies.

## Academic and Physician Critics

### Eric Topol
Cardiologist, then chairman of the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine at the Cleveland Clinic. Along with Mukherjee and Nissen, Topol had flagged the cardiovascular signal in *JAMA* in 2001. Following the withdrawal, he published a *New York Times* op-ed, "Good Riddance to a Bad Drug" (October 2, 2004), an editorial in the *Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine*, and — most substantively — "Failing the Public Health — Rofecoxib, Merck, and the FDA" in *NEJM* (October 21, 2004), arguing that neither Merck nor the FDA fulfilled its responsibility to the public over the drug's five-and-a-half-year time on the market, that Merck prioritized its more than $2.5 billion in annual sales and over $100 million in direct-to-consumer advertising over conducting a dedicated cardiovascular safety trial, and that FDA had waited passively for data to accumulate for roughly two years after the first strong risk signals rather than compelling such a trial itself.

### Bruce Psaty and Curt Furberg
Psaty (University of Washington) and Furberg (Wake Forest University) — both cardiovascular epidemiologists with a research history examining conflicts of interest in drug-safety evaluation, including an earlier JAMA paper on the withdrawn statin cerivastatin (Baycol) — published "COX-2 Inhibitors — Lessons in Drug Safety" in *NEJM* (February 2005), a companion piece to APPROVe's formal publication, describing the VIGOR-era myocardial infarction signal (five times higher in the rofecoxib arm) as a warning that went insufficiently heeded, and arguing the episode raised broader questions about the integrity of the U.S. drug-safety system. Psaty testified before a Senate panel on Vioxx's risks in 2004, and Psaty and Kronmal's later JAMA article (2008, described above) extended the critique into the separate Alzheimer's-trial mortality data.

## Litigation and the Legal Record

### Ernst v. Merck & Co. (2005)
The first Vioxx product-liability case to reach trial, brought by [[Carol Ernst]] over the death of her husband Robert Ernst, a 59-year-old personal trainer who had taken Vioxx for roughly eight months. Plaintiff's attorney [[Mark Lanier]] argued at trial that Merck had known of cardiovascular risk well before adequately warning physicians and patients. On August 19, 2005, a Brazoria County, Texas jury found Merck liable and awarded $253.4 million ($24 million compensatory, $229 million punitive), an amount immediately subject to reduction under Texas's punitive-damages cap to approximately $26 million. Merck appealed; in 2008, a Texas appellate court reversed the verdict, finding insufficient evidence that Robert Ernst's specific cause of death (a cardiac arrhythmia attributed by the plaintiff's experts to an underlying Vioxx-induced blood clot) had been reliably established — a reversal on evidentiary/causation grounds specific to that decedent's cause of death, not a finding that Vioxx was safe.

### The broader litigation and 2007 settlement
Merck faced more than an estimated 50,000 Vioxx-related lawsuits at the litigation's peak. In November 2007, Merck agreed to a $4.85 billion global settlement to resolve the bulk of the U.S. personal-injury claims, without admitting liability.

### Peterson v. Merck Sharp & Dohme (Aust.) Pty Ltd (2010)
An Australian Federal Court class-action proceeding in which the "hit list" documents described above were introduced into evidence, and in which the court found — per the reported judgment — that the VIGOR article had not accurately represented safety data available to its authors at the time of review.

### United States v. Merck, Sharp & Dohme (2011–2012)
The U.S. Department of Justice announced in November 2011 that Merck would plead guilty to a single misdemeanor count under the Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act for introducing a misbranded drug into interstate commerce, tied to Merck's promotion of Vioxx for rheumatoid arthritis before that indication was FDA-approved (1999–2002), and would pay $950 million in total fines and civil settlement funds: a $321.636 million criminal fine and a $628.364 million civil settlement resolving allegations that Merck made false or misleading statements about Vioxx's cardiovascular safety and to state Medicaid programs. Merck formally entered its guilty plea in December 2011 and was sentenced in March 2012 by U.S. District Judge Patti Saris. Merck's public statements maintained the civil settlement did not constitute an admission of liability, and noted the government's acknowledgment that there was no basis to conclude Merck's senior management had directed the underlying violations.

## Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s Regulatory-Capture Framework and Vioxx

Kennedy's own primary writing on FDA structure — developed most fully in *The Real Anthony Fauci* (2021) — centers on the [[PDUFA]] (Prescription Drug User Fee Act) funding mechanism, under which pharmaceutical companies pay fees to FDA each time they submit a drug for approval; Kennedy states in the book that this funding stream constitutes 45 percent of the FDA's budget, and treats it as the structural mechanism by which the agency's incentives are aligned with industry approval timelines rather than public safety. Kennedy and allied writers (for example, a Brownstone Institute essay on regulatory capture explicitly addressed to Kennedy's HHS agenda) have since used Vioxx as the paradigm case for this argument: FDA approved the drug in 1999, is described as having failed to withdraw it despite internal warning signs, and Merck — not the agency — ultimately pulled the drug from the market.

As HHS Secretary, Kennedy has carried this framework directly into agency policy, telling FDA staff in an April 2025 address that the agency had at points become what he called a "sock puppet" for the industries it regulates, and pushing to reform the PDUFA user-fee structure — the same mechanism his book identifies as central to the Vioxx-era capture argument. (Reporting on the practical effects of Kennedy's associated FDA staffing cuts, including on the office responsible for negotiating PDUFA renewal, falls outside this document's primary-source scope and is addressed in the [[Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]] profile's HHS Secretary Tenure section.)

Notably, PDUFA itself was created in 1992 substantially in response to a *different* drug-approval-speed crisis — delays in reviewing the first antiretroviral drugs for HIV/AIDS, including [[AZT (Zidovudine)]] — meaning the funding mechanism Kennedy identifies as enabling the Vioxx failure was originally built to solve the opposite problem (drugs reaching patients too slowly) that dominates the earlier AZT-era chapters of his own book. This tension — between faster-approval and safer-approval as competing regulatory goods — recurs across Kennedy's treatment of both cases.

## Recurring Historical Patterns

- **The safety-signal-to-reframing pattern**: a trial shows a concerning signal (VIGOR's MI rate) → the sponsor offers an alternative causal explanation (naproxen cardioprotection) → publication proceeds without the most complete adverse-event data → independent researchers (FitzGerald, Topol, Psaty, Furberg) continue raising concern in the literature for years → a dedicated trial (APPROVe) eventually confirms the risk → withdrawal follows rapidly once confirmed. Kennedy and allied critics treat this same sequence as a template visible in other drug-safety episodes, including cerivastatin/Baycol (the subject of Psaty and Furberg's earlier JAMA conflict-of-interest paper) and, in Kennedy's broader body of work, AZT (see [[AZT (Zidovudine)]]).
- **Journal-industry entanglement**: the VIGOR "Expression of Concern," the ghostwriting revelations, and the Elsevier-published sponsored journal are treated by these sources as evidence of a broader structural vulnerability in how clinical evidence reaches physicians, a theme Kennedy generalizes into his critique of medical-journal and CME funding elsewhere in his body of work.
- **Whistleblower marginalization**: Graham's account of post-testimony professional isolation is treated by Kennedy and other critics as continuous with the pattern Kennedy describes for other dissenting scientists across his body of work (see [[Peter Duesberg]] and [[Kary Mullis]] in [[AZT (Zidovudine)]], and the Simpsonwood-era account of Thomas Verstraeten in [[Thimerosal]]) — professional and reputational cost as a recurring feature of institutional dissent, regardless of whether the dissent was later vindicated (as with Graham) or remains scientifically rejected (as with Duesberg).
- **Regulatory-capture funding mechanisms**: PDUFA user fees are the specific funding-structure argument Kennedy applies to Vioxx, paralleling his treatment of CDC's Vaccine Safety Datalink funding and NIAID's grant-and-patent structure elsewhere in this knowledge base.

## Editorial Note on Scientific and Legal Status

*(Included per this knowledge base's general practice — see [[Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]], [[AZT (Zidovudine)]], and [[Thimerosal]] for the same approach.)*

Unlike the other pharmaceutical-controversy documents in this knowledge base, Vioxx is not a case where the central factual claims remain a minority or scientifically rejected position. The core critique reconstructed above — that Vioxx carried excess cardiovascular risk, that this risk was foreseeable and partly foreseen before and shortly after approval, that relevant trial data were incompletely represented in the initial VIGOR publication, and that Merck's marketing conduct included efforts to track and discredit critical physicians — is supported by Merck's own 2011 federal guilty plea, the *NEJM* editors' own published findings, and extensive court-supervised discovery across multiple jurisdictions. Genuine remaining areas of dispute include: the precise scale of excess deaths attributable to Vioxx (independent estimates, including Graham's, range roughly from 30,000 to over 55,000 U.S. deaths, a range some FDA-affiliated and industry-affiliated statisticians have argued is imprecise given the observational methods used to generate it); whether cardiovascular risk was present from the start of use or concentrated after roughly 18 months of continuous use, as APPROVe's design assumed; and how much of the failure should be attributed to Merck's specific conduct versus the structural, industry-funded design of the FDA review process that Kennedy and others argue is a general vulnerability rather than a Vioxx-specific one. The 2008 reversal of the Ernst verdict on appeal is included above because it is part of the primary legal record; it reflects a Texas court's finding about the sufficiency of causation evidence for one specific decedent, not a broader finding disputing Vioxx's cardiovascular risk profile, which by 2008 was no longer contested by Merck.
