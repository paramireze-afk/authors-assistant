## 1. Ghalibaf: The U.S. Must Be "Punished" Before It Accepts Iran's Terms

The lead story centers on Iranian Parliament Speaker **Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf**, one of the central figures in Iran's indirect negotiations with Washington. In a televised speech on Wednesday, he said the United States must be punished in order for it to accept Iran's demands for ending the war. **⚑1**

The remarks came after Iran received Washington's latest response in the indirect talks. The head of Iran's Supreme National Security Council called that response inadequate because the U.S. didn't agree to all of Tehran's conditions.

According to Iran's Press TV, Ghalibaf said:

> "The United States must be punished, change its behavior, and be made to understand unequivocally that it must accept the legitimate demands of the Iranian nation."

### What Iran is actually asking for

Iran's position, as DeCamp has been covering it, is that the U.S. must first fulfill the obligations it agreed to in the **memorandum of understanding (MOU) signed in June**. Those include:

- An end to the U.S. blockade of Iran
- Sanctions waivers
- The release of some frozen Iranian funds
- A genuine ceasefire in Lebanon

Only after those steps, in Iran's view, should negotiations on the nuclear issue and other matters begin. Ghalibaf said that because he didn't trust the U.S., he made sure the MOU had two parts. DeCamp's reading: the first part consists of the U.S. delivering those measures and Iran fully opening or facilitating traffic through the **Strait of Hormuz**; only then would the two sides move on to negotiate a final nuclear deal.

### "Negotiation and fighting"

Ghalibaf also said Iran must be prepared for both negotiations and fighting, with the goal of establishing deterrence against future attacks:

> "On the battlefield before God, we must believe that we should fight courageously against our coercive enemy while also engaging in negotiation to establish lasting deterrence."

### The Iran–Iraq War as a reference point

What DeCamp found most revealing was Ghalibaf's invocation of the **Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988)**, suggesting Iran is preparing for the possibility of a very long conflict. He said:

> "In the eight years of sacred defense we fought against Saddam's Ba'athist regime. Of course, we paid a price. We suffered hardships, but in the end, for several decades, no one dared or had the desire to attack our beloved country, Iran."

The implication, as DeCamp reads it, is that Iran may need to endure something similar again to re-establish deterrence for another few decades.

DeCamp contrasts this with the American time horizon. In the U.S., people ask whether the war will last past the midterms. DeCamp's own expectation is that it will continue in some form at least until the end of Trump's presidency, though he says he hopes he's wrong. **⚑2** Iran, after decades of sanctions and pressure, likely operates with a much longer-term mindset.

**Background worth knowing:**
- During the Iran–Iraq War, the U.S. backed Saddam Hussein, providing intelligence and other support while knowing he was using chemical weapons against Iran. **⚑3**
- Ghalibaf fought in that war himself as a member of the IRGC, rising through the ranks to become a senior combat commander who took part in major battles. The fighting was trench-style warfare reminiscent of World War I, so this is a man who has seen serious combat and is closely involved in Iran's decision-making on the current war.

## 2. Netanyahu: It's "Not Convenient" to Attack Iran Before Elections

Asked on Israel's Army Radio about the possibility of striking Iran before the **Knesset elections on October 27**, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said it was "not convenient for us" and "no good" — effectively saying no. Trump has said something similar.

DeCamp's reaction was sardonic: these are two leaders with records of misleading the public about war, so their statements should be weighed accordingly. He points to Israeli media reporting on **deception campaigns** that both the U.S. and Israel ran ahead of the June 2025 war and the current conflict, which included strategic leaks and staged appearances of disagreement between Washington and Tel Aviv. **⚑4**

### The Ravid reporting

DeCamp also revisited reporting from **Barak Ravid of Axios**. Before the current war, Ravid published stories suggesting a deal might be close and that Netanyahu and Trump were at odds — but he was also reporting that the U.S. and Israel were planning a major war, and that eight days before launching it they were considering assassinating Iran's Supreme Leader. Both strands turned out to contain truth.

So when Ravid now reports that another round of attacks is under consideration, DeCamp thinks it deserves attention. At the same time, he argues Ravid essentially publishes what U.S. and Israeli officials want published, so the current "will they or won't they" coverage may itself be part of an information war designed to keep Iran guessing. **⚑5**

### What an attack would mean for the election

If the U.S. and Israel did strike Iran, Iran would likely fire large numbers of missiles at Israel. If that happened close to the vote, it could even lead to the elections being postponed.

DeCamp's overall assessment: he can't say whether an attack comes before or after the U.S. midterms, but all signs point toward escalation. He doesn't believe Trump will accept the conditions Iran is holding to, and he doesn't believe Trump will withdraw from the region either. **⚑2**

## 3. Houthis Warn of More Attacks on Saudi Oil Facilities

Ansarallah (the Houthis) warned on Wednesday that more attacks on Saudi oil facilities are coming, following a strike on the headquarters of state oil giant **Saudi Aramco in Dhahran**, in eastern Saudi Arabia just a few miles from the Persian Gulf and deep inside the country. There were no reported casualties, but many observers saw the strike as a demonstration of reach.

Military spokesman **Yahya Saree** said attacks also targeted **King Khalid International Airport** in Riyadh again, as well as an oil refinery in Riyadh. These came after Saudi Arabia bombed oil infrastructure in Yemen. In a Telegram statement, Saree said:

> "The Yemeni armed forces affirm that their operations towards Saudi depths will continue in the coming days targeting Saudi enemy airports and oil facilities, and their consequences will be severe for the enemy by the permission and power of God."

Saree renewed the evacuation warning for Saudi airports and said Ansarallah would continue its "siege for siege" policy against Saudi Arabia.

### On the Yemeni side

- Yemen's Saba news agency reported that at least one woman was killed and four other civilians wounded by Saudi airstrikes in a district of **Taiz province** in the southwest.
- Ansarallah controls nearly all of Taiz province except Taiz city itself, which remains under siege with ground fighting ongoing. DeCamp says Ansarallah appears close to taking it.
- Saree said there had been **42 Saudi airstrikes and missile attacks** in the previous 24 hours.

DeCamp also notes the U.S. is supporting the Saudi air campaign. **⚑6**

## 4. Israel Has Killed More Than 1,500 Gazans Since the "Ceasefire"

The Palestinian death toll in Gaza since the ceasefire deal has now passed **1,500** — DeCamp calls it a grim reality, and says plainly that this is no ceasefire. **⚑7**

### The numbers

- Gaza's Health Ministry said Wednesday that Israeli attacks killed two Palestinians in the previous 24 hours, and a third died of earlier wounds, bringing the post-ceasefire death toll to just over 1,500 (see transcript note below).
- **5,323** Palestinians have been wounded in the same period.

### The Israeli side

By official IDF figures, **seven soldiers** have been killed in Gaza since the deal:

- **Four** were killed in what the IDF called combat in **October 2025**, the month the deal was signed.
- **Two** were killed last month in what the IDF described as an accident involving an Israeli military drone.
- **One** was killed in February 2026 by other Israeli soldiers.

That means no Israeli has been killed by an attack from Gaza since October 2025. Hamas has launched no rockets at Israel, yet Israel continues daily strikes while Gazans live in tents and blown-out buildings that can collapse on them — even though, according to President Trump, Hamas has agreed to a deal to give up its weapons.

### The "means to an end" argument

DeCamp connects this to a piece he discussed the day before about a new Israeli military doctrine, in particular its treatment of peace agreements. His interpretation: the Gaza agreement wasn't an end but a means to an end — a way to continue occupying part of Gaza, keep up demolitions and daily strikes, and keep killing Palestinians while facing far less international pressure than before the deal. **⚑8**

### Wednesday's attacks

- A woman was killed in a bombing in **Gaza City** in front of her three-year-old daughter. A local journalist heard the child screaming, went in, and pulled her out of the fire; there is video of the rescue. Her father had reportedly been killed earlier, leaving her orphaned.
- Gunfire killed at least one Palestinian in northern Gaza, and there were also attacks in the south.

DeCamp says he repeats these points often because of how little attention the situation receives.

## 5. Israeli Defense Minister Threatens Lebanon's Shi'ite Community

*Story by Jason Ditz.*

Israeli Defense Minister **Israel Katz** warned that if the war with Iran resumes and Lebanon joins in, Israel will take more territory, with the result being the destruction of Lebanon's Shi'ite community. He said **a million Shi'ites** would be displaced and lose their homes. **⚑9**

DeCamp notes that Katz, a member of Netanyahu's Likud party, draws less attention for such statements than Bezalel Smotrich or Itamar Ben Gvir — though, he adds, even their statements get little coverage in mainstream U.S. media. Katz has previously said Shi'ite villages need to disappear.

According to **New York Times** reporting based on satellite imagery, at least **48 villages in southern Lebanon** have been completely destroyed.

## 7. Heavy Russian Strikes Hit Ukraine's Power Plants

Russian strikes overnight Tuesday into Wednesday killed at least nine people across Ukraine and left hundreds of thousands without power after multiple power plants were hit. An apartment building in **Kyiv** was struck during the barrage, killing at least two civilians. Russia's Defense Ministry announced strikes on power stations in Kyiv and **Kharkiv**.

### The "energy ceasefire" that wasn't

This comes despite President Trump announcing days earlier that the two sides had reached an energy ceasefire. Both Russia and Ukraine said at the time there was no such agreement. Axios reported that the U.S. proposal covered only attacks on **oil refineries**, not power plants, and that Russia never agreed to it.

For several days Ukraine did appear to hold off on Russian refineries. But on Wednesday, a Russian deputy prime minister accused Ukraine of targeting refineries in **Saratov** and **Ryazan**. Ukraine denies it, and DeCamp says he hasn't seen evidence that drones actually struck the refineries.

### Ukraine's drone barrage

- At least one civilian was killed in the Saratov region.
- Russia's Defense Ministry said **92 Ukrainian drones** were shot down over the Black Sea and multiple Russian regions.
- Moscow-region authorities said **493 drones** were launched toward the Moscow oblast, with **180** downed over or near Moscow itself.

### Starlink

Ukrainians report that their **Starlink** service, used for drone operations (likely not the long-range ones), was throttled. Elon Musk said decisions restricting the speed or use of **Starshield** — the government-controlled version of the service — are made by the U.S. military and ultimately the president, not SpaceX. DeCamp says the real story remains unclear. **⚑10**

His bottom line: despite all the diplomatic back-and-forth, nothing about the war has effectively changed.

## 8. Black Sea Shipping Hit With Escalating Attacks

*Story by John Weekes.*

Drone attacks on ships in the Black Sea have increased sharply. On Tuesday, the Guinea-flagged cargo ship **Dignity** was targeted and the Ukrainian-operated, Malta-flagged coastal freighter **Chelsea** was disabled — both roughly **20 miles off the Bulgarian coast**. Last week, a Togo-flagged cargo ship was sunk near Bulgaria.

### The attribution question **⚑11**

- Bulgaria's president blamed Russia for the deteriorating security situation but did not formally blame it for the sinkings.
- Bulgaria's prime minister summoned the Russian ambassador and told her it would make no sense for Ukraine to destroy its own ships. The ambassador replied that it could have been a third party — in effect, Russia is denying responsibility for these attacks.
- Russia *has* claimed other ship attacks, including one Wednesday on a vessel it said was supplying the Ukrainian military. DeCamp speculates Russia may be avoiding credit for the ones near Bulgaria.
- Ukraine has also been hitting more ships in the Black Sea, including a tanker shown fully ablaze off **Sochi**.

DeCamp observes that this escalation seems to run in tandem with attacks around the Strait of Hormuz: nearly every day now brings headlines of commercial ships being attacked somewhere in the world.

## 10. Trump: Somaliland Is Its Own Country — But Should Be "Somalia Land"

*Via Middle East Eye.*

At a rally Monday, Trump said he considers **Somaliland** a separate country, though he'd prefer it be called "Somalia land":

> "I call it Somalia land not Somaliland because actually it's totally different country. Some people like Somaliland. That has nothing to do. I said, 'You got to be kidding. I think they should change the name immediately.'"

It's unclear whether this signals any change in U.S. policy. DeCamp doubts it means much for formal recognition.

### Context

- **Israel** recently became the only country to recognize Somaliland. DeCamp argues this serves Israeli interests, including a foothold from which to attack Yemen, and the U.S. may have similar interests. **⚑12**
- The U.S. is establishing a military base in **Bosaso**, in Puntland, just east of Somaliland.
- But the U.S. still strongly backs Somalia's **federal government**. U.S. airstrikes against al-Shabaab are technically on its behalf; strikes on the ISIS affiliate in Puntland are coordinated with Puntland's local government.

### Somalia's internal crisis

Several of Somalia's member states have withdrawn from the federal system over disputes with the central government, including the president's constitutional changes and his push for universal suffrage in place of the clan-based voting system. Unlike Somaliland, these states haven't seceded — they favor strong federalism and regional autonomy within one Somalia. Puntland also has territorial disputes with Somaliland.

So the picture isn't as simple as the U.S. following Israel's lead on recognition. The U.S. is still, at least formally, propping up a foreign-backed federal government struggling to impose central control. Recently **Turkey** launched airstrikes around **Baidoa**, entering the fighting between the federal government and one of the states — a significant escalation of foreign involvement in Somalia's internal conflicts.
