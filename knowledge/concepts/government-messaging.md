# When Can the U.S. Government Legally Deceive, Propagandize, or Withhold Information From the American Public?

*A research memorandum on statutes, doctrines, court decisions, and documented controversies*

---

## How to read this memo

Government information-control law is not one body of doctrine — it's a patchwork of appropriations riders, intelligence statutes, military doctrine, evidentiary privileges, and First Amendment case law, each governing a different actor and audience. Throughout, three categories are kept distinct:

- **Affirmatively authorized** — a statute, order, or holding expressly permits the conduct.
- **Not prohibited** — no law forbids it, but nothing affirmatively blesses it either; legality rests on absence of a rule.
- **Contested gray zone** — agencies have relied on categories (foreign vs. domestic, "coordination" vs. "coercion," public affairs vs. information operations) that critics argue have been stretched past their intended scope, sometimes confirmed by GAO or inspector-general findings, sometimes not.

---

## 1. Foreign-audience public diplomacy: the Smith–Mundt framework

The core statute is the **U.S. Information and Educational Exchange Act of 1948** (Smith–Mundt Act, 22 U.S.C. § 1461 et seq.), passed to let the State Department and its successor broadcasters (Voice of America, and now the U.S. Agency for Global Media) produce material *for foreign audiences* while addressing Cold War fears that the executive branch would turn those tools on Americans. For over sixty years the Act — reinforced by a 1972 amendment and Section 208 of the Foreign Relations Authorization Act, Fiscal Years 1986–1987 — operated as a *de facto domestic dissemination ban*: government-made public-diplomacy content could be broadcast abroad but not legally distributed inside the United States.[1][2]

**2013 — the Smith–Mundt Modernization Act.** Congress repealed the domestic dissemination ban, allowing State Department and USAGM foreign-facing content to be made available to U.S. audiences on request and, later, online.[1][3] Proponents (including the ACLU) framed this as a First Amendment and transparency improvement — Americans could now access material their tax dollars funded, and government could rebut extremist propaganda reaching the U.S. via the internet.[5] Critics said it removed a structural safeguard and effectively **authorized the domestic footprint of government-made messaging** for the first time since 1948.[2]

**What the law still forbids.** The 2013 change was not a blanket permission. It remains illegal for State/USAGM to use these materials *to target Americans* with advertising or persuasion campaigns; the repeal only removed the *access* ban, not a prohibition on domestically directed propagandizing.[4] Congress has revisited this repeatedly — including a **2025 bill to repeal the Modernization Act** and a 2025 **"SPIN Act"** (Stopping Propaganda Indoctrination Nationally) reintroducing an explicit domestic-targeting prohibition — evidence that lawmakers themselves consider the current line ambiguous.[1][8]

**Documented gray-zone controversy.** Researchers Kim Andrew Elliott/Sager and, more recently, a 2025 analysis by researcher Nick Sager (unrelated) found that USAGM materials had, at points, reached domestic audiences in ways that "potentially violated the Smith-Mundt Act's prohibition on domestic propaganda targeting," prompting internal reforms whose effectiveness is difficult to verify from the outside.[4] This is a clean example of the "not prohibited vs. contested" line: dissemination is *authorized*; deliberate domestic *targeting* is still *banned*, but proving the difference in practice — an ad that "spills over" versus one engineered to reach Americans — is where critics say the statute has been tested.

## 2. Covert action: the National Security Act framework

**Statute.** 50 U.S.C. § 3093 (National Security Act of 1947, as amended, formerly 50 U.S.C. § 413b) defines **covert action** as "an activity or activities of the United States Government to influence political, economic, or military conditions abroad, where it is intended that the role of the United States Government will not be apparent or acknowledged publicly."[44][45] Key features:

- The President must issue a **written finding** that the action is necessary to support identifiable U.S. foreign-policy objectives and is important to national security.[45]
- Congressional intelligence committees must be notified, with limited exceptions for restricted "Gang of Eight" notification in extraordinary circumstances.[38][42]
- Critically, **§ 3093(f)/(a)(5) expressly bars any covert action "intended to influence United States political processes, public opinion, policies, or media."**[38][41] This is the single clearest statutory line in the entire body of law: covert action *aimed at Americans* is illegal, full stop — Congress drew the foreign/domestic distinction directly into the covert-action statute itself.
- The definition **excludes** several categories from "covert action" coverage, notably traditional diplomatic, military, and law-enforcement activities and "traditional... intelligence collection." This carve-out is where much of the gray zone lives: an activity that looks like influence operations can be reclassified as "traditional military activity" or program support and thereby escape the covert-action finding/notification regime altogether.[40][43]

**Executive Order 12333** (Reagan, 1981, since amended) implements and constrains this framework for the Intelligence Community. It restricts CIA collection on U.S. persons, bars assassination, and — per its historical purpose after the Church Committee — was intended to prevent intelligence agencies from using their foreign-intelligence authorities to conduct **domestic political or media influence activity**.[26][30] The order does not itself contain a single "no domestic propaganda" clause as clean as § 3093(f); its domestic-protection function comes from a web of provisions restricting collection on U.S. persons and requiring Attorney General-approved guidelines (12333 §2.3 procedures) before any activity that could sweep in Americans.[28]

**Case study — ZunZuneo (2009–2012).** USAID, through contractor Creative Associates and shell companies routed through foreign banks, built a Twitter-like SMS platform for Cuban users, intending — per USAID's own planning documents — to build a subscriber base that could later be used to organize political dissent and "renegotiate the balance of power between the state and society."[63][68] Because Cuba is a *foreign* audience, this sits outside the domestic-propaganda prohibitions above; the live legal question was whether it constituted unnoticed **covert action** requiring a presidential finding. USAID and the White House insisted the program was "discreet," not "covert," and pointed to a 2013 GAO review that found no legal violation; independent reporting noted GAO's report did not actually examine ZunZuneo and that "it is unclear whether the scheme was legal under U.S. law, which requires written authorization of covert action by the president and congressional notification."[62][65][67] A subsequent **USAID Inspector General review** found no evidence of fraud but documented that the program's funding-concealment methods "pose inherent risks" and devoted a full appendix to weighing the program against the statutory covert-action definition without reaching a conclusion.[60] This is a paradigm case of the second category above — an agency operating in an area the law does not clearly forbid, defended as legal, but never squarely adjudicated.

## 3. Military information operations and PSYOP/MISO doctrine

Department of Defense doctrine — Joint Publication 3-13 (*Information Operations*), JP 3-13.2/3-53 (*Military Information Support Operations*, the renamed successor to "psychological operations") — governs DoD's influence and deception activities. Two structural rules matter most:

- **Foreign-audience limitation.** MISO is doctrinally defined as operations that "convey selected information and indicators to **foreign** audiences to influence their emotions, motives, objective reasoning, and ultimately the behavior of foreign governments, organizations, groups, and individuals."[49] MISO doctrine explicitly separates itself from **Public Affairs (PA)**, the function responsible for truthful, attributed communication with domestic and international media, and states that MISO products are not to be directed at the U.S. public.[51]
- **Covert Action Statute cross-reference.** DoD's own MISO instruction states plainly: *"MISO and the Covert Action Statute. This instruction does not authorize the [combatant commander] to conduct or provide support to covert action. All MISO conducted by DOD should be in a manner consistent with 50 USC § 413b."*[50] In other words, DoD's military-deception and influence authorities are explicitly bounded by the same covert-action statute discussed in Part 2 — a formal acknowledgment that military information activities can slide into covert action if they cross certain lines (domestic-audience influence, unacknowledged U.S. role, political-process effects).

**Military deception (MILDEC)** is a separate, narrower doctrinal category — battlefield deception of *adversary* military decision-makers (false radio traffic, decoys, etc.) — governed by the law of armed conflict's ruses-of-war doctrine, which is lawful so long as it does not involve perfidy (e.g., misuse of protected emblems) and is not directed at the U.S. public.

**Case study — the Pentagon military analyst program (2002–2008).** A Pulitzer Prize–winning *New York Times* investigation by David Barstow, based on 8,000 pages obtained via a two-year FOIA lawsuit, revealed that the Pentagon's Office of Public Affairs recruited more than 75 retired military officers who appeared on television as independent "military analysts."[19][20] Internal documents repeatedly called them **"message force multipliers"** and "surrogates" who could deliver administration talking points "in the form of their own opinions," while many had undisclosed financial ties to defense contractors.[18][19] Then-CBS News president Andrew Heyward called it "a deliberate attempt to deceive the public."[23] Congress requested GAO and DoD Inspector General investigations; the **DoD IG's 2009 report, after a two-year inquiry, concluded the program complied with existing DoD regulations and directives** — i.e., nothing in the program technically violated a written rule, even though its purpose and effect (covertly shaping ostensibly independent television commentary) was what the "publicity or propaganda" and covert-propaganda doctrines discussed in Part 4 exist to prevent.[17][20] This is the paradigm "not prohibited" case: the activity used the Public Affairs authority (talking to media, arranging trips, giving briefings) rather than a Title 10 MISO or Title 50 covert-action authority, so none of the specific statutory bars applied, even though critics — including a former NBC analyst who called it "psyops on steroids" — argued the program was functionally domestic influence operations run through an intermediary category (PA) not built for message control of this kind.[18]

## 4. Domestic "publicity or propaganda": appropriations law and GAO enforcement

Separate from Smith–Mundt and covert action is a recurring **appropriations rider**, reenacted annually in some form since the 1950s, currently phrased (e.g., in the Financial Services and General Government Appropriations Act): *"No part of any appropriation... shall be used directly or indirectly, including by private contractor, for publicity or propaganda purposes within the United States not heretofore authorized by Congress."*[14] GAO (via its Comptroller General decisions, which function as authoritative — though not judicially binding — interpretations of appropriations law) has built a three-part doctrine under this rider, barring the use of federal funds for:

1. **Covert propaganda** — communications that fail to disclose the government's role as the source;
2. **Self-aggrandizement** — communications that primarily promote the agency or officials rather than inform the public;
3. **Purely partisan activity.**[10][14]

Violations also typically trigger the **Antideficiency Act** (31 U.S.C. § 1341), because an agency that spends money on something Congress banned has, by definition, spent money it wasn't appropriated.[10]

**Confirmed violations (GAO decisions, not court rulings — these are administrative/legal opinions binding as a matter of appropriations practice but not self-executing court judgments):**

- **Armstrong Williams / Department of Education (2005).** GAO found the Department violated the propaganda prohibition by paying commentator Armstrong Williams $240,000 to promote the No Child Left Behind Act on his TV show and in his syndicated column without disclosing the financial relationship — "covert propaganda," and a related Antideficiency Act violation.[10][11][12]
- **Karen Ryan video news releases / HHS (2004) and ONDCP "fake news" segments (2005).** GAO found that government-produced video segments made to look like independent news broadcasts — including one where an actor posed as a reporter — were covert propaganda because "the failure of an agency to identify itself as a source of a prepackaged news story misleads the viewing public."[13] The Department of Education's Inspector General had earlier reviewed a related contract and found no legal violation — GAO's subsequent opinion directly contradicted the agency's own IG, illustrating how contested the boundary can be even between oversight bodies.[11]
- **EPA "Thunderclap" social media campaign (2015).** GAO found EPA's use of a crowd-amplification social media tool to promote a rule constituted covert propaganda, while parallel EPA hashtag campaigns (#DitchTheMyth) did **not** violate the rule because EPA's role was disclosed — showing that *attribution*, not persuasion itself, is the dispositive legal test.[14]

**What this body of law actually forbids** is narrower than "propaganda" in the colloquial sense: agencies may **advocate for their own policies and programs** (this is affirmatively authorized government speech, see Part 6) as long as the government's role is disclosed. What's illegal is *concealment of the source* — turning private or ostensibly independent voices into unattributed vehicles for the message. This is the doctrinal thread that also runs through the Pentagon analyst controversy above, though GAO's own 2009 review of that program (at Congress's request) reportedly reached more limited findings than the DoD IG review, underscoring how fact-intensive "covert propaganda" determinations are.

## 5. Law enforcement deception

Unlike the intelligence/military/public-diplomacy statutes above, deception by domestic law enforcement toward individual suspects is governed almost entirely by **constitutional criminal-procedure doctrine**, not a propaganda statute, because the deception is directed at a specific person in custody, not "the public."

**Frazier v. Cupp, 394 U.S. 731 (1969).** Police falsely told a murder suspect that his cousin/co-suspect had already confessed and implicated him. The Supreme Court held that police misrepresentation of evidence, while "relevant," is **not by itself enough to make an otherwise voluntary confession inadmissible**; voluntariness is assessed under a **"totality of the circumstances"** test.[31][33] This established that lying about the existence of evidence — a "false evidence ploy" — is a **permitted** interrogation tactic.[37]

**The outer limit is voluntariness**, rooted in the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments: deception that is coercive enough to overbear a suspect's free will (threats, promises of leniency tied to false facts, misrepresenting the suspect's *legal rights* rather than the evidence) can still render a confession involuntary and inadmissible.[34][35] *Miranda v. Arizona* and *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428 (2000) (holding Miranda a constitutional rule Congress cannot override by statute), constrain a different piece of the same interaction — the requirement to warn a suspect of rights — but do not prohibit deception about facts once warnings are given.[34]

Separately, **entrapment doctrine** (a defense, not a constitutional rule) limits — without banning — government deception used to induce someone to commit a crime they were not otherwise predisposed to commit; it does not restrict deception used for investigation or evidence-gathering generally. Undercover operations, confidential informants, and sting operations are affirmatively lawful investigative tools under this framework, subject to Fourth/Fifth Amendment and entrapment limits rather than any anti-deception statute.

## 6. Government speech, compelled attribution, and the First Amendment ceiling

A separate doctrinal track determines when the government's own persuasive speech is even subject to First Amendment scrutiny at all.

**The government speech doctrine.** In **Johanns v. Livestock Marketing Association, 544 U.S. 550 (2005)**, the Supreme Court held that when the government sets the message and approves its content, that speech is "government speech" and is **exempt from ordinary First Amendment scrutiny**, even when funded by a mandatory assessment on people who disagree with it — "Beef. It's What's for Dinner." was held to be the government's own speech, immune from a compelled-speech challenge.[52][55] This doctrine gives agencies broad affirmative authority to fund and disseminate their own persuasive messaging, including through private intermediaries, "merely because it solicits assistance from nongovernmental sources in developing specific messages," so long as the government approves the final content.[52]

**Limits on the doctrine.** *Legal Services Corporation v. Velazquez*, 531 U.S. 533 (2001), refused to extend Rust-style government-speech reasoning to a case where the government tried to restrict what independently funded private lawyers could argue in court — a reminder that government speech doctrine covers the government speaking as itself, not government control over private speakers' independent expression.[59] Justice Souter's dissents in the government-speech line (Johanns and progeny) have repeatedly warned that the doctrine, unconstrained, could let government "boot-strap" persuasive campaigns into a First-Amendment-free zone merely by claiming authorship.[59]

**Jawboning and platform pressure — the modern frontier.** *Murthy v. Missouri*, 603 U.S. 43 (2024), is the most significant recent case testing where **government persuasion of private intermediaries** (social media platforms) becomes unconstitutional coercion. States and individual plaintiffs alleged that Biden administration officials pressured Facebook, YouTube, and X to remove COVID-19 and election-related content. The Supreme Court, 6–3, **did not reach the First Amendment merits**; it held the plaintiffs lacked Article III standing because they could not trace specific platform moderation decisions to specific government communications, given that platforms had independent, preexisting content-moderation policies.[87][90][94] Internal communications surfaced in litigation — including Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg's 2024 letter to Congress stating that "senior officials from the Biden administration ... repeatedly pressured our teams for months to censor certain COVID-19 content, including humor and satire," and that he regretted not being "more outspoken about it"[89] — were central to the plaintiffs' factual case but were not sufficient, in the Court's view, to establish standing to litigate the underlying constitutional question. The companion case *NRA v. Vullo* (2024) confirmed the underlying principle that direct government *coercion* of a private intermediary to suppress speech **can** violate the First Amendment; what *Murthy* leaves unresolved is exactly how much "encouragement" (short of a regulatory threat) crosses that line.[87][91] EFF's assessment captures the state of the law well: the Court "did not answer the important First Amendment question... how does one distinguish permissible from impermissible government communications with social media platforms," while narrowing who has standing to litigate it in the first place.[91]

## 7. Classification, withholding, and evidentiary secrecy

These authorities govern *withholding* rather than affirmative deception, but are central to "what critics argue the government has effectively been allowed to do."

- **Executive Order 13526** (2009, retained across administrations) is the operative classification authority. It authorizes classification of information in eight defined categories where unauthorized disclosure "could reasonably be expected to cause" damage to national security, at the Confidential/Secret/Top Secret levels, administered by Original Classification Authorities and reviewed by the Information Security Oversight Office.[80][86]
- **FOIA Exemption 1** (5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1)) permits withholding of information that is "in fact properly classified" under that Executive Order — courts require agencies to show both procedural compliance and substantive fit within a classification category.[79][84][85] The **"Glomar response"** — refusing to confirm or deny that responsive records even exist — is a further, judicially sanctioned tool (originating in *Phillippi v. CIA*, 1976, concerning the Glomar Explorer) available under Exemption 1 (and analogously Exemption 3) when even acknowledging a record's existence would itself reveal classified information.[79]
- **The state secrets privilege**, first fully articulated in **United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1 (1953)**, is a common-law evidentiary privilege allowing the executive to withhold evidence — even from a court in ongoing litigation — on a sworn claim that disclosure would harm national security. If validly invoked, the privilege is **absolute**; courts differ on whether that means only the specific evidence is excluded or the entire case must be dismissed.[81][82][83] *Reynolds* itself has become a case study in the "gray zone/critics" category: when the underlying Air Force accident report was declassified decades later, it reportedly contained no state secrets at all, only evidence of the government's own negligence — fueling the enduring criticism that the privilege, unlike a specific statute, has almost no independent judicial verification mechanism and relies substantially on executive branch good faith.[81][83]

None of these authorities permit affirmative falsehoods to the public; they permit **silence** — a legally distinct but practically overlapping tool, since strategic non-disclosure and misdirection often function similarly to a lie in shaping public understanding.

## 8. The historical arc: Church Committee to the present

**1975–76 — the Church Committee.** The Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities (Church Committee), convened after Seymour Hersh's 1974 *New York Times* reporting on CIA domestic surveillance, produced the foundational public record of Cold War-era information abuses, including:

- **Operation/Project Mockingbird** — CIA cultivation of and, in a narrower 1963 instance, wiretapping of journalists; the Committee's 1976 report stated the CIA "maintains a network of several hundred foreign individuals... who provide intelligence for the CIA and at times attempt to influence opinion through the use of covert propaganda," while then-DCI William Colby also acknowledged historical relationships with American media figures.[70][72][74]
- **COINTELPRO** — FBI covert operations to infiltrate and discredit domestic political organizations (civil rights groups, anti-war activists), including anonymous letters designed to destroy targets' marriages and employment.[70][71]
- **MKULTRA** — CIA human-subject mind-control experimentation.[69]

These findings led directly to the modern oversight architecture: permanent House and Senate intelligence committees, the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (1978), and — most relevant here — the statutory covert-action definition and congressional-notification regime in the National Security Act discussed in Part 2, along with Executive Order 12333's restrictions on domestic activity by foreign-intelligence agencies.[30]

**Post-9/11 through the present.** The pattern recurs in each era discussed above: the Pentagon analyst program (2002–2008), the GAO "covert propaganda" enforcement wave (2004–2015), the 2013 Smith–Mundt repeal debate, ZunZuneo (2009–2014), and the COVID-era platform-pressure litigation culminating in *Murthy* (2024) and the ongoing 2025–2026 legislative fights over Smith-Mundt's scope (the SPIN Act and a bill to repeal the 2013 Modernization Act, both introduced in 2025 partly in response to renewed political controversy).[1][8] The throughline critics identify is consistent: **each episode involves an agency using a legally ambiguous intermediary or category — a paid commentator, a "military analyst," a foreign development program, a platform "consultation"— to achieve an influence effect that a squarely domestic, squarely covert, or squarely unattributed government campaign would clearly not be permitted to achieve.**

## 9. Summary table: authorized, unregulated, and contested

| Activity | Legal status | Governing authority |
|---|---|---|
| State Dept/USAGM producing content for foreign audiences | **Authorized** | Smith–Mundt Act, 22 U.S.C. §1461 |
| That content becoming available to U.S. audiences on request | **Authorized since 2013** | Smith-Mundt Modernization Act of 2012 |
| Deliberately *targeting* Americans with that content for persuasion | **Prohibited** | Foreign Relations Auth. Act §208, as amended |
| Covert action intended to influence U.S. political processes/opinion/media | **Prohibited** | 50 U.S.C. §3093(f) |
| Covert action abroad, with presidential finding + notification | **Authorized** | 50 U.S.C. §3093(a)–(d) |
| "Traditional military/diplomatic activity" that resembles influence ops abroad | **Contested/gray** | Carve-out from §3093(e) definition; DoD MISO doctrine cross-reference |
| Agencies advocating their own policies, with attribution | **Authorized** | Government speech doctrine (*Johanns*) |
| Agencies funding "independent" commentators/news segments without disclosure | **Prohibited (as covert propaganda)** | Appropriations "publicity or propaganda" rider; GAO decisions; Antideficiency Act |
| Government persuading/coordinating with media or platforms, disclosed | **Authorized (contested at the margins)** | Government speech doctrine; *Murthy v. Missouri* standing limits |
| Government coercing platforms to suppress specific speech | **Prohibited if proven** | *NRA v. Vullo*; First Amendment (merits unresolved by *Murthy*) |
| Police lying about evidence to a suspect in custody | **Authorized** | *Frazier v. Cupp*; voluntariness/totality-of-circumstances test |
| Police coercion that overbears free will | **Prohibited** | Fifth/Fourteenth Amendment voluntariness doctrine |
| Withholding properly classified information | **Authorized** | EO 13526; FOIA Exemption 1 |
| Withholding evidence in litigation via state secrets claim | **Authorized, largely unreviewable** | *United States v. Reynolds* |
| Affirmative government lies to the public with no statutory hook | **Not clearly regulated at all** | No general "anti-lying" statute exists; remedies (if any) run through the specific frameworks above |
