# The Balkan Wars: How Yugoslavia's Breakup Became the Template for Post–Cold War Intervention

## Introduction

For most Americans, the wars that tore Yugoslavia apart in the 1990s are remembered, if at all, as a morality play: Serbian aggressors, helpless victims, a hesitant West that finally found its nerve, and a NATO air campaign that stopped a genocide. Writers in the non-interventionist and realist traditions — Scott Horton, Justin Raimondo, Andrew Bacevich, John Mearsheimer, Stephen Walt, Jeffrey Sachs and others — tell a different and more complicated story.

In their telling, the Balkan wars were not primarily a test of Western conscience. They were the first great proving ground of America's post–Cold War "unipolar moment": the place where Washington learned to wage war without congressional authorization or UN approval, where it partnered with jihadists and guerrilla armies it would later call terrorists, where NATO reinvented itself from a defensive alliance into an expeditionary one, and where Russia drew conclusions about Western intentions that still shape the war in Ukraine.

This article lays out that case, the history behind it, and — at the end — the strongest objections to it.

---

## Part I: The Breakup (1991–1992)

### A federation without a referee

Socialist Yugoslavia was a federation of six republics and two autonomous provinces, held together after 1945 by Josip Broz Tito, by Cold War geopolitics, and by a carefully balanced constitutional order. Tito died in 1980. The Cold War ended in 1989. A severe debt and inflation crisis through the 1980s — worsened, critics argue, by IMF austerity programs — hollowed out the federal state and gave republican elites every incentive to turn to nationalism.

Slobodan Milošević rode Serbian nationalism to power in Belgrade; Franjo Tuđman did the same with Croatian nationalism in Zagreb. The non-interventionist writers are not apologists for Milošević, but they insist on a point that popular memory tends to erase: the Yugoslav wars had several nationalist leaderships, several sets of grievances, and several sets of war crimes.

### Recognition before settlement

Slovenia and Croatia declared independence on June 25, 1991. Slovenia's departure was brief and relatively bloodless. Croatia's was not, because roughly 12 percent of its population was Serb, concentrated in the Krajina and Slavonia, and those Serbs had vivid memories of the World War II Ustaše regime's extermination campaign against them.

Realists such as Mearsheimer have long argued that the decisive Western error came early: Germany's push, followed by the European Community in January 1992, to recognize Slovenia and Croatia before any agreement had been reached on borders, minority rights, or the fate of the remaining republics. Recognizing the old internal administrative lines as international borders turned millions of people into unwilling minorities in new states overnight. The logic of the realist critique is simple: if you create a state and leave a large, armed, frightened minority inside it with no guarantees, you have created the conditions for war.

### Bosnia: the plan that was walked away from

Bosnia-Herzegovina was the most mixed republic of all — roughly 44 percent Muslim (Bosniak), 31 percent Serb and 17 percent Croat at the 1991 census. Its independence referendum in early 1992 was boycotted by most Bosnian Serbs.

In March 1992, European mediators produced the Lisbon Agreement (the Carrington–Cutileiro plan), which would have created a cantonized Bosnia divided among its three communities. All three leaders initially signed it. Bosniak leader Alija Izetbegović then withdrew his signature.

Here the antiwar critique leans heavily on a 1993 *New York Times* report that U.S. Ambassador Warren Zimmermann had told Izetbegović, in effect, that he need not sign something he did not want. Horton and others in the Antiwar.com orbit treat this as an early instance of Washington sabotaging a negotiated settlement in favor of a maximalist outcome. Zimmermann himself later disputed that interpretation, and historians still argue over how much weight the conversation carried. What is not disputed is the outcome: weeks later Bosnia was at war, and roughly 100,000 people would die before it ended.

The pattern the critics identify — a deal on the table, an American signal that a better one might be had, then war — is one they see repeated later, in Rambouillet in 1999 and in Istanbul in 2022.

## Part II: The Bosnian War (1992–1995)

### The Vance–Owen plan and the "lift and strike" debate

In 1993, UN envoy Cyrus Vance and EU envoy David Owen proposed a revised cantonal plan. The Clinton administration declined to put its weight behind it, criticizing it as rewarding ethnic cleansing, and floated instead "lift and strike": lifting the UN arms embargo on the Bosnian government while bombing Bosnian Serb forces. Mearsheimer and Robert Pape argued in *The New Republic* that the honest answer was the one nobody wanted to say aloud — some form of partition, since the communities would not consent to live under one another's rule. Every plan that eventually ended the war, including Dayton, ended up looking a great deal like the plans that had been rejected earlier.

### The Iranian pipeline and the mujahideen

This is the heart of Scott Horton's treatment of Bosnia in *Enough Already*, where he presents the Bosnian war as a direct link in the chain that led to September 11.

Officially, the United States supported the UN arms embargo on all parties. In practice, in April 1994 the Clinton administration gave Croatia what became known as a "no instructions" answer when Zagreb asked whether Washington objected to Iranian weapons being shipped through Croatian territory to the Bosnian government. The arrangement was later exposed by the *Los Angeles Times* and investigated by Congress. Iran, Saudi Arabia and other states poured arms, money and fighters into Bosnia.

Among those fighters were several thousand foreign mujahideen, many veterans of the Afghan war against the Soviets, organized in units such as the El Mudžahid detachment. Horton's point is that the American government knowingly tolerated a pipeline that built up the same transnational jihadist network it would later declare war on. He frequently notes that the 9/11 Commission Report records that two of the eventual September 11 hijackers, Nawaf al-Hazmi and Khalid al-Mihdhar, had traveled to Bosnia in 1995 to fight. To Horton, Bosnia belongs in the same story as Afghanistan in the 1980s and Libya and Syria in the 2010s: Washington backing radical Islamist fighters against a secular enemy of the moment, then reaping the blowback.

### Srebrenica

In July 1995, Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić overran the UN "safe area" of Srebrenica and murdered roughly 8,000 Bosniak men and boys. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) and the International Court of Justice have both ruled the massacre a genocide, and the remains of thousands of victims have been identified through DNA analysis. Mladić and Bosnian Serb political leader Radovan Karadžić were both convicted.

The serious non-interventionist writers do not deny this crime. Their argument is about context and selectivity: that the "safe areas" were not demilitarized and were used as bases for raids by Bosniak forces under Naser Orić into surrounding Serb villages; that Western powers were selective about which atrocities they publicized; and that Srebrenica was subsequently used to justify a whole doctrine of intervention whose later applications — Libya above all — produced catastrophe.

### Operation Storm: the ethnic cleansing America helped with

If Srebrenica is the atrocity everyone remembers, Operation Storm is the one the non-interventionists insist on remembering too. In August 1995, the Croatian army overran the Serb-held Krajina in a matter of days. Somewhere between 150,000 and 200,000 Krajina Serbs fled or were driven out, in what is widely described as the largest single act of ethnic cleansing of the Yugoslav wars. Hundreds of mostly elderly Serb civilians who stayed behind were killed.

The Croatian army had been trained and advised by Military Professional Resources Inc. (MPRI), a private contractor staffed by retired U.S. generals and operating with Washington's approval. The United States provided diplomatic cover. Raimondo and other Antiwar.com writers of the era treated Storm as proof that American policy was not about opposing ethnic cleansing as such, but about choosing whose ethnic cleansing to support. (The Croatian commander, Ante Gotovina, was convicted by the ICTY at trial and then acquitted on appeal in 2012 — a ruling Serbs regard as an injustice and supporters regard as vindication.)

### Deliberate Force and Dayton

A second mortar attack on Sarajevo's Markale marketplace in August 1995 became the trigger for NATO's Operation Deliberate Force, a bombing campaign against Bosnian Serb forces. (The attribution of the Markale shellings has been disputed for decades, though the ICTY attributed them to Bosnian Serb forces.) Combined with the Croatian–Bosniak ground offensive, the bombing pushed the Serbs to the table.

The Dayton Accords of November 1995 ended the war. The result was a Bosnia divided into a Serb Republic (Republika Srpska) and a Bosniak–Croat Federation, roughly along a 49/51 territorial split — in substance, not far from the partition plans that had been rejected in 1992 and 1993. Mearsheimer and Stephen Van Evera argued at the time that Dayton was an unstable half-measure that would require a permanent foreign protectorate. Three decades later, Bosnia is still overseen by an international High Representative with power to impose laws and remove elected officials.

## Part III: Kosovo (1998–1999)

### From "terrorists" to allies

Kosovo was a province of Serbia with an ethnic Albanian majority, and Milošević had stripped it of its autonomy in 1989. For most of the 1990s, Kosovo Albanians resisted nonviolently under Ibrahim Rugova. By 1996–1998, the Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) had emerged, attacking Serbian police, officials, and Albanians it considered collaborators.

In February 1998, U.S. special envoy Robert Gelbard publicly described the KLA as a terrorist group. Within about a year, Washington was effectively its air force. Raimondo, writing on Antiwar.com throughout 1998 and 1999, hammered this reversal relentlessly, along with reporting tying parts of the KLA to heroin trafficking networks. Horton cites the episode as a recurring pattern: a group is labeled terrorist when convenient and rebranded as freedom fighters when it serves a larger strategic aim.

The later record has given the critics more ammunition. A 2010 Council of Europe report by Swiss senator Dick Marty accused senior KLA figures of organized crime and worse. In 2020, former KLA leader and Kosovo president Hashim Thaçi was indicted for war crimes and crimes against humanity by the Kosovo Specialist Chambers in The Hague, and his trial opened in 2023.

### Račak and Rambouillet

In January 1999, the bodies of some 40 Albanians were found at Račak. American diplomat William Walker, head of the OSCE monitoring mission, immediately called it a massacre of civilians, and it became the political trigger for war. The forensic and political interpretation of Račak — massacre of civilians versus KLA fighters killed in combat and later arranged — remains contested in the antiwar literature, though Western governments and the ICTY treated it as a massacre.

Then came the Rambouillet talks in February–March 1999. The point the non-interventionists return to again and again is Appendix B of the proposed agreement, which would have given NATO forces free movement and legal immunity not only in Kosovo but throughout the entire Federal Republic of Yugoslavia. No sovereign government could have accepted that. Even Henry Kissinger later remarked that the text read as a provocation and a pretext for bombing rather than a serious offer. The critics' conclusion: Rambouillet was designed to be refused.

### Operation Allied Force

NATO began bombing on March 24, 1999. The campaign lasted 78 days.

**It was illegal under international law.** It had no UN Security Council authorization, and it was not a response to an attack on any NATO member. Jeffrey Sachs has repeatedly made this the core of his argument that NATO is not the purely defensive alliance it claims to be: the 1999 bombing of Belgrade, he says, is the clearest proof.

**It was unconstitutional at home.** Andrew Napolitano and other libertarians stress that Congress never authorized the war. In April 1999 the House of Representatives actually voted on a resolution supporting the air campaign, and it failed on a 213–213 tie. Clinton continued regardless, and the courts declined to intervene. The War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock passed without consequence. To these writers, Kosovo was the moment the presidency's war power became effectively unlimited — a precedent Obama later cited explicitly when he bombed Libya without congressional approval in 2011.

**It hit civilians.** Human Rights Watch documented roughly 500 civilian deaths. NATO struck a passenger train at Grdelica, refugee convoys near Đakovica, Serbian state television's headquarters (killing 16 media workers), and — on May 7 — the Chinese embassy in Belgrade, killing three Chinese journalists. It also struck bridges, power plants and factories, and used cluster munitions and depleted uranium.

**It may have accelerated the very expulsions it claimed to prevent.** Roughly 850,000 Kosovo Albanians fled or were driven out of the province, the overwhelming majority *after* the bombing started. Defenders of the war argue Serbia had a pre-planned expulsion scheme ("Operation Horseshoe"); critics note that the documentary basis for Horseshoe has itself been questioned, and argue that bombing gave Belgrade both motive and cover.

**It was less militarily effective than advertised.** Andrew Cockburn, in *Kill Chain* and in his reporting on air power, draws on the postwar findings that Serbian forces had used decoys extensively and that NATO had destroyed only a small fraction of the tanks and artillery it claimed. The army that withdrew from Kosovo in June 1999 was largely intact. The war ended through Russian diplomatic pressure on Milošević and the threat of a ground invasion, not because air power had won it.

### The aftermath in Kosovo

After NATO's KFOR force entered Kosovo, the direction of ethnic cleansing reversed. Large numbers of Serbs, Roma and other non-Albanians — commonly estimated at around 200,000 — fled or were driven out, and in March 2004 anti-Serb riots destroyed homes and Orthodox churches and monasteries under the noses of NATO peacekeepers. The United States built Camp Bondsteel, one of its largest bases in the region. Kosovo declared independence in 2008 with American backing; Serbia, Russia, China and a number of EU members still do not recognize it. Daniel Larison was among the most consistent critics of that recognition, arguing it shredded the principle of territorial integrity the West would later invoke elsewhere.

## Part IV: Why It Still Matters

### The template

Andrew Bacevich, in *American Empire* and later *America's War for the Greater Middle East*, treats the Balkans as the place where the Clinton administration perfected "coercive diplomacy" — the routine use of force short of declared war, sold as humanitarian and managed by air power so that Americans paid almost no visible cost. He argues the Kosovo war was ultimately fought less for the Albanians than for the credibility of NATO at its fiftieth anniversary.

Stephen Walt, in *The Hell of Good Intentions*, sees the Balkans as the founding success story of "liberal hegemony," the bipartisan foreign-policy consensus that America could and should remake other societies. The perceived success in Bosnia and Kosovo emboldened the people who went on to argue for Iraq in 2003 and Libya in 2011. The "Responsibility to Protect" doctrine that justified Libya was developed in large part as an answer to the questions raised by Kosovo's illegality.

### The Russian lesson

This is the theme that links the Balkans to the present. In March 1999, Russian Prime Minister Yevgeny Primakov was flying to Washington when he learned the bombing was about to begin; he ordered his plane to turn around over the Atlantic. Weeks earlier, Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic had joined NATO. To Moscow, the sequence looked unmistakable: NATO was expanding eastward, and it was now willing to bomb a Slavic, Orthodox country without UN approval and over Russia's objections.

Mearsheimer, in his 2014 *Foreign Affairs* essay on the Ukraine crisis, and Horton, in *Provoked*, both trace the deterioration of U.S.–Russian relations through this moment. When Russia annexed Crimea in 2014, Vladimir Putin explicitly cited the Kosovo precedent — Western-backed secession from a sovereign state without that state's consent. The critics do not say the comparison is fair or that it justifies Russia's actions. Their point is that Washington wrote the rulebook it now accuses others of breaking.

### The jihadist thread

Finally, Horton's argument returns to blowback. The Bosnian pipeline helped build the networks that produced al-Qaeda's post-1995 generation. The precedent of using radical Islamist fighters as auxiliaries against a secular government recurred in Libya and Syria. The Balkans, in this view, were not an exception to the war on terror but one of its prologues.

## Part V: The Case Against This Critique

The non-interventionist reading is a minority view, and its opponents raise serious objections. A fair account should state them.

**Non-intervention had already been tried, and it produced Srebrenica.** Interventionists such as Samantha Power (*A Problem from Hell*) and Richard Holbrooke (*To End a War*) argue that for three years the West did largely what non-interventionists recommended — embargo, negotiate, stay out — and the result was some 100,000 dead, the siege of Sarajevo, and a genocide. When NATO finally used force in 1995, the war ended within months.

**The atrocity record is not symmetrical.** All sides committed crimes, but the ICTY's convictions, the demographic data, and the scale of the siege of Sarajevo and the Bosnian Serb campaign of 1992 point to Bosnian Serb forces, backed by Belgrade, as responsible for the largest share of killing and expulsion in Bosnia. Critics of the antiwar literature argue that emphasizing Operation Storm, Orić's raids, or KLA crimes can slide into false equivalence.

**Kosovo's Albanians wanted out.** Whatever the legality of the 1999 war, the Albanian majority had suffered a decade of repression, and hundreds of thousands were displaced in 1998 before NATO acted. Most Kosovars regard NATO as their liberator, and Kosovo's independence has since been recognized by over 100 countries. In 2010 the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion that Kosovo's declaration of independence did not violate international law.

**Legality isn't everything.** The Independent International Commission on Kosovo famously concluded that the war was "illegal but legitimate" — that the Security Council was blocked by Russian and Chinese vetoes and that the humanitarian stakes justified action anyway.

**The Russia argument cuts both ways.** Many scholars reject the idea that Kosovo or NATO enlargement explains Russian behavior, arguing that Russian revanchism had domestic and imperial roots and that Putin's invocation of Kosovo was opportunistic.

**Many specific claims remain disputed.** The significance of Zimmermann's role in the Lisbon collapse, what happened at Račak, the authenticity of Operation Horseshoe, the attribution of the Markale shellings, and casualty figures on every side are all contested. Readers should weigh primary sources, ICTY trial records, and the work of historians across the spectrum rather than relying on any one school.

## Conclusion

The writers in this tradition do not argue that Milošević was a good man, that Serbs committed no atrocities, or that the people of Bosnia and Kosovo did not suffer terribly. Their argument is that the Balkan wars were sold to Americans as a simple story of good intentions, when in fact they involved sabotaged settlements, a secret arms pipeline to jihadists, sponsorship of one side's ethnic cleansing, an illegal and unauthorized air war, and a set of precedents — on presidential war powers, on humanitarian intervention, on NATO's role, on recognizing secession — that have shaped every American war since and poisoned relations with Russia.

Whether one ultimately agrees with them or with the interventionists, the Balkan wars are where the debates over Iraq, Libya, Syria and Ukraine began.
