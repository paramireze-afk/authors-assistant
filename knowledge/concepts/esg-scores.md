# Governance by Other Means: An Intellectual History of the Anti-ESG Movement

*How Hayek, Friedman, Sowell, Thiel, Greenwald, and Ramaswamy converge on a single argument — that ESG is not primarily an investing strategy, but a new architecture of power*

---

## How to read this

This is not a balanced overview of ESG. It's a reconstruction of a worldview — an attempt to lay out, as coherently and charitably as possible, how a specific intellectual tradition understands ESG: why they think it emerged, what problem they believe it's really solving, why it became so powerful, how it actually moved through corporate America institution by institution, and what they think it does to a society over the long run.

The thinkers pulled together here — Friedrich Hayek, Milton Friedman, Thomas Sowell, Peter Thiel, Glenn Greenwald, and Vivek Ramaswamy — did not build this argument together, and they disagree with each other on plenty (Hayek and Greenwald, in particular, would find little common political ground). But when you read them on this specific question, a single underlying argument emerges, assembled from different eras and different disciplines. The goal here is to show you that argument in its strongest form, in their own words wherever possible — not to adjudicate whether it's correct. A short closing section gestures at where the argument is most contested, so you know where to look if you want to pressure-test it yourself — but the weight of this piece is on understanding the critics on their own terms, the way you'd want to understand any coherent school of thought from the inside.

## Part I — The deep foundation: Hayek and the knowledge problem

To understand why later critics treat ESG as more than a corporate fad, you have to start with an argument that predates ESG by seventy years: Friedrich Hayek's critique of centralized economic planning.

In his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society," Hayek argued that the information required to allocate resources well in a complex economy — what people actually want, what's scarce, what trade-offs are worth making — is not held by any central authority. It exists in fragments, scattered across millions of individual minds, much of it tacit and un-articulable even to the person who holds it. The price system, in Hayek's account, is not just a market mechanism; it's an information-processing system, a way of aggregating dispersed, local knowledge into a single signal (a price) without anyone needing to understand the whole picture.

Hayek returned to this theme throughout his career, most memorably in *The Fatal Conceit* (1988), where he wrote that "the curious task of economics is to demonstrate to men how little they really know about what they imagine they can design." Central planners, no matter how well-intentioned or well-credentialed, cannot replicate the market's information-processing power because the knowledge they'd need to plan well doesn't exist anywhere in a form that could be collected and handed to them. Hayek called this **the knowledge problem**, and formalized a version of it as **"the pretense of knowledge"** in his 1974 Nobel lecture — a warning that economists and planners often mistake the appearance of scientific rigor for actual predictive competence, especially in complex systems.

Hayek's second, related argument — laid out most famously in *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) — is about the political consequences of central planning, not just its economic inefficiency. His claim was that once a society accepts the premise that some centralized body should direct economic outcomes according to a single, coherent plan or set of values, it inevitably has to start overriding the diverse, competing values that free individuals would otherwise pursue through markets. Someone has to decide *whose* values the plan serves — and that requires concentrating power in ways that, Hayek argued, tend to erode both economic efficiency and political freedom over time, almost regardless of the planners' intentions.

**Why this matters for ESG, in the critics' framing:** ESG scoring is, at bottom, an attempt to encode a *specific, centralized* set of values — carbon targets, diversity ratios, governance norms — into a standardized rating that then gets applied across an entire economy, influencing which companies get capital and on what terms. From a Hayekian lens, this looks less like "adding more information to markets" (the way its defenders describe it) and more like a **soft form of central planning**: a small number of ratings agencies and asset managers deciding, in advance and by formula, which trade-offs companies *should* make on emissions, labor, or governance — trade-offs that, in a Hayekian market, would otherwise be negotiated locally, imperfectly, and adaptively by millions of buyers, workers, and investors responding to actual prices and actual consequences.

## Part II — Friedman: the corporation as a limited, accountable institution

If Hayek supplies the epistemological argument (planners can't know enough), Milton Friedman supplies the *institutional* argument: even if you could know enough, corporate executives are the wrong people to be making these choices, because of who they answer to and who they don't.

Friedman's 1970 *New York Times Magazine* essay, "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits," is the founding document of the entire critical tradition explored here — everyone from Sowell to Ramaswamy either builds on it or explicitly measures their argument against it. Its central claim, quoting his own earlier formulation from *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962), is stated almost as a maxim: "There is one and only one social responsibility of business - to use it resources and engage in activities designed to increase its profits so long as it stays within the rules of the game, which is to say, engages in open and free competition without deception or fraud."

Friedman's argument, unpacked:

- A corporate executive is an employee of the shareholders. Their job is to run the business according to shareholders' wishes — generally, to make as much money as possible — while conforming to the "rules of the game": both law and what he called ethical custom.
- When an executive spends company resources on a "social responsibility" beyond that — say, reducing pollution beyond what's legally required, or forgoing profit to support some other cause — they are, in effect, **spending someone else's money** (the shareholders', through lower returns; the customers', through higher prices; or the employees', through lower wages) to pursue a goal that executive personally favors.
- This is, in Friedman's account, a form of **taxation without representation**: an unelected private actor imposing costs on others to fund causes those others never voted for.
- Friedman's conclusion isn't that social problems don't matter — it's that they belong to **government**, precisely *because* government is (at least in principle) publicly accountable in a way a private company's management is not. Businesses pursuing "social responsibility" aren't heroically stepping into a vacuum; they're quietly taking over a function that a democratic polity is supposed to perform, and doing it without the mandate to do it legitimately.
- Friedman was also making an *efficiency* argument, separate from the accountability one: he believed executives are generally not well-positioned to judge social trade-offs competently, and that asking them to do so distracts from the actual expertise they were hired to exercise — running a competitive business.

**Why this matters for ESG:** ESG, in this frame, isn't a new idea so much as Friedman's 1970 warning arriving fifty years later, institutionalized and scaled. Where Friedman worried about individual executives occasionally moonlighting as social reformers, critics argue ESG has turned that into a *standing, systemized apparatus* — ratings agencies, compliance departments, proxy votes, executive compensation tied to non-financial metrics — that operates continuously and at the scale of the entire public-equities market, not just a few high-profile companies. The accountability gap Friedman flagged as an occasional abuse becomes, in the critics' telling, a **permanent institutional feature** of how large swaths of the economy are now run.

## Part III — Sowell: knowledge, disparities, and the limits of centralized moral judgment

Thomas Sowell's relevant contribution isn't about ESG by name — the term postdates most of his major writing — but two of his recurring arguments, developed across decades of work in economics and social policy, are load-bearing for how later critics think about the "S" (social) and "G" (governance) components of ESG specifically.

**First: the disparity argument.** In books like *Discrimination and Disparities* (2018), Sowell argues that unequal statistical outcomes between groups — in income, employment, board representation, whatever the metric — are routinely and, in his view, wrongly assumed to be evidence of discrimination, when in fact outcomes are shaped by an enormous number of factors that have nothing to do with animus or bias. As he puts it elsewhere in his work: statistical disparities do not automatically mean discrimination. His broader point is methodological: it is very easy to observe an unequal outcome and construct a plausible-sounding moral narrative to explain it, and very hard to actually verify that narrative against the full set of alternative explanations. He argues this error is not innocent — once a specific narrative is treated as self-evidently true rather than as a hypothesis to be tested, it becomes the basis for policy interventions — quotas, mandated targets, "equity" metrics — that may not actually address the real cause of the disparity.

**Second: the knowledge argument**, developed independently but in the same spirit as Hayek's. In *Knowledge and Decisions* (1980) and later *Intellectuals and Society* (2009), Sowell makes a version of Hayek's dispersed-knowledge point but sharpens it into a warning about power specifically: "It is far easier to concentrate power than to concentrate knowledge." He is particularly skeptical of what he calls, in *The Vision of the Anointed* (1995), the tendency of a credentialed elite to believe their judgment entitles them to override the decentralized decisions of ordinary people — workers choosing jobs, consumers choosing products, communities organizing themselves — in the name of a better social outcome. On his account, that elite systematically undercounts how much real, distributed knowledge already exists in "the competition of the marketplace" and social processes involving millions of people, compared to the "secondhand knowledge of generalities" a smaller group of experts actually holds.

**Why this matters for ESG:** put these two arguments together, and critics have a specific objection to the "S" and "G" pillars of ESG that goes beyond Friedman's accountability point. It's not just that ESG standards are imposed by unaccountable actors (Friedman's objection) — it's that the standards themselves often rest on **unexamined causal assumptions** about what a "good" diverse board, workforce, or supply chain looks like, assumptions treated as settled facts rather than contested empirical claims. A board-diversity target, in this reading, isn't a neutral technical metric like a debt-to-equity ratio — it's a policy conclusion smuggled in as if it were a measurement.

## Part IV — How the institutions actually built ESG: a critic's institutional history

This is where the critics' argument stops being abstract philosophy and becomes a story about specific organizations, in a specific order, making specific decisions. Understanding *how* ESG physically moved through corporate America is, in the critics' own telling, as important as understanding *why* they object to it — because the mechanism is the argument. If ESG had simply been one consulting firm's product, it wouldn't be interesting. What makes it significant, in this account, is that a fairly small number of interlocking institutions — international bodies, ratings firms, proxy advisors, and the Big Three asset managers — created a closed loop that could move an idea from a conference room in Davos into the compensation formula of a Fortune 500 CEO within a few years.

### The idea-generating layer: UN bodies and the World Economic Forum

- The **UN Global Compact**, launched in 2000, is where the term "ESG" was effectively born: a 2004 report it convened, *Who Cares Wins*, coined the acronym and pitched it explicitly to financial institutions as a way of formalizing non-financial risk analysis.
- The **UN-backed Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI)**, launched in 2006, gave asset managers a formal document to sign, publicly committing them to incorporate ESG factors into investment analysis and ownership practices. Once a firm signs, reputational pressure to be seen following through builds — a mechanism critics view as a kind of soft, peer-enforced compliance system operating entirely outside any legislature.
- The **World Economic Forum (WEF)**, under founder **Klaus Schwab**, is the institution critics treat as the philosophical and organizational engine of the whole movement. Schwab had championed the idea he called "stakeholder capitalism" since the WEF's founding in 1971, and in December 2019 the Forum issued a new "Davos Manifesto" explicitly built around it — arguing companies should be "trustees of society," not simply instruments of shareholder return. The manifesto's own language calls for realigning executive performance metrics away from "purely shareholder returns" toward "long-term shared value creation."
- In September 2020, the WEF went further, publishing *Measuring Stakeholder Capitalism: Towards Common Metrics and Consistent Reporting of Sustainable Value Creation* — a joint project with the world's four largest accounting firms (**Deloitte, EY, KPMG, and PwC**) to build a standardized set of ESG metrics that companies worldwide could adopt. Schwab's own framing at the launch was direct: "With these metrics, the business world will finally be able to walk the talk on their commitment to ESG performance and the stakeholder capitalism principle."

**The critics' reading of this layer:** none of these bodies were elected by anyone, and none of them are subject to a vote that could remove them if their standards prove unpopular or harmful. Yet by getting the Big Four accounting firms — who audit or advise the overwhelming majority of large public companies worldwide — to co-develop a "standard" set of metrics, the WEF achieved something no single government agency could have achieved as quickly: a de facto global reporting standard that companies would begin adopting not because any law required it, but because their own auditors, and the norms of "best practice" among their peers, now expected it.

### The scoring layer: ratings agencies

Once a standard vocabulary existed, private ratings firms — **MSCI, Sustainalytics (Morningstar), S&P Global, ISS ESG, Bloomberg** — built proprietary scoring models on top of it, translating company disclosures into a single letter grade or numeric score. Critics point to a structural feature here that compounds the accountability problem raised in Parts I–III: because there is no single agreed methodology, each rater is, in effect, unilaterally deciding what "good" governance or "good" environmental performance means — and because different raters routinely produce sharply divergent scores for the same company, there's no external check forcing convergence toward accuracy the way, for example, competing credit-rating agencies are disciplined by observable default rates. A rating can be wrong for years without ever being falsified in a way the market clearly registers.

### The vote-execution layer: proxy advisory firms

This is, in the critics' account, the layer where scoring turns into actual corporate power — and the one drawing the sharpest political fire as of 2025–2026. Two firms, **Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS)** and **Glass Lewis**, control more than 90% of the global proxy advisory market — the business of telling institutional shareholders how to vote on director elections, executive pay, and shareholder proposals. Because a large share of institutional investors default to these firms' recommendations rather than independently researching every vote at every company they hold, ISS and Glass Lewis exert outsized leverage: research cited in 2025 congressional testimony found that a negative ISS recommendation is associated with roughly a 20-to-30 percentage point swing in opposing votes.

Critics have specifically flagged a structural conflict of interest: ISS both issues voting recommendations to shareholders *and* sells governance consulting services to the very companies it evaluates — advising companies, in effect, on how to earn the favorable rating it will later issue. A 2025 congressional hearing was titled, pointedly, **"Exposing the Proxy Advisory Cartel: How ISS & Glass Lewis Influence Markets."** Committee chair Rep. Ann Wagner summarized the concern this way: "Two firms — ISS and Glass Lewis — control 97% of the proxy advisory market. That concentration alone would warrant scrutiny... But more troubling is how their influence goes far beyond research — they now routinely dictate outcomes of shareholder votes." In late 2025 several state attorneys general opened investigations into whether the two firms had misrepresented how much weight they gave ESG and DEI factors versus pure financial performance, the Federal Trade Commission opened an antitrust inquiry into the firms' market dominance, and in December 2025 the Trump administration issued an executive order directly addressing proxy advisor practices and shareholder proposal rules — after which both ISS and Glass Lewis began publicly revising their voting guidelines, with ISS moving away from its blanket "for" recommendations on environmental and social shareholder proposals toward case-by-case review.

**The critics' reading of this layer:** this is, for critics, the clearest real-world instance of Sowell's "vision of the anointed" and Thiel's "cartel" argument operating in concrete, documented, litigated form — two firms nobody elected, whose specific conflicts of interest are now the subject of federal antitrust scrutiny, functionally setting governance norms across the entire investable public market.

### The capital-allocation layer: the Big Three asset managers

Finally, the mechanism that gives all of the above teeth: **BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street**, the world's three largest asset managers, run largely through passive index funds that must hold nearly the entire market simultaneously. Because index funds can't sell and walk away from an underperforming holding the way an active investor might, their main lever of influence is **voting and "engagement"** — precisely the channel that ISS and Glass Lewis recommendations flow through. BlackRock CEO Larry Fink's influential annual letters to CEOs — especially the 2018 "A Sense of Purpose" letter and the 2020 letter declaring climate risk to be investment risk — are, in the critics' account, the moment the world's largest single shareholder in most of corporate America explicitly endorsed the WEF's stakeholder-capitalism framework and asked companies to report accordingly.

### The complete loop, and why critics call it a system rather than a trend

Put the four layers together and critics describe a closed, self-reinforcing loop: an international body (WEF, UN) generates the values and vocabulary → an accounting or ratings firm translates it into a measurable score → a proxy advisor turns that score into an actual shareholder-vote recommendation → the largest asset managers, structurally required to vote on nearly every company they hold, execute those recommendations at scale → the resulting influence on cost of capital, board composition, and executive pay pressures individual companies to adopt the original standard, which reinforces the ratings agencies' and proxy advisors' authority to keep scoring against it. No single actor in this loop needed to coerce anyone directly; each institution was simply doing its normal job — rating, advising, voting, managing money — and the aggregate effect, in the critics' account, was a governance system that operates continuously, across the whole economy, without ever passing through an election.

### How it spread inside individual companies

Institutionally, this loop is also why critics argue ESG stopped being a marketing add-on and became embedded structure inside companies themselves during the 2018–2022 period specifically:

- **Chief Sustainability Officer** roles and dedicated ESG teams proliferated inside large public companies, often reporting directly to the CEO or board.
- **Executive compensation** committees began tying a portion of bonus pay to ESG or DEI metrics — meaning a CEO's personal income was now, in part, a function of the same scores ISS, Glass Lewis, and the ratings agencies were producing.
- **Consulting firms** (the Big Four, plus specialist ESG consultancies) built large practices helping companies improve their scores — creating an industry with a direct financial interest in ESG's continued relevance and expansion, a dynamic critics view as structurally similar to the conflicts flagged in the proxy-advisor layer.
- **Business schools** incorporated ESG and stakeholder-capitalism frameworks into MBA curricula, professionalizing and normalizing the vocabulary for a new generation of managers before it was ever legislated anywhere.
- **Regulators**, especially in the EU (the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation) and at times the U.S. SEC, began proposing mandatory ESG disclosure rules — the critics' clearest example of the informal loop above eventually seeking to convert itself into binding law, which is precisely the move Ramaswamy and allied state officials organized most aggressively to block after 2022.

## Part V — Thiel: ESG as cartel behavior and "enemy-naming"

Peter Thiel's critique comes from a different angle than Hayek, Friedman, or Sowell's — less about epistemology or accountability in the abstract, and more about what he sees ESG actually *doing*, mechanically, inside markets.

Thiel's most widely circulated remarks on ESG came at the Bitcoin 2022 conference in Miami, where he called ESG "perhaps the real enemy" of open, competitive markets. In his words: "I think that ESG is just a hate factory. It's a factory for naming enemies, and we should not be allowing them to do that." He grouped Larry Fink together with figures like Warren Buffett and Jamie Dimon as part of, in his phrase, the "finance gerontocracy that runs the country."

There's a sharper structural argument underneath the rhetoric, consistent with Thiel's broader writing on competition (in *Zero to One*, 2014, he argues capitalism's natural tendency, left to its own logic, is toward monopoly rather than competition). Applied to ESG, the implication critics draw is this: **coordinated ESG commitments among the largest asset managers function like a cartel**, even without any formal agreement. If BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street — who collectively hold enormous stakes in nearly every major public company — all adopt the same emissions targets or the same board-diversity expectations (as they arguably did through joint memberships in coalitions like the Net Zero Asset Managers initiative and Climate Action 100+), individual companies lose the ability to compete by offering a *different* mix of trade-offs to investors who might want it. This is precisely the theory Texas and ten other states advanced in their November 2024 antitrust lawsuit against the three firms, alleging their joint coalition membership functioned to suppress coal production and raise energy prices — a legal claim that gives Thiel's "cartel" framing a concrete, litigated form.

This also connects to Sowell's "vision of the anointed": ESG raters and mega-asset-managers are, in Thiel's telling, a new class of self-appointed arbiters, deciding which industries and business models are socially acceptable — a power that used to belong either to competitive markets or to elected government, exercised now by neither.

## Part VI — Greenwald: the "industrial complex" pattern, applied to finance

Glenn Greenwald doesn't write primarily about ESG — his relevant body of work is about what he calls the **censorship-industrial complex**: the pattern of government actors achieving outcomes that would be unconstitutional or politically costly if done directly (state censorship of protected speech) by *pressuring or coordinating with nominally private companies* to do it instead. As Greenwald put it in one interview: "Tech corporations have no democratic accountability at all, they can do whatever they want." His core observation is structural, not partisan: a private company has no First Amendment obligations and isn't subject to the checks that constrain a government actor directly. In a 2022 Twitter thread widely cited in coverage of his views, he summarized the underlying dynamic bluntly: "This is *Govt censorship* disguised as corporate autonomy."

Greenwald has applied a version of this same structural logic to financial "debanking" — instances (Canadian authorities freezing protesters' bank accounts during the 2022 trucker protests, or payment processors cutting off politically disfavored individuals and organizations) where financial access, rather than speech directly, becomes the lever. His broader framework treats this as one instance of a general pattern he calls the fusion of state and corporate power, in which nominally separate institutions — media companies, tech platforms, banks, asset managers — begin to act in a coordinated way to produce outcomes that public opinion or the electoral process alone would not have delivered.

**Why this matters for ESG, extending Greenwald's framework by analogy (this is the critics' application, not a claim Greenwald has made in these specific words):** critics who invoke this pattern argue ESG is finance's version of the same mechanism. A legislature that wanted to, say, restrict fossil fuel production or mandate specific board compositions would have to pass a law — subject to debate, elections, courts, and the possibility of being voted out. But if regulators, NGOs, and large asset managers coordinate — through vehicles like the Net Zero Asset Managers initiative or Climate Action 100+, or through the ISS/Glass Lewis voting-recommendation loop described in Part IV — to pressure companies toward the *same substantive outcome* via capital allocation and shareholder voting rather than legislation, the result functions like policy without ever passing through a legislature, a vote, or a court that could strike it down as unconstitutional overreach.

## Part VII — Ramaswamy: synthesizing the argument into "stakeholder capitalism vs. democracy"

Vivek Ramaswamy's contribution, laid out most fully in *Woke, Inc.: Inside Corporate America's Social Justice Scam* (2021) and developed further across interviews, is best understood as a **synthesis** — he takes Hayek's suspicion of centralized value-setting, Friedman's accountability argument, Sowell's skepticism of imposed metrics, and Thiel's concentration-of-power concern, and organizes them into a single, explicitly *democratic-theory* argument, which he frames as distinct from all of theirs.

Ramaswamy is careful to distinguish his objection from Friedman's. As he put it directly in an interview: "I worry not how politics will infect business, but how business will infect our politics." Friedman worried that stakeholder-style social spending would make companies **less efficient** — a worry about market performance. Ramaswamy has said he's largely sympathetic to that concern, but that it's not his central one; his argument runs the other direction.

In the same vein, describing what stakeholder capitalism and ESG actually are, he's said plainly: "Stakeholder capitalism is the idea that corporations should advance not only profit, but also particular values." And on his view of the movement's substance: "It's just a farce. The ESG movement is basically an excuse for raising capital under a virtuous banner. It's nothing other than dressing up old-school investing in new-age clothing."

The argument, reconstructed in full:

1. **The corporate bargain.** Ramaswamy leans on a specific legal-historical claim: the modern corporation exists because society granted it **limited liability** — a legal shield protecting shareholders from being personally responsible for the company's debts and harms. This is not a natural right; it's a deliberate policy choice, extended on the premise that in exchange, corporations would stay within a defined lane — producing goods and services, generating profit within the law — rather than becoming independent political actors. If a corporation starts functioning as a vehicle for political and social advocacy, Ramaswamy argues, it has stepped outside the terms of that bargain, while continuing to enjoy the legal protections that bargain provided.
2. **Money vs. votes.** In a democracy, the formal mechanism for resolving contested social questions is supposed to be one person, one vote, mediated through elected representatives who can be replaced. Stakeholder capitalism, Ramaswamy argues, creates a parallel channel where money substitutes for votes: whoever controls the largest pools of capital — a handful of asset managers, a handful of activist coalitions — can move corporate behavior, and therefore social outcomes, in a preferred direction, entirely independent of whether a majority of voters would have chosen that outcome through the ballot box. He has argued: "A small group of investors and CEOs is determining things that ought to be adjudicated through democracy."
3. **Why it happened when it did.** Ramaswamy locates the origin of this shift around the 2008 financial crisis, arguing that in its aftermath, a more radical strand of left-wing activism (loosely associated with the Occupy Wall Street movement) recognized that street protest and electoral politics were slow and uncertain — but that corporate boardrooms, already frightened by the reputational and political fallout of the crisis, were comparatively easy to pressure. Rather than winning elections or building durable legislative majorities, activists could achieve faster results by pressuring a small number of powerful institutional gatekeepers — the exact WEF-to-ratings-agency-to-proxy-advisor-to-asset-manager loop traced in Part IV — to adopt their preferred standards.
4. **Why it kept scaling.** Once ESG scores became embedded in fund construction, executive compensation, lending terms, and insurance pricing, Ramaswamy argues a **self-reinforcing loop** took hold: companies adopted ESG-friendly policies not necessarily because their leadership held strong convictions about them, but because doing so lowered their cost of capital and reduced reputational risk, while resisting them raised both.
5. **The democratic harm.** Stakeholder capitalism, in his account, corrodes both systems at once: markets get worse at allocating capital efficiently (Friedman's worry), and democratic self-governance gets weaker because the public loses the ability to actually decide, through elections, questions that are increasingly being pre-settled by capital markets instead.

**The long-run vision critics like Ramaswamy describe:** if this trajectory continues unchecked, the critics' shared expectation is a gradual, largely invisible transfer of the *substance* of governance — the actual power to set binding social and environmental norms — away from legislatures, courts, and voters, and toward a small, self-selecting set of institutions that never faced an election and can't be removed by one. Because this transfer happens gradually, through mechanisms most citizens never directly observe (a fund's proxy vote, an ESG score's effect on a company's cost of capital), critics argue it's especially dangerous *precisely because* it doesn't look like a coup or a dramatic power grab — it looks like ordinary business.

## Part VIII — The broader pattern critics see: governance through institutions, not elections

Zooming out further than ESG alone, many of these critics — and the wider intellectual and political movement they're part of — read ESG as one instance of a larger phenomenon they believe characterizes contemporary governance generally: a shift from resolving contested public questions through **elected, accountable, revocable** processes (legislatures, courts, elections) toward resolving them through **networks of unelected institutions** that coordinate informally and are extremely difficult to vote out, reverse, or even fully see.

This is presented here descriptively — as how a significant number of critics interpret the larger pattern — not as a claim that the interpretation is correct.

In this reading, ESG sits alongside several other phenomena the same critics tend to group together as instances of the same underlying shift:

- **Financial "debanking" and payment-processor deplatforming** — Greenwald's core example, where access to financial infrastructure, rather than a court ruling or a law, determines whether an organization or individual can operate.
- **Content moderation on major technology platforms** — where a handful of companies, sometimes under direct or indirect government pressure, set the practical boundaries of acceptable public speech at a scale no single government censor historically could.
- **Standard-setting by international bodies** (the WEF, the UN, international accounting-standards boards) — which produce voluntary frameworks that, once adopted by enough large institutions, become de facto mandatory even without ratification by any national legislature.
- **Central bank and regulatory "mission creep"** — critics on this broader spectrum have also pointed to instances of financial regulators incorporating climate-risk mandates into bank supervision, treating a contested political question (the appropriate pace of decarbonization) as if it were a routine, technical safety-and-soundness matter.

The shared thread critics draw across all of these: in each case, a genuinely contested political question — how much should society restrict a legal industry, what speech should be permitted, how fast should decarbonization proceed — gets resolved not by a vote, but by a **coordinated set of institutions each acting within their normal, legal authority**, producing an aggregate outcome that no single actor could be blamed for and no single election could reverse. Ramaswamy's "money substitutes for votes" argument and Greenwald's "government censorship disguised as corporate autonomy" argument are, in this broader reading, the same structural complaint applied to two different domains — finance and speech — with Thiel's "cartel" framing and Sowell's "vision of the anointed" supplying the economic and epistemic diagnosis of why a comparatively small, credentialed, well-networked class of institutional actors ends up in this position in the first place.

Critics describe the long-run risk of this pattern, if it continues unchecked, as a kind of **quiet institutional entrenchment**: a society that still holds elections and still has legislatures, but where an increasing share of the decisions that actually govern daily life — what a company must disclose, what speech a platform will carry, what a bank will finance — has already been made elsewhere, by actors those elections can't reach.

## Part IX — A text diagram of the mechanism

Critics generally describe the pathway from idea to real-world corporate behavior as a loop with four stages. A simplified version, reconstructed from the institutional history in Part IV:

```
STAGE 1 — IDEA & VOCABULARY
World Economic Forum (Klaus Schwab) · UN Global Compact · UN PRI
        → defines "stakeholder capitalism" and coins/popularizes "ESG"
        → recruits the Big Four accounting firms to build common metrics
                        │
                        ▼
STAGE 2 — MEASUREMENT
MSCI · Sustainalytics · S&P Global · ISS ESG · Bloomberg
        → translate the WEF/UN vocabulary into proprietary scores
        → scores attach to individual companies, publicly and to clients
                        │
                        ▼
STAGE 3 — VOTE EXECUTION
ISS · Glass Lewis  (>90% of the proxy advisory market)
        → convert scores into concrete "vote yes / vote no" recommendations
        → institutional clients default heavily to these recommendations
                        │
                        ▼
STAGE 4 — CAPITAL ALLOCATION
BlackRock · Vanguard · State Street  (dominant holders in nearly every major public company)
        → vote proxies at scale, in line with recommendations
        → engage management directly; influence cost of capital, board seats, executive pay
                        │
                        ▼
CORPORATE BEHAVIOR CHANGES
        → Chief Sustainability Officer roles, ESG-linked executive comp,
          board diversity policies, emissions targets, disclosure practices
                        │
                        └──────────────► reinforces Stage 1's legitimacy,
                                          restarting the loop
```

**The critics' point in diagramming it this way:** no single arrow in this chain is, by itself, an act of government coercion — which is exactly why critics argue the system is so hard to challenge through ordinary political or legal means. Each institution is simply doing its normal job. The democratic-legitimacy objection (Ramaswamy), the cartel objection (Thiel), the state-corporate-fusion objection (Greenwald, applied by analogy), and the epistemic objection (Hayek and Sowell) are, in this reading, four different ways of describing the same structural fact: that a loop with real governing power over the economy runs entirely outside the loop of elections, legislatures, and courts that's supposed to be the source of legitimate authority in a democracy.

## A brief note on where this argument is most contested

Since you asked for the strongest form of the critics' case rather than a balanced treatment, it's worth being clear about where that case draws the most serious pushback, so you know where to dig if you want to test it:

- **Empirical scale claims.** Some economists dispute how much actual behavioral influence ESG scoring exerts over corporate decisions in practice, versus how much is symbolic or marketing-driven — arguing the "governance by markets" framing may overstate ESG's causal power relative to more mundane drivers like customer demand, litigation risk, or ordinary regulation.
- **The voluntary-vs-coercive distinction.** Defenders of stakeholder capitalism argue there's a meaningful difference between government coercion and companies or investors voluntarily choosing to reward or punish behavior with their own capital — which they'd characterize as markets functioning normally, not a democratic harm, even when the outcome resembles policy.
- **Whether shareholder primacy was ever truly "neutral."** Critics of the critics argue that treating shareholder-profit-maximization as the natural, apolitical baseline (against which ESG is a "departure") is itself a value-laden framing — that Friedman's own model already involved political choices about whose interests count.
- **The institutional history is already reversing.** Defenders would also note that much of the loop described in Part IV has visibly weakened since 2022 — Vanguard's 2022 exit from NZAM, BlackRock's January 2025 exit, ISS and Glass Lewis both revising their ESG-related voting guidelines under political and legal pressure in 2025–2026 — which they'd argue shows the system was never as self-reinforcing or unaccountable as the critics' model implies, since ordinary political and legal pressure proved able to unwind much of it within a few years.

These objections don't refute the tradition reconstructed above — they're the live disputes a historian of this school of thought should know exist. But understanding them wasn't the goal of this piece; understanding the critics' own reasoning, in the strongest and most coherent form available, was.

## Glossary

- **Knowledge problem** (Hayek) — the claim that the information needed to allocate resources well is too dispersed and localized for any central authority to gather and use effectively; the price system solves this without requiring anyone to have complete knowledge.
- **The pretense of knowledge** (Hayek) — Hayek's warning against mistaking the appearance of scientific rigor, especially in economics and social planning, for genuine predictive competence in complex systems.
- **Shareholder primacy / shareholder capitalism** (Friedman) — the view that a corporation's core obligation is to maximize returns to shareholders within the law, leaving broader social questions to democratically accountable government.
- **Stakeholder capitalism** (Schwab/WEF) — the view that corporations owe obligations to a wider set of parties (employees, communities, environment, customers), not just shareholders; formalized by the WEF's 2019 Davos Manifesto and endorsed separately by the U.S. Business Roundtable the same year.
- **Vision of the anointed** (Sowell) — Sowell's term for a class of credentialed elites who believe their judgment entitles them to override decentralized decisions made by ordinary people, in the name of a better social outcome.
- **Disparate-impact reasoning** — inferring discrimination or systemic bias from unequal statistical outcomes between groups, without necessarily ruling out other causal explanations; a pattern Sowell argues is applied too readily.
- **Cartel behavior** (Thiel's usage) — coordinated action among competitors that narrows genuine market competition, even without a formal agreement; applied by Thiel to coordinated ESG commitments among dominant asset managers, and by Texas et al. in their 2024 antitrust suit against BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street.
- **Censorship-industrial complex** (Greenwald) — Greenwald's term for the coordinated network of government actors, media, and technology platforms that achieves speech-restricting outcomes government could not pursue directly.
- **State-corporate fusion** — the pattern, central to Greenwald's work, of government achieving outcomes through pressure on nominally private, unaccountable intermediaries rather than direct, accountable state action.
- **Debanking** — denial or termination of banking/financial services to an individual or organization, sometimes alleged to be politically motivated rather than purely risk-based.
- **Limited liability** — the legal shield protecting a corporation's shareholders from personal responsibility for the company's debts and legal liabilities; central to Ramaswamy's "corporate bargain" argument.
- **Proxy advisory duopoly** — the market structure in which two firms, ISS and Glass Lewis, control more than 90% of the business of advising institutional shareholders how to vote at corporate meetings.
- **Robovoting** — the practice, criticized in 2025 congressional testimony, of institutional investors automatically following a proxy advisor's default voting recommendation without independent review.
- **Universal ownership** — the fact that very large diversified investors like index funds hold stakes across nearly the entire market, which supporters argue creates an incentive to care about systemic risks, and which critics argue creates unprecedented, concentrated voting power over the whole economy.
- **Governance by financial markets** — the critics' organizing concept for outcomes on contested social and environmental questions being effectively decided through capital allocation and shareholder voting rather than legislation.
