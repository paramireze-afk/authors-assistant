# The Back Door to War: The Revisionist Case on Pearl Harbor

## The charge

The indictment is simple. Franklin Roosevelt wanted war, his country didn't, and he got one anyway by making sure Japan threw the first punch.

Charles Beard, the most celebrated American historian of his generation, made the charge in 1948 in *President Roosevelt and the Coming of the War, 1941*. He accused Roosevelt of campaigning as a man of peace while secretly steering the nation into a war Congress never chose. Charles Tansill, a Georgetown diplomatic historian, gave the thesis its name in 1952: *Back Door to War*. Rear Admiral Robert Theobald, who was at Pearl Harbor when the bombs fell, went further. He concluded Roosevelt kept the Hawaiian commanders in the dark so the attack would succeed. Robert Stinnett, a decorated Navy veteran of the Pacific war, spent seventeen years digging through Navy archives and FOIA releases. In *Day of Deceit* (2000) he charged that the provocation was planned on paper fourteen months in advance and that the Japanese fleet was tracked to its launch point.

Behind the provocation, historians like William Appleman Williams saw something older: a century of American expansion across the Pacific that made collision with Japan close to inevitable. On this reading, Pearl Harbor was not where the war started. It was where Manifest Destiny ran out of ocean.

## Manifest Destiny goes to sea

William Appleman Williams, in *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959), argued that America never stopped expanding when it hit the Pacific coast. It simply traded the wagon for the warship. The frontier became the "Open Door": an informal empire of markets, bases, and coaling stations that had to keep growing to keep the American economy growing. Beard had made the same point in the 1930s, warning that the hunt for foreign markets would drag America into Asian wars.

The relationship with Japan began at gunpoint. In 1853, Commodore Matthew Perry sailed into Edo Bay with black-hulled warships, trained his guns on the shore, and told a closed nation it would open its ports or else. The unequal treaties that followed stripped Japan of control over its own tariffs and put Americans beyond the reach of Japanese courts. Japan's first lesson from America: the side with the modern guns writes the rules.

Then America kept coming west:

- **1867:** Alaska bought, Midway seized.
- **1893:** American sugar planters, backed by U.S. Marines, overthrew Hawaii's Queen Liliuokalani. Washington annexed the islands in 1898 over Japanese protest.
- **1898–1902:** America took Guam and the Philippines from Spain, then crushed a Filipino independence movement. Roughly 200,000 Filipino civilians died. American troops used water torture and burned villages.
- **1899–1900:** Secretary of State John Hay's "Open Door" notes declared China, Japan's giant neighbor, open for American business.

The man who drew the map was Captain Alfred Thayer Mahan. His *Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890) taught that great nations rise by owning the sea lanes and the islands along them. Theodore Roosevelt read it like scripture. In 1908 he sent sixteen white-painted battleships around the world, with a showy stop in Tokyo Bay, to let Japan know who owned the ocean.

By 1900 the U.S. flag flew from Alaska to Manila. Williams' conclusion was blunt: a power that treated the whole Pacific as its frontier was bound to run into the one Asian nation that had refused to become a colony.

## Japan learns the rules, then gets punished for them

Henry Scott Stokes, Tokyo bureau chief for *The New York Times* and *The Times* of London, spent five decades in Japan. In *Falsehoods of the Allied Nations' Victorious View of History* (2013) he argued that Japan did exactly what the West taught it to do: industrialize, arm, and take colonies. Taiwan in 1895. Korea in 1905–1910. Washington signed off. In the 1905 Taft–Katsura understanding, America accepted Japan's grip on Korea in exchange for Japan accepting America's grip on the Philippines.

Then, Stokes and others argue, every time Japan reached the table, the West moved it:

- **1905, Portsmouth.** Theodore Roosevelt brokered the end of the Russo-Japanese War. Japan won on the battlefield and lost at the table, denied an indemnity. Tokyo rioted.
- **1906, San Francisco.** The city school board segregated Japanese children. The following "Gentlemen's Agreement" choked off Japanese immigration.
- **1919, Versailles.** Japan asked for one sentence in the League of Nations covenant affirming racial equality. It won a majority of the votes. Woodrow Wilson, chairing the session, declared that it needed a unanimous vote and killed it. The same men who preached self-determination refused to say a Japanese was the equal of a white man.
- **1922, Washington Naval Conference.** Japan was locked into a 5:5:3 battleship ratio, permanently third behind Britain and America.
- **1924, Exclusion Act.** Congress barred Japanese immigrants outright. In Tokyo it was treated as a national humiliation; protest rallies were held and at least one man killed himself outside the American ambassador's residence.

All the while, the U.S. Navy was rehearsing war with Japan. **War Plan Orange**, drafted around 1906 and revised for three decades, imagined a blockade that would starve the Japanese home islands into submission. In 1941 the blockade came in the form of an oil embargo.

Indian jurist Radhabinod Pal, sitting as a judge at the Tokyo war crimes trials, put the double standard most sharply. In his 1,200-page dissent he argued that the Western powers had built their empires by conquest and had no standing to hang Japanese leaders for doing the same. To Pal and Stokes, Japan in the 1930s was claiming a Monroe Doctrine for Asia. Washington, which had its own in the Americas, called it aggression.

## A president who wanted war and a country that didn't

By the summer of 1940 France had fallen and Britain was fighting alone. Roosevelt was convinced America had to get into the war against Hitler. The American people were not. Through 1941, Gallup found roughly four in five Americans opposed to entering the war. The America First Committee had some 800,000 members.

Beard built his whole book on the gap between what Roosevelt said and what he did. Campaigning for a third term in Boston on October 30, 1940, the president told the nation's parents: "Your boys are not going to be sent into any foreign wars." Beard called the 1940 campaign a deception. He argued Roosevelt was already moving the country toward war while promising peace.

Tansill traced how Roosevelt fought an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic: fifty destroyers to Britain, Lend-Lease, the occupation of Iceland, American warships escorting British convoys, and in September 1941, "shoot on sight" orders against German submarines. The American destroyer *Greer* tracked a U-boat for hours, signaling its position to British aircraft, until the sub fired. Roosevelt went on the radio and called it an unprovoked attack.

But Hitler, bogged down in Russia, would not take the bait. He ordered his U-boat commanders to avoid incidents with American ships. The Atlantic door was shut.

That, Tansill argued, is why the president turned to the back door. Japan had joined Germany and Italy in the Tripartite Pact in September 1940. If Japan attacked America, the country would enter the war united, enraged, and with the president's hands clean. Congressman Hamilton Fish, Roosevelt's own representative from Hyde Park and his bitterest critic, put it plainest: Roosevelt needed a war, and Japan was the way in.

## The blueprint: the McCollum memo

Stinnett's centerpiece is a five-page memo dated October 7, 1940. Its author was Lieutenant Commander Arthur McCollum, head of the Far East desk at the Office of Naval Intelligence. McCollum was born in Japan to Baptist missionaries, spoke Japanese before he spoke English, and briefed the White House on Japanese intercepts. His memo laid out the problem coldly: the American public would not support war unless Japan attacked first. Then he listed eight steps to make it happen:

1. Arrange with Britain to use British Pacific bases, especially Singapore.
2. Arrange with the Netherlands to use bases and supplies in the Dutch East Indies.
3. Give all possible aid to Chiang Kai-shek's China.
4. Send a division of long-range heavy cruisers to the Orient.
5. Send two divisions of submarines to the Orient.
6. Keep the main strength of the U.S. fleet in the Pacific, around Hawaii.
7. Insist that the Dutch refuse Japan's demands for economic concessions, especially oil.
8. Completely embargo all U.S. trade with Japan, together with a British embargo.

Then the line Stinnett says gives the game away: "If by these means Japan could be led to commit an overt act of war, so much the better."

The memo went to Captain Dudley Knox and Captain Walter Anderson, two of Roosevelt's closest naval advisers. Knox wrote back that he agreed with the courses of action. Stinnett charges that over the next fourteen months all eight steps were carried out. The last one, the total embargo, came in July 1941, and Japan struck less than five months later.

Stinnett points to two episodes he says show Roosevelt was willing to spend American lives to get his incident:

- **The fleet as bait.** Roosevelt kept the Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor instead of its home base in San Diego. Its commander, Admiral James Richardson, protested that the anchorage was exposed and undersupplied. He took the complaint to the Oval Office in person in October 1940. Roosevelt fired him a few months later. Richardson later recorded the president's reply: that sooner or later the Japanese would commit an overt act against the United States.
- **Expendable cruisers.** In 1941 Roosevelt sent cruisers on "pop-up" runs into Japanese home waters, unannounced, to provoke a reaction. Stinnett quotes the president: he wanted the cruisers to keep popping up and didn't mind losing one or two. Admiral Kimmel protested that the missions were ill-advised and would provoke war.

## Tightening the noose

In 1941, the argument goes, Washington closed every exit but one.

**The oil cutoff.** After Japan moved troops into southern Indochina in July 1941, Roosevelt froze all Japanese assets in the United States. Britain and the Dutch East Indies followed. In practice it was a total oil embargo. Japan imported roughly 80 to 90 percent of its oil, most of it from America, and its navy had about two years of reserves. After that, its fleet would sit dead in the water. Herbert Hoover, in *Freedom Betrayed*, called the sanctions the decisive provocation. Short of shooting, he argued, they were war in every sense, and they left Japan two choices: give up its empire in humiliation, or seize the oil fields of the Dutch East Indies by force. Washington knew which it would pick.

**The peace offer nobody answered.** In August 1941, Prime Minister Fumimaro Konoe, the leader of Tokyo's peace faction, asked to meet Roosevelt face to face, in Honolulu or Juneau, wherever the president liked. Ambassador Joseph Grew begged Washington from Tokyo to take the meeting, warning that if Konoe failed, the militarists would take over. Secretary of State Cordell Hull demanded Japan concede the main points before any summit. Hoover called the refusal one of the great tragedies of the war. Konoe's government fell in October. General Hideki Tojo took his place.

**The ultimatum.** On November 26, after Washington quietly dropped a proposed three-month truce, Hull handed Japan his ten-point note. It demanded that Japan pull every soldier out of China and Indochina, recognize no Chinese government but Chiang Kai-shek's, which Tokyo read as abandoning its client regimes, and in effect walk away from the Axis. Hamilton Fish called it a war ultimatum hidden from Congress and the American people. Fish pointed out that Congress didn't learn the Hull Note's terms until after the bombs fell. The next day, Hull told Secretary of War Henry Stimson that he had washed his hands of it, and that it was now up to the Army and the Navy.

**The smoking gun.** The document Beard and every writer after him quote is Stimson's diary entry for November 25, 1941. That day Roosevelt met with his war council: Stimson, Hull, Navy Secretary Frank Knox, General George Marshall, and Admiral Harold Stark. Stimson wrote that the president raised the likelihood of an attack as soon as the following Monday. The question, Stimson wrote, was "how we should maneuver them into the position of firing the first shot without allowing too much danger to ourselves."

To Beard, that sentence settled it. This was not a government working to keep the peace. It was a war cabinet planning who would be blamed for starting the war.

## What Washington knew, and what Hawaii didn't

Provoking the attack is one charge. Theobald, Toland, and Stinnett go further: Washington saw it coming and let it land.

**The machines that never went to Hawaii.** By 1940, Army codebreakers had cracked Japan's top diplomatic cipher, code-named Purple. The decrypts, called MAGIC, gave Washington Tokyo's secret cables almost as fast as Japan's own diplomats read them. Purple decoding machines went to Washington, to the Philippines, and even to the British. Theobald hammered the point that none went to Pearl Harbor, the base of the fleet most likely to be hit.

**The bomb plot.** On September 24, 1941, Tokyo ordered its spy at the Honolulu consulate to divide Pearl Harbor into five zones and report exactly which warships were moored in each, down to which ones were tied up side by side. Theobald asked the obvious question: why would Japan need a target grid of one harbor unless it meant to bomb it? Washington decoded the message. Kimmel and Short never saw it.

**Warnings from every direction.** In January 1941, Ambassador Grew cabled that Peru's minister in Tokyo had heard Japan planned a surprise mass attack on Pearl Harbor. In August, British double agent Dusko Popov handed the FBI a German questionnaire, much of it about Pearl Harbor's defenses, airfields, and torpedo nets. J. Edgar Hoover sat on it. Toland, in *Infamy* (1982), added the "winds" message, a coded weather phrase in a Japanese news broadcast that would signal war with America. Captain Laurance Safford, the Navy's top codebreaker, swore under oath that it had been intercepted in early December. The copies, he testified, later vanished from Navy files.

**Tracking the strike force.** Stinnett went after the official story that the Japanese carriers crossed the North Pacific in total radio silence. Using intercept logs he pried loose through FOIA, he claimed the fleet did transmit and that U.S. stations in Hawaii, the Aleutians, and on the West Coast took bearings on it. Toland quotes a Navy intelligence man in San Francisco who said he plotted signals moving east toward Hawaii. And Stinnett points to a November 25 Navy order routing all U.S. and Allied shipping away from the North Pacific. He called it a "vacant sea" order: the waters the strike force sailed through were cleared so no merchant ship would stumble on it and raise the alarm.

**The last night.** Japan's fourteen-part reply to the Hull Note was decoded in Washington the evening of December 6. Roosevelt read the first thirteen parts and said, "This means war." No alert went to Hawaii that night. The next morning, General Marshall, by his own account, was out horseback riding. When his warning to Hawaii finally went out, it was sent by commercial telegram, not the Navy's radio or the scrambler phone on his desk. It reached Short's headquarters hours after the attack, carried by a Japanese-American messenger boy on a motorcycle.

**The ships that weren't there.** Theobald noted what the Japanese didn't find. The carriers *Enterprise* and *Lexington* had been ordered out of Pearl Harbor days earlier to ferry planes to Wake and Midway. *Saratoga* was in San Diego. What Japan caught was eight aging battleships, the kind of ship the attack itself proved obsolete. Theobald's conclusion: Washington left the old fleet as bait and got the new one out of the way.

**The fall guys.** Within weeks, the Roberts Commission found Kimmel and Short guilty of "dereliction of duty." Both were forced into retirement and spent the rest of their lives as the men who lost Pearl Harbor. Kimmel's *Admiral Kimmel's Story* (1955) laid out everything Washington knew and never sent him. His family fought for decades to clear his name. In 1999 the U.S. Senate voted that both men had been denied vital intelligence available in Washington and should have their ranks restored. No president has ever done so.

## Voices from inside the room

The case wasn't built by outsiders. Much of it came from men who held power at the time.

- **Herbert Hoover.** The former president spent two decades writing *Freedom Betrayed*. His family kept it locked away until 2011. In it, Hoover recounts a 1946 conversation with General Douglas MacArthur. Hoover said Roosevelt was a madman who wanted to get into the war. MacArthur, by Hoover's account, agreed, and added that the July 1941 sanctions were provocative enough that Japan was bound to fight even with suicide staring it in the face.
- **Oliver Lyttelton.** Churchill's Minister of Production told the American Chamber of Commerce in London in June 1944: "Japan was provoked into attacking the Americans at Pearl Harbor. It is a travesty on history ever to say that America was forced into the war." Under fire, he issued a clarification. But one of Britain's war ministers had said it, in public, with the war still on.
- **Justice Radhabinod Pal.** At the Tokyo war crimes tribunal, Pal dissented on every count and voted to acquit every defendant. On the Hull Note he quoted American writer Albert Jay Nock: even Monaco or Luxembourg would have taken up arms against the United States on receiving such a note. Japan built him a memorial at the Yasukuni Shrine.
- **Rear Admiral Robert Theobald.** He was commander of destroyers at Pearl Harbor that Sunday morning. In *The Final Secret of Pearl Harbor* (1954), he concluded that Roosevelt deliberately kept Hawaii blind so the attack would succeed. Kimmel wrote the foreword. So did Fleet Admiral William "Bull" Halsey, the most famous American admiral of the Pacific war, who called Kimmel and Short "military martyrs."
- **Admiral Husband Kimmel.** The fleet commander said it flat out in *Admiral Kimmel's Story* (1955): he and Short had been made the scapegoats for decisions taken in Washington.
- **Hamilton Fish.** Roosevelt's own congressman, from his home district in Hyde Park. In *Tragic Deception* (1983), Fish argued that the Hull Note was a war ultimatum concealed from Congress, and that the president lied the country into war.

The argument never went away. Harry Elmer Barnes gathered the early critics in *Perpetual War for Perpetual Peace* (1953). Its title became a slogan. Today, antiwar and libertarian writers treat Pearl Harbor as the original template for a pattern they see repeating: the Gulf of Tonkin in 1964, Iraqi weapons of mass destruction in 2003. In each, they argue, a reluctant public was maneuvered into war by an incident the government had invited, inflated, or invented.

## The end of the frontier

Put the authors side by side and the story runs in one line from Perry's warships to Hawaii.

Williams and Beard supply the motive: an American empire of markets and bases pushing west for a century, from Perry's black ships to Hawaii, Guam, Manila, and the Open Door in China. Stokes and Pal supply Japan's side: a nation that copied the Western model of empire, then was told the club was closed. It was refused racial equality, capped in battleships, barred from immigrating, and finally choked off from oil.

Tansill, Hoover, and Fish supply the method: a president who promised peace while waging an undeclared war, refused Konoe's summit, and handed Tokyo an ultimatum he knew it would reject. McCollum's memo, as Stinnett reads it, is the plan, and Stimson's diary is the confession. Theobald, Toland, and Stinnett supply the cover-up: the bomb plot message never sent, the warnings ignored, the carriers moved to safety, the telegram sent the slow way, and two commanders hung out to dry.

What followed, they note, was the old dream fulfilled. By 1945 Japan lay in ashes, two of its cities destroyed by atomic bombs, its constitution written by American officers, and the Pacific an American lake. Okinawa, Yokosuka, and bases strung from Guam to the Philippines still hold American troops today. Pearl Harbor was not the start of a defensive war. It was the last chapter of Manifest Destiny, and the bill for it was paid by 2,403 Americans killed that Sunday morning.
