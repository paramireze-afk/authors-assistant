## The bottom line

Campbell's headline view hasn't changed: **if the organism involved is ordinary plague (*Yersinia pestis*), the risk of a pandemic is very, very low.** Plague is well understood, treatable with antibiotics, and endemic in this part of Siberia.

What has changed is his confidence that it *is* ordinary plague. Several features of the case — and especially the scale of the Russian response — lead him to suspect something else may be going on. His working hunch is that the scientist **probably did not die of plague as we normally know it**, while stressing that this is an assessment, not a confirmed finding.

## What happened

A young laboratory worker, Daria, died of fatal pneumonia after an incident connected to the **Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute** in Siberia. According to Campbell, she was a qualified biomedical scientist — which he finds concerning in itself, since she would have understood the risks of her work better than most.

Reports of a **second death** circulated in several media outlets, but Russian officials have denied it. Officially, there is one death. Russia's public health watchdog says it has run thousands of tests and found no dangerous pathogens.

## The central unanswered questions

Campbell frames the story around a set of "known unknowns" — things we know we don't know:

- **What pathogen killed her?** Was it *Yersinia pestis*, a modified or weaponized strain, or an entirely different organism?
- **Has plague been confirmed or ruled out** by laboratory testing?
- **What do the post-mortem, microbiology, and genome sequencing show?** Campbell is confident Russian authorities have all of this by now; they simply haven't released it.
- **What was the route of exposure,** and what exactly happened in the lab?
- **Did containment and reporting procedures work?** On the evidence so far, he says, apparently not.

He is careful to say he is not singling out Russia: he argues that many Western governments have also withheld data, particularly around COVID. But in this case, Russia is the country holding the critical information.

---

## Why Campbell doubts it was ordinary plague

His reasoning rests on how the case unfolded:

1. **She would have known what to do.** A biomedical scientist aware of a lab exposure would know to take prophylactic antibiotics immediately.
2. **The incubation period was long.** A heavy lab exposure would be expected to cause illness quickly.
3. **Antibiotics should have worked.** Given her job, clinicians would almost certainly have tested for plague and given large doses of antibiotics once she fell ill.

He acknowledges an important caveat: someone who arrives acutely ill with **pneumonic plague can still die despite the best antibiotics**, because the disease is so aggressive. But in this case, she would likely have had days of warning in which to start treatment early. That's why the apparent failure of antibiotics stands out to him.

## The scale of the Russian response

This is the part Campbell finds most telling. Reported measures include:

- **Several hospitals across the city closed and quarantined**, apparently for **three weeks** — far longer than normal for plague.
- **Hospital staff quarantined.** Since medical staff often work across several hospitals, this could reflect staff movement — or possibly cases we haven't heard about.
- **Workers at a local aluminium plant told to wear masks.** Campbell notes this is reasonable for pneumonic plague, which spreads through large respiratory droplets rather than fine aerosols like COVID.
- **Public events cancelled**, including a citywide community clean-up, though other activities have gone ahead.
- **Doctors openly talking about plague.**
- **Mongolia and Kazakhstan** have reportedly introduced health surveillance at their borders with Russia.

### Why this matters

Plague is endemic in south-eastern Siberia near Mongolia. Local doctors will have seen cases before, and the protocols for a plague exposure are well rehearsed. Normally, cases there don't trigger this kind of reaction.

For context, Campbell points out:

- The US sees around **seven plague cases a year**, mostly linked to prairie dogs and their fleas, and there was a plague death in **Arizona in 2025**.
- The **last human-to-human plague transmission in the US was in 1924**.
- Plague outbreaks occur regularly in parts of Africa, India, and Asia.

So when authorities act as though they're dealing with a highly transmissible disease rather than a familiar, treatable one, Campbell concludes: **"they're not acting like it's plague; they're acting like it's something else."**

## Possibilities on the table

Campbell lists three broad scenarios, without favouring one definitively:

| Scenario | What it would mean |
|---|---|
| **Ordinary *Yersinia pestis*** | Low pandemic risk; controllable with antibiotics and standard measures. |
| **Antibiotic-resistant (possibly engineered) plague** | Would explain treatment failure. Senior doctors he consulted confirmed that engineering antibiotic resistance into bacteria is possible. |
| **A different agent, such as a virus** | Would put the situation in "a completely different category," depending on its transmissibility and lethality. |

He also raises the question of whether **person-to-person transmissibility** could have been deliberately enhanced — again stressing that this is possible in principle but unknown in this case.

## The lab's history

Campbell says that defectors from the Soviet intelligence services told the West at the end of the Cold War that this facility was part of the **Soviet biological weapons programme**. Its current name — an anti-plague research institute — sounds benign, but in his view it should be thought of as a **biolab** that likely studies a range of bacteria and viruses, not just plague.

### Is this connected to the war in Ukraine?

Campbell is clear that he sees **no direct link**. The lab is roughly **3,000 miles** from Ukraine, and a deliberate release that far away would make no military sense. His assessment is that this was **an accident**, not a deliberate release.

## A history of opacity

To explain his scepticism about official Russian statements, Campbell points to two historical cases:

**Sverdlovsk anthrax leak, 1979.** Anthrax spores escaped from a Soviet military facility. The CDC estimates **65–70 people** died from inhaled anthrax. The cause was officially denied until **1992**, when Boris Yeltsin acknowledged it came from military bioweapons work — **13 years** later.

**Chernobyl, 1986.** After the explosions and graphite fire released large amounts of radioactive material, the nearby town of Pripyat was evacuated only after about **36 hours**, and May Day events in Kyiv went ahead as fallout drifted over participants. Soviet authorities only began admitting what had happened when radiation alarms went off in **Sweden** and at **Sellafield** in West Cumbria, England, near where Campbell lives.

His prediction: Russian authorities will likely release information about the current incident **only if it becomes undeniable**.

## Key epidemiological numbers to watch

If a new, transmissible agent is involved, two numbers will matter most:

- **R (reproduction number):** how many people, on average, each infected person goes on to infect. An R of 3, 4, or 5 would be alarming.
- **Infection fatality rate:** what proportion of infected people die.

For now, neither is known — and we don't know whether such an agent exists at all.

### How pneumonic plague has behaved before

Campbell offers two benchmarks that explain why he isn't worried *if* this is ordinary pneumonic plague:

- **Madagascar, 2017:** a large outbreak of about **2,500 cases**, with an estimated R of **2.4** before it was brought under control, in a setting with weaker health infrastructure.
- **United States, 1900–2009:** CDC analysis of pneumonic plague reports found an R of about **1.18**, with notable outbreaks in San Francisco (1904), Oakland (1919), and Los Angeles (1924).

Both outbreaks were ultimately contained, even over a century ago in the US case.
