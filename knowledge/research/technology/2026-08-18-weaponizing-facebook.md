# When the Feed Became the Battlefield

In February 2011, a 59-year-old retired auto shop manager in rural Ontario named Janice Clinch spent her mornings doing something new: pinpointing the coordinates of Muammar Gaddafi's forces from Facebook posts and tweeting them at NATO, asking the alliance to "clean up" the targets. She had never been to Libya. She had become, in the words of the reporter who found her, a volunteer intelligence analyst — working from a laptop in the woods of eastern Ontario.[^globemail]

It's tempting to treat that image as the beginning of a story: ordinary people, a new technology, and war meeting for the first time. It isn't. Psychological operations, propaganda, mass surveillance, and covert influence campaigns are not inventions of the social media age — they are among the oldest tools of statecraft, refined continuously through the twentieth century by intelligence agencies, militaries, and the private contractors who serve them. What changed around 2009 to 2012 was not the existence of these techniques. What changed was the substrate they operated on. For the first time, propagandists, spies, and psychological-operations specialists had access to enormous, identifiable populations of real people, with their real social relationships mapped out in front of them, their moods and preferences logged in real time, reachable instantly and at almost zero marginal cost, through systems that could also be used to watch them. This article traces how that substrate got built, who noticed its value first, and how — piece by piece, contract by contract — older instruments of influence and surveillance were rebuilt for it. The record shows convergence more clearly than conspiracy: several institutions arriving, within a few years of each other, at the same realization, because the opportunity was sitting in plain view for anyone positioned to exploit it.

## Before Facebook: the psyops shop that saw it coming

The company that would later become notorious as Cambridge Analytica's parent did not start with Facebook data, and its methods were not invented for platforms at all. It started in a British military-contracting niche that predates the smartphone era entirely, and its history is the clearest illustration of the article's central claim: the toolkit came first; the platform came later.

In 1990, a British ad executive named Nigel Oakes founded the **Behavioural Dynamics Institute (BDI)**, a research outfit built around what marketers call "strategic communication" — applying psychological and behavioral science to shift attitudes at scale. Out of BDI grew **SCL Group** (Strategic Communication Laboratories), which by the mid-2000s was openly marketing itself, at a London arms fair, as the first private company to sell psychological-warfare services to a national military.[^bylinetimes] SCL went on to hold British Ministry of Defence contracts (2014–2015), a Foreign Office contract (2008–2009), and a Home Office contract (2009); it received provisional "List X" accreditation, meaning it was cleared to handle sensitive government material.[^medium-scl] It also trained the British Army's 15th Psychological Operations Group and, according to reporting by *Fast Company*, worked for the Norwegian government's defense research agency, Saudi Arabia, and multiple U.S. defense agencies.[^fastcompany-scl]

Critically, SCL's foundational client work — the field-testing ground where its behavioral-influence methodology was actually built and refined — was in Afghanistan and Iraq, what one industry account calls the "battle for hearts and minds."[^wsws-scl] According to reporting reviewed by journalists, this work was overseen, in part, by Michael Flynn, then director of intelligence for the international coalition force in Afghanistan — the same Michael Flynn who later became a Trump campaign surrogate and, briefly, national security adviser, and who advised SCL as it pursued government contracts after the 2016 election.[^medium-scl] A 2013 NATO strategic-communications document from the National Defence Academy of Latvia independently confirms SCL was conducting "qualitative data gathering" for coalition forces in Maiwand Province, Afghanistan, as early as 2010.[^opendemocracy-scl]

In 2012–2013, SCL spun up a U.S.-facing subsidiary aimed at electoral politics, funded by hedge-fund billionaire Robert Mercer and given its political direction by Steve Bannon. Its name was **Cambridge Analytica**.[^ca-org] It's worth being precise about what the documented record does and doesn't establish here, because this is one of the places where an exciting-sounding claim can outrun its evidence. What's well documented, from multiple independent sources, is *institutional and methodological lineage*: the same parent company, the same founder-level behavioral-science philosophy, some of the same personnel and advisory relationships (Flynn most notably), and a consistent industry-wide description of Cambridge Analytica's approach as psychological-operations doctrine repackaged for electoral marketing.[^occrp] What is not established by the public record is that any specific tool, dataset, or targeting model built for Afghan counterinsurgency was directly copied line-for-line into the Facebook-harvesting operation that targeted American and British voters in 2016. The honest description is that Cambridge Analytica *grew out of* a military-psyops firm and *inherited its behavioral-influence philosophy and personnel*, not that it performed a literal technology transfer from a specific battlefield system. That distinction matters, and it doesn't weaken the larger point — if anything, it sharpens it: an organizational capability built for counterinsurgency found a new, far larger, far more profitable substrate in commercial social media, and adapted to it.

## The Arab Spring: two governments discover the same substrate at once

By the time Cambridge Analytica was formally incorporated, the U.S. government had already spent several years experimenting, quite openly, with social media as a tool of foreign policy — not covertly, but as declared "21st century statecraft." This is the second major thread in the story, running in parallel to SCL's private-sector psyops trajectory, and it converges with it by the mid-2010s.

In 2009, the State Department appointed **Jared Cohen** — a young policy-planning staffer under both Condoleezza Rice and Hillary Clinton, later director of Google Ideas — as a point person on social media and youth outreach.[^npr-cohen] Cohen has publicly described leading a delegation of Silicon Valley executives, including Twitter co-founder Jack Dorsey, to Baghdad in 2009, and building a relationship with Dorsey that led, weeks later, to a real and documented episode: when Iran's disputed June 2009 election set off mass protests organized partly over Twitter, the State Department contacted Twitter and asked it to delay scheduled maintenance so the service would stay up during the unrest. Cohen has confirmed this happened in his own words on NPR.[^npr-transcript]

Alongside Cohen, **Alec Ross**, Secretary of State Hillary Clinton's senior adviser for innovation, built out what the department branded "21st century statecraft" — using platforms to support dissidents, connect activists, and promote what Clinton's State Department explicitly called "internet freedom" as a foreign-policy pillar.[^brookings-ross][^npr-ross] Cohen also co-founded **Movements.org** (the Alliance of Youth Movements), an organization that, according to contemporary reporting in the *Jerusalem Post*, explicitly taught activists worldwide, "in an explicit, step-by-step manner," how to use social media tools to organize protests — sponsored by corporate backers including Google, Facebook, YouTube, MSNBC, and Pepsi.[^jpost-youtube] It is documented, not speculative, that senior State Department officials were consciously building relationships with the platforms and training foreign activists in their use, years before Tunisia or Egypt erupted — meaning the U.S. government was actively cultivating this substrate as a policy tool before it had any idea a wave of uprisings was coming.

Then, in December 2010–February 2011, the uprisings actually broke out — first in Tunisia, then Egypt, then Libya, then across the region. In Egypt, the best-documented individual case is **Wael Ghonim**, a Google marketing executive based in Dubai, who anonymously ran a Facebook page called "We Are All Khaled Said," commemorating a young man beaten to death by police. Academic case studies of the page's content confirm Ghonim spent months building an audience and gradually normalizing political engagement before calling for the January 25, 2011 protests that toppled Hosni Mubarak.[^researchgate-ghonim] Ghonim was arrested and detained by Egyptian state security during the uprising; his tearful television interview after release is widely credited with re-energizing the protests.[^britannica-ghonim] It's worth being precise about what this case does and doesn't show: Ghonim was a private individual acting, by his own account, independently — not on Google's or any government's instructions — but his employer's product was the platform, and his position (a Google employee with international visibility, at the exact moment the State Department was actively courting Silicon Valley executives for foreign-policy purposes) is itself part of the story of how tightly Silicon Valley personnel and Middle East activism had become intertwined by 2011, even without evidence of direct coordination in his specific case.

Libya is the case where the military and intelligence dimension is most directly documented, and it's the clearest single example of the substrate shifting from an organizing tool into a targeting-adjacent one within the same conflict. As the civil war escalated, a crowdsourced project called the **Libya Crisis Map** — created at the UN's request, using the same "Ushahidi" crisis-mapping technology built for the 2010 Haiti earthquake — began aggregating Twitter reports from opposition-held areas.[^fp-libya] Reporting by *Foreign Policy*, drawing on a paper co-authored by an analyst from the State Department's own Office of Intelligence and Threat Analysis, found that the map's content shifted over time from humanitarian information toward messages of direct tactical value to the NATO-led no-fly-zone operation — reports like "Government tanks posted at all entrances to Ajdabiyah" or "Greek vessel arrives in Tripoli delivering weapons."[^fp-libya] Separately, NATO officials confirmed on the record, in a 2011 briefing, that the alliance's intelligence "fusion center" used open-source Twitter data to generate "usable intelligence," and a NATO official told *Technology Review* directly that the alliance had taken advantage of civilian social-media reporting coming out of Libya in a way it had never had access to before, while acknowledging that the question of civilians effectively helping identify bombing targets was "an uncomfortable subject" for the organization.[^atlanticcouncil-nato] This is about as close as the public record gets to a documented, on-the-record admission that a military alliance used crowdsourced social media data operationally in a live air campaign — the same platforms the State Department had spent two years cultivating as organizing tools were, within months, feeding a targeting picture for an actual bombing campaign.

So by 2011, two things were true simultaneously and were not, as far as the documented record shows, run from a single coordinated plan: the U.S. State Department had spent two years deliberately cultivating platforms and training activists as an instrument of "soft power," while separately, ordinary users and a NATO intelligence "fusion center" were, in real time, converting the same platforms into targeting-adjacent tools for an actual air war. Whether these threads were coordinated at a higher level than what's documented is speculative; that both were happening, openly, in overlapping time and space, on the same infrastructure, is not — and it's the moment every serious national-security institution watching from the sidelines took note.

## What Snowden showed: the intelligence agencies moved fast to catch up

Two years after Libya, Edward Snowden's 2013 disclosures gave the clearest documentary evidence yet that intelligence agencies had, by then, built dedicated infrastructure for treating social media as both a surveillance target and a battlespace to shape — and, in at least one case, showed a direct institutional line back to the Arab Spring as the trigger.

**PRISM**, revealed by *The Washington Post* and *The Guardian* in June 2013 via a leaked 41-slide NSA presentation, described a program under which the NSA obtained data — including stored communications — from nine major U.S. internet firms: Microsoft, Yahoo, Google, Facebook, PalTalk, AOL, Skype, YouTube, and Apple.[^npr-prism] It's important to be precise about what is and isn't established here: the companies uniformly denied giving the NSA "direct" or "unfettered" server access, and the exact mechanism of access was genuinely disputed in the reporting.[^wapo-prism] What is not disputed — confirmed later on the record by NSA general counsel Rajesh De before the U.S. Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board — is that the data was obtained through a compulsory legal process under Section 702 of the FISA Amendments Act, with the "full knowledge" of the companies involved, even if "PRISM" was an internal codename the companies say they didn't recognize by that name.[^theregister-prism] Facebook is one of the nine companies named. PRISM represents the surveillance half of this story — hoovering up what people already said on these platforms — as distinct from the influence-operations half described below.

That influence-operations half runs through **GCHQ's Joint Threat Research Intelligence Group (JTRIG)**. A leaked 2011 GCHQ internal document, "Behavioural Science Support for JTRIG's Effects and Online HUMINT Operations," written by psychologist Mandeep K. Dhami, laid out the unit's toolkit and its underlying behavioral-science research agenda for manipulating online discourse.[^intercept-jtrig] Other leaked JTRIG materials, published by NBC News and the ACLU, list specific named tools: UNDERPASS (manipulating the outcome of online polls), SLIPSTREAM (artificially inflating website page views), GESTATOR (amplifying chosen messages on YouTube), SILVERLORD (removing content GCHQ deemed "extremist"), SPRING BISHOP (finding private photographs of targets on Facebook), and CHANGELING (spoofing any email address).[^spyscape-jtrig] JTRIG also reportedly used sexual "honey traps" and denial-of-service attacks against targets including hacker collectives.[^nbc-jtrig] The Intercept's Glenn Greenwald, who worked directly with these documents, has published the "Art of Deception" training manual in full.[^intercept-manipulate] Read against SCL's Afghanistan-era behavioral-influence work, JTRIG is the same basic idea — state-run psychological effects operations, run by professionals with behavioral-science training — rebuilt for a platform environment where the targets are identifiable individuals reachable at scale rather than an occupied population reachable through leaflets and radio.

A related, more narrowly scoped GCHQ program called **Squeaky Dolphin**, revealed in a 2014 NBC News report based on a leaked August 2012 presentation, demonstrated GCHQ's ability to monitor YouTube views, Facebook "likes," and Blogger/Blogspot visits in real time, using commercially available analytics software (a version of Splunk).[^nbc-squeakydolphin] The presentation is titled "Psychology: A New Kind of SIGDEV [Signals Development]." One detail from Wikipedia's summary of the NBC reporting is the clearest documented cause-and-effect link in this entire story: a 2012 UK parliamentary intelligence oversight report had specifically criticized British intelligence agencies for missing the early warning signs of the Arab Spring uprisings and called for improved "global" monitoring capability — and the Squeaky Dolphin demonstration to American counterparts came later that same year.[^wiki-squeakydolphin] In other words, the Arab Spring didn't just demonstrate the platforms' power to activists and diplomats; it demonstrated a capability gap to the intelligence services that had missed it coming, and they built new real-time social-media monitoring infrastructure specifically in response.

## The Pentagon's own sock puppets

While GCHQ was building "Effects" tools, the U.S. military was doing something structurally similar — and it, too, traces back to an older discipline (persona-based deception and covert messaging) rebuilt for the new substrate, discovered publicly through a hacking incident rather than an official disclosure.

In February 2011, *The Guardian* revealed that U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM) — which oversees U.S. operations across the Middle East and Central Asia — had awarded a $2.76 million contract to a Los Angeles firm called Ntrepid to build "online persona management" software.[^boingboing-sockpuppet] The system, according to the contract terms reported by multiple outlets, would let a single U.S. service member operate up to 10 separate fake online identities, each with a "convincing background, history and supporting details," with up to 50 controllers working simultaneously, using randomized IP addresses and "traffic mixing" for what the contract literally called "cover and powerful deniability."[^huffpost-sockpuppet] CENTCOM's spokesman said publicly that the tool was intended for foreign-language (Arabic, Farsi, Urdu, Pashto) counter-propaganda only, and that using it against English-speaking or domestic U.S. audiences would be unlawful.[^boingboing-sockpuppet]

The story broke into public view largely because of a separate 2011 incident: the hacker collective Anonymous breached the servers of a security firm called **HBGary Federal** and published roughly 70,000 internal emails. Those emails showed HBGary had, separately, been developing its own persona-management software and had proposed a campaign — for a law firm representing Bank of America and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce — to target journalists (including Glenn Greenwald) and progressive activist groups.[^rawstory-hbgary] Journalist and Anonymous-adjacent researcher **Barrett Brown** built a crowdsourced research project, Project PM, to mine both the HBGary emails and, later, emails hacked from the private intelligence firm Stratfor, to document the broader private "cyber-industrial complex" — for which Brown was later prosecuted and served roughly four years in federal prison, on charges ultimately centered on sharing a hyperlink and threatening an FBI agent rather than on the hacking itself.[^democracynow-brown] Then-CENTCOM commander General David Petraeus told the Senate Armed Services Committee the persona-management program was meant to "counter extremist ideology and propaganda"; independent reporting could not confirm whether firms linked to the HBGary "dirty tricks" campaign had deployed similar tools domestically, though researchers flagged the concern.[^uncommonthought] Taken together with JTRIG, this is the second, parallel national intelligence apparatus — this time American — building the same basic capability (deniable, industrial-scale fake-persona operations) at almost exactly the same moment as its British counterpart, using contractors rather than in-house staff.

## The venture-capital layer: In-Q-Tel

A separate, better-documented thread concerns **In-Q-Tel**, the CIA's own venture capital arm, founded in 1999 to fund technology of intelligence interest — and it shows the same capability being built a third way: not through a contractor doing psychological operations, and not through an intelligence unit doing "Effects," but through direct investment in the commercial analytics companies built to mine the platforms themselves. Its investment record is public, and it is substantial: a 2016 document obtained by *The Intercept* under public records requests showed at least four In-Q-Tel portfolio companies — Dataminr, Geofeedia, PATHAR, and TransVoyant — specifically built tools to mine and analyze social media data (Dataminr, for instance, visualizes Twitter data streams to flag breaking events for government, financial, and corporate-security clients).[^intercept-inqtel] In-Q-Tel's earlier investment in a firm called Visible Technologies, which specialized in real-time sentiment analysis of social media chatter, was reported by *The Telegraph* as early as 2009 — meaning the CIA's venture arm was funding social-media-mining technology before the Arab Spring had even happened, in parallel with, not merely in reaction to, the State Department's own courtship of the platforms.[^cbs-cia-socialmedia]

Here, it is important to be precise about a claim that circulates widely online but is not well supported: that Facebook itself was directly funded or created with CIA/In-Q-Tel money. Even sources sympathetic to that broader narrative concede the documented link is, in their own words, "tenuous" — Facebook's early $12.7 million funding round came from Accel Partners, whose manager James Breyer later joined Facebook's board and had separately served on In-Q-Tel's board, which is a network overlap, not documented direct investment in Facebook by the CIA's venture fund.[^corbett-inqtel] The clearer, better-documented story is In-Q-Tel's investment in the *analytics companies that mine* platforms like Facebook and Twitter — Palantir (co-founded by Peter Thiel, an early Facebook board member, and backed in part by In-Q-Tel) being the most prominent — rather than the platforms themselves.[^gaia-inqtel]

## Cambridge Analytica goes public

By 2018, all three of these threads — the psyops-contractor lineage, the state's cultivation of the platforms, and the intelligence community's own social-media capability-building — had been running in parallel, mostly out of public view, for the better part of a decade. Cambridge Analytica is the moment the private-sector version of this story broke fully into public view.

The public reckoning came in March 2018, when *The Observer* (The Guardian's Sunday edition) and *The New York Times* simultaneously published the account of **Christopher Wylie**, Cambridge Analytica's former director of research, based on documents and testimony he had provided to journalist Carole Cadwalladr over roughly a year of reporting.[^cnbc-wylie] Wylie's account, later confirmed in substantial part by Facebook itself and by UK and U.S. congressional hearings, described how a Cambridge University researcher, Aleksandr Kogan, built a Facebook personality-quiz app called "thisisyourdigitallife." Under Facebook's data-sharing policies at the time, the app didn't just collect data on the roughly 270,000 people who took the quiz — it pulled data from their friend networks too, eventually reaching an estimated 50 to 87 million Facebook profiles, depending on the estimate cited at different points in the investigation.[^cambridgeanalytica-wylie][^pbs-wylie] That data was passed to Cambridge Analytica, then working for Trump campaign chairman Steve Bannon, and used to build "psychographic" targeting models for the 2016 campaign; Wylie also testified about parallel use in the Brexit "Vote Leave" campaign via a Canadian firm, AggregateIQ.[^globalnews-wylie]

Facebook suspended Wylie's own accounts within days of the story breaking, saying it could not restore them while he refused to cooperate with the company's internal review — a decision widely criticized at the time as retaliatory, given Wylie was the whistleblower rather than the wrongdoer.[^fortune-wylie] Wylie testified before both British Parliament and the U.S. Senate Judiciary Committee in May 2018.[^pbs-senate] Mark Zuckerberg testified before Congress the following month. Cambridge Analytica itself filed for insolvency in May 2018, though — as reporting by the anti-corruption outlet OCCRP later documented — a former employee opened a successor UK firm within a week that went on to sell similar "behavioral modification" training to, among others, the Canadian and Dutch militaries, underscoring that the underlying capability didn't disappear with the company's brand; it simply changed letterhead and kept selling the same institutional know-how to new government clients.[^occrp]

## DARPA tries to build a science of it

While the CIA was investing and GCHQ was operating, the Pentagon's research arm was trying to formalize the whole domain into an actual field of study — a fourth, distinctly transparent, government approach to the same underlying capability. In July 2011, DARPA launched the **Social Media in Strategic Communication (SMISC)** program with roughly $42–50 million in funding over several years.[^ipa-smisc] SMISC's stated goals, in DARPA's own language, were to (1) detect and track the spread of ideas and deceptive messaging, (2) recognize "persuasion campaign structures and influence operations," (3) identify who was behind them and measure their effects, and (4) build capability for automated content generation and bot detection.[^darpa-smisc][^cccblog-smisc] The program explicitly included research into automated bot detection and, in 2015, ran a public "Twitter Bot Detection Challenge" aimed at identifying bots spreading anti-vaccination content — with university teams from Indiana, USC, Georgia Tech, and elsewhere competing for a roughly $4 million prize pool.[^dartmouth-bot] DARPA has since followed SMISC with a successor program, INCAS, aimed at detecting foreign "geopolitical influence campaigns" with "quantified confidence."[^sociable-incas] Unlike the JTRIG and CENTCOM programs, SMISC's basic-research orientation and public grant structure make it one of the more transparent parts of this entire story — the government told the public, in detail, what it was building and why, years in advance, framing it explicitly as both offense (understanding how to run influence campaigns) and defense (detecting when adversaries do the same).

## The story doesn't stop in 2011 — it professionalizes

The most important corrective to any narrative that treats this as a single early-2010s moment is a 2022 investigation showing the U.S. military's own social-media influence operations continued, and matured considerably, for a full decade afterward — the clearest evidence that what began as improvised contracts (Ntrepid, HBGary) evolved into a durable, ongoing capability.

In August 2022, researchers at the **Stanford Internet Observatory**, working with the commercial analysis firm **Graphika**, published "Unheard Voice," based on data voluntarily provided by Twitter and Meta after both platforms had independently taken down networks of accounts for "coordinated inauthentic behavior."[^lawfare-pentagon] The scope was substantial: Twitter's dataset covered nearly 300,000 tweets from 146 accounts active between March 2012 and February 2022; Meta's dataset covered 39 Facebook profiles, 16 pages, two groups, and 26 Instagram accounts active from 2017 to mid-2022.[^cyberscoop-meta] Some accounts used AI-generated ("deepfake" or GAN-generated) profile photos — a technical sophistication the 2011 Ntrepid contract never had access to — and some shared links to websites whose footers openly disclosed sponsorship by U.S. Central Command, while others obscured that origin over time.[^theintercept-mktashif] Neither Twitter nor Meta formally attributed every account to the U.S. government, but Meta specifically confirmed U.S. military involvement in significant parts of the network, and the accounts' focus tracked closely with CENTCOM's geographic area of responsibility — the Middle East and Central Asia, the exact same theater as the original 2011 persona-management contract.[^cyberscoop-meta] The disclosure prompted Colin Kahl, then U.S. under secretary of defense for policy, to order an internal Pentagon review of all clandestine military social-media influence operations.[^spokesman-pentagon] Reporting in 2026 by *The Intercept* found that even after funding controversies, some of the propaganda websites tied to this network simply rebranded rather than shutting down — Al-Shorfa became Al-Mashareq, Mawtani became Diyaruna — continuing to carry (now-quieter) CENTCOM sponsorship disclosures.[^intercept-2026]

This is arguably the single best-documented data point for answering the question of *whether* the U.S. government continued running large-scale covert social-media influence operations well past the Arab Spring era: independent, non-governmental researchers, working from platform-provided data the platforms themselves generated by enforcing their own rules, found a decade-long, evolving operation running continuously from the same year CENTCOM's original sock-puppet contract was signed. It is not speculation; it is confirmed by the platforms and by an internal Pentagon review that the finding was serious enough to warrant.

## What Scott Horton and his guests have said about this

Antiwar.com editorial director and longtime foreign-policy podcaster **Scott Horton** has covered several strands of this story directly over more than a decade of interviews, generally from a non-interventionist, civil-libertarian editorial position critical of both the surveillance state and the wars it accompanied.

On Libya specifically — the case study where this article found the clearest documented link between social media and live military targeting — Horton interviewed **Alan J. Kuperman**, a University of Texas public affairs professor, twice: first in April 2011, while the war was still underway, when Kuperman argued in a Boston Globe op-ed that the NATO bombing of retreating Libyan forces and of Gaddafi's home city of Sirte (populations with no plausible threat to civilians) showed the "protect civilians" justification was pretext for regime change,[^scotthorton-kuperman2011] and again in February 2015, when Kuperman revisited the intervention's rhetorical bait-and-switch and expressed surprise that Russia had gone along with a UN resolution he considered transparently a cover for "sacking Tripoli."[^scotthorton-kuperman2015] Horton also interviewed Cato Institute scholar **Doug Bandow** in January 2020 under the title "America Wrecked Libya for a Generation," in which Bandow traced how the "protect civilians" framing was used specifically to secure Chinese and Russian acquiescence at the UN Security Council before the mission expanded into regime change.[^scotthorton-bandow] None of these interviews focus on social media's technical role the way this article does; their relevance here is that they document, from primary interview subjects, the propaganda and justification apparatus that surrounded the same 2011 Libyan intervention in which crowdsourced social media was separately confirmed to have fed NATO's targeting picture — two different layers (rhetorical justification and tactical intelligence-gathering) of the same information war.

On the Snowden disclosures specifically, Horton interviewed *NSA: The Untold Story* author **James Bamford** in September 2014 about NSA-Israel intelligence-sharing arrangements revealed in Bamford's *New York Times* reporting,[^scotthorton-bamford] interviewed former CIA officer **Philip Giraldi** in July 2013 defending Snowden as a whistleblower rather than a traitor,[^scotthorton-giraldi] and later interviewed **Glenn Greenwald** directly (January 2024) about the legacy of the Snowden leaks and what happened to the archive of documents after the initial reporting.[^scotthorton-greenwald] On the HBGary/persona-management/Barrett Brown thread specifically, Horton interviewed WhoWhatWhy's Christian Stork in August 2013 about the documented connections between the HBGary hack, Anonymous, and Brown's prosecution,[^scotthorton-stork] and later interviewed Brown himself around his December 2014 sentencing hearing.[^douglaslucas-media] On Arab Spring-era government funding of activist networks specifically, a 2026 episode of the *Crossing Faiths* podcast featuring Horton includes a guest ("Pinna") who describes firsthand experience working with government-funded organizations during the Arab Spring, with Horton providing critical framing about the "military-industrial complex" and what he calls "manufactured intelligence" as drivers of the interventions that followed.[^crossingfaiths] A separate, more recent interview with Barrett Brown — on a different, affiliated program, *The Free Thought Project Podcast*, rather than Horton's own show — has Brown discussing Peter Thiel's Palantir and its ties to the intelligence community as a modern successor to the earlier HBGary-era "cyber-industrial complex," though that particular conversation is not documented as having aired on the Scott Horton Show itself and is noted here only for completeness.[^spotify-brown]

It's worth being clear about the nature of this material: Horton's program is an independent, opinionated foreign-policy talk show, not an investigative outlet breaking primary documents itself — its value here is as a long-running record of skeptical, non-mainstream synthesis and interviews with primary figures (Greenwald, Bamford, Brown, Giraldi, Kuperman, Bandow) who *were* directly involved in reporting or living through these events. Horton's own framing is explicitly critical of the national-security state; that's a stated editorial position, not a claim this piece is endorsing or rejecting, but it's useful context for weighing how his coverage selects and frames the underlying documented facts above.

## So when did it happen — and what was actually new?

Putting the timeline side by side:

- **1990–2010:** A British psy-ops contracting industry (BDI/SCL) develops behavioral-influence techniques in Afghanistan and Iraq, well before smartphones and Facebook feeds were significant vectors — documented by contracts, NATO reports, and multiple independent journalistic investigations. The methodology predates the platform by two decades.
- **2009–2010:** The U.S. State Department begins deliberately using platforms as declared instruments of foreign policy ("21st century statecraft," Movements.org), while asking Twitter directly to stay online during Iran's 2009 protests — publicly acknowledged by the officials involved. The CIA's venture arm is separately already funding social-media-mining startups.
- **2011:** The Arab Spring shows, in real time and in front of the whole world, that these platforms could organize mass mobilization (Egypt) and generate militarily useful open-source intelligence (Libya) — documented by NATO's own on-record admissions and a State Department analyst's co-authored study. In the same year, CENTCOM's persona-management contract and the DARPA SMISC program are both publicly disclosed, showing the Pentagon moving, openly and in parallel, to build both offensive and defensive social-media capability.
- **2012–2014:** GCHQ's JTRIG "Effects" operations and Squeaky Dolphin real-time monitoring are built and later exposed by Snowden — with a documented institutional link back to intelligence agencies having been caught flat-footed by the Arab Spring itself.
- **2013–2016:** SCL/Cambridge Analytica migrates its Afghanistan/Iraq-honed behavioral-influence philosophy into commercial electoral politics — first documented by industry reporting, later confirmed in exhaustive detail by whistleblower testimony and congressional hearings.
- **2018:** The public learns the scale of what had been happening to their own data, via Christopher Wylie's disclosures.
- **2022:** Independent researchers, using platform-provided data, confirm the U.S. military ran a decade-long, evolving covert social-media influence campaign that had continued the entire time — meaning this was never a closed chapter from the Arab Spring era, but an ongoing, maturing capability.

None of the individual techniques in this timeline are new. Psychological operations date at least to the world wars; propaganda dates to antiquity; signals intelligence and mass surveillance are a twentieth-century intelligence-agency staple; persona-based deception and disinformation are tradecraft as old as espionage itself. What changed, and what this record actually shows changing, is the substrate all of it now runs on: a small number of commercial platforms holding identifiable real-name populations, their genuine social graphs, their revealed preferences and emotional reactions, updating in real time, distributed by opaque algorithms that can be studied, gamed, or purchased, and reachable — for the price of a contract, a research grant, or a data-sharing agreement — at a scale and precision that no leaflet drop, radio broadcast, or door-to-door canvass ever offered. SCL's Afghanistan playbook, GCHQ's honey traps, CENTCOM's fake personas, and the CIA's sentiment-analysis investments are all, at bottom, old capabilities. Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube didn't invent what they were used for. They simply became the largest, richest, best-instrumented population ever made available to the people whose job is to watch and move that population — which is exactly why every institution in this story reached for the same substrate within a few years of each other, largely without needing to coordinate at all.

If there's a single honest answer to "who learned to weaponize social media first and most effectively," the documented record doesn't point to one villain or one master plan. It points to several institutions — a British psy-ops contracting industry, the U.S. State Department's public diplomacy arm, GCHQ, CENTCOM, and eventually campaign consultancies working for both major U.S. parties and in elections worldwide — arriving at broadly the same insight at nearly the same time (roughly 2009–2012), because the underlying opportunity was sitting in plain sight for anyone with the incentive and the institutional tools to exploit it. What's genuinely disputed, and where this piece has tried to flag the line clearly, is how *centrally coordinated* any of this was across institutions — the documented record shows parallel, overlapping, sometimes contracting-driven convergence far more clearly than it shows a single command structure directing all of it.
