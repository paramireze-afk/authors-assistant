# Why They Chant: A Century of U.S. Policy Toward Iran and the Logic of Blowback

*This piece presents the case as non-interventionist critics like Scott Horton and Daryl Cooper make it. It doesn't quote them; it lays out the argument in the spirit of their work. A section at the end covers the strongest counterarguments, and items marked **⚑** are flagged as contested or debated.*

---

## The Question Nobody in Washington Asks

When an American president cites Iran's "Death to America" chant, it's offered as self-explanatory proof that Iran's rulers are irrational fanatics who hate America for what it is. The non-interventionist tradition asks a different question: **what did we do?**

That isn't the same as saying America deserves to be hated, or that Iran's government is good. It's the question any serious strategist should ask about an adversary: what grievances does the other side hold, which are real, and which of our own policies keep producing new enemies?

The CIA itself has a word for this. **"Blowback"** first appeared in a 1954 internal CIA history of the operation that overthrew Iran's government the year before. The term meant the unintended consequences of covert operations, which the American public would experience without ever knowing their cause. Political scientist Chalmers Johnson later built a book around the idea, published a year before 9/11.

The story of Iran is where the word was born. It's a good place to start.

## Part I: The Iran Story

### 1953: The Original Sin

In 1951, Iran's parliament voted to nationalize the country's oil industry, which had been controlled by the British-owned **Anglo-Iranian Oil Company** (later BP). The company took the lion's share of the profits from Iranian oil. Prime Minister **Mohammad Mossadegh**, a secular nationalist, was enormously popular for standing up to it.

Britain responded with an embargo and pressed Washington for help. The Eisenhower administration, framing Mossadegh as a communist risk, agreed. In August 1953 the CIA and British intelligence organized **Operation Ajax** (TPAJAX): paying crowds, bribing officers and politicians, and planting propaganda. Mossadegh was overthrown and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. **⚑1**

The U.S. government officially acknowledged the CIA's role in documents declassified in 2013, sixty years after the fact. Iranians never needed the confirmation.

For Horton and those in his camp, this is the root: Iran had a functioning, if messy, constitutional politics, and the United States destroyed it to help a British oil company. Every later Iranian government would point to it.

### 1953–1979: The Shah and SAVAK

With Mossadegh gone, **Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi** ruled as an increasingly absolute monarch for twenty-six years, armed and backed by Washington. His secret police, **SAVAK**, trained with American and Israeli help, became notorious for surveillance, imprisonment, and torture of dissidents.

A pivotal moment came in 1964, when the Shah's government granted U.S. military personnel legal immunity in Iran. A then-obscure cleric named **Ruhollah Khomeini** denounced it as a "capitulation" that put Americans above Iranian law. He was exiled for it, and the speech made him a national figure.

To the non-interventionist reading, the U.S. spent a quarter-century tying itself to an unpopular ruler, so when that ruler fell, America fell with him.

### 1979: Revolution and Hostages

The Shah was overthrown in early 1979. In October, the Carter administration admitted him to the U.S. for cancer treatment. Many Iranians, remembering 1953, feared Washington was preparing to restore him again. In November, students stormed the U.S. embassy and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days.

Horton-style accounts don't excuse the hostage-taking. They do insist on the sequence: Americans remember 1979 as the beginning. Iranians remember it as the middle.

This is the heart of the "history starts in 1979" problem. In the American telling, Iran attacked us out of nowhere. In the Iranian telling, they finally threw off a dictatorship the U.S. had imposed.

### 1980–1988: Backing Saddam

In September 1980, Iraq's **Saddam Hussein** invaded Iran. The war lasted eight years and killed hundreds of thousands of people, with trench warfare, human-wave assaults, and attacks on cities.

The United States tilted toward Iraq:

- In 1982 it removed Iraq from the list of state sponsors of terrorism, opening the way for aid.
- In December 1983, special envoy **Donald Rumsfeld** met Saddam in Baghdad to improve relations.
- The U.S. provided Iraq with credits, dual-use technology, and battlefield intelligence.
- Iraq used **chemical weapons** against Iranian troops and, at Halabja in 1988, against Iraqi Kurdish civilians. Declassified CIA files reported by *Foreign Policy* in 2013 indicate U.S. officials provided targeting intelligence in 1988 knowing Iraq was likely to use chemical weapons in those offensives. **⚑2**

At the same time, in what became the **Iran–Contra** scandal, the Reagan administration secretly sold weapons to Iran, partly to free hostages held by Hezbollah in Lebanon and partly to fund Nicaraguan rebels. Critics see in this the hallmark of the era: not a coherent strategy, but cynical manipulation of both sides.

Iran's own leaders, including Parliament Speaker Ghalibaf, who fought as an IRGC commander in that war, still invoke it today. Thousands of Iranian veterans live with the lung damage from mustard gas. To them, the U.S. was a co-belligerent.

### 1988: Iran Air Flight 655

In July 1988, during U.S. naval operations in the Persian Gulf, the cruiser **USS Vincennes** shot down **Iran Air Flight 655**, a civilian Airbus on a scheduled route, killing all **290 people aboard**, including 66 children. The ship was inside Iranian territorial waters at the time.

The U.S. called it a tragic mistake. It never formally apologized, though it paid compensation in 1996 without admitting liability. The ship's commander later received a medal for his service during that tour. **⚑3**

Most Americans have never heard of it. Every Iranian has.

### The 1990s: Dual Containment and Sanctions

After the Cold War, the Clinton administration adopted **"dual containment"** of both Iraq and Iran, building up a permanent U.S. military presence in the Gulf. Sanctions on Iran tightened throughout the decade, including the 1996 Iran and Libya Sanctions Act.

Iran's reformist president **Mohammad Khatami**, elected in 1997, called for a "dialogue of civilizations" and made overtures to the American people. Non-interventionists argue these openings were largely squandered.

### 2001–2003: Cooperation, Then "Axis of Evil"

After 9/11, Iran, which considered the Taliban an enemy, actually **helped the United States**. Iranian diplomats played a constructive role at the December 2001 Bonn conference that set up Afghanistan's post-Taliban government, a story told by U.S. envoy James Dobbins.

A month later, in his January 2002 State of the Union address, President George W. Bush named Iran part of an **"Axis of Evil."** Iranian reformists who had argued for engagement were humiliated.

In May 2003, a proposal reached the State Department via the Swiss ambassador in Tehran outlining a possible **"grand bargain"**: talks covering the nuclear program, support for Hezbollah and Hamas, and recognition of a two-state solution, in exchange for security guarantees and lifted sanctions. The Bush administration didn't respond. **⚑4**

For Horton, this is one of the great missed chances: the moment when Iran was most willing to deal, and the U.S. was most sure it didn't need to.

### 2003: The Iraq War Hands Iran Its Greatest Victory

This is perhaps Horton's signature argument about the region. When the U.S. invaded Iraq in 2003, it toppled Iran's worst enemy — the very man it had backed against Iran in the 1980s — and then installed in power the **Shi'ite exile parties** that had spent the Saddam years based in or allied with Iran, including SCIRI and the Dawa party.

In other words, the United States fought a war that turned Iraq from Iran's chief rival into a partner. Horton argues the Bush administration essentially did Tehran's work for it, then blamed Iran for its growing influence.

Iran also backed militias that fought U.S. troops during the occupation, supplying explosively formed penetrators (EFPs) that killed hundreds of American soldiers. Non-interventionists acknowledge this, and their point is that American soldiers were there to be targeted only because of a war that never needed to be fought.

The occupation also gave birth to al-Qaeda in Iraq, which later became **ISIS**. That's blowback of a different kind, but from the same decision.

### 2007–2015: The Nuclear Question

In 2007, a **U.S. National Intelligence Estimate** concluded with high confidence that Iran had halted its nuclear weapons program in 2003. Hawks were furious; the report helped head off a war in Bush's last years.

In the years that followed, Iran's nuclear scientists were assassinated, and the U.S. and Israel deployed the **Stuxnet** computer worm against Iran's enrichment centrifuges. Sanctions grew tighter.

In 2015, the Obama administration and other world powers reached the **Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA)**. Iran accepted strict limits on enrichment and the most intrusive inspections in the world in exchange for sanctions relief. The IAEA repeatedly verified Iranian compliance.

### 2018–2020: Tearing It Up

In 2018, President Trump withdrew from the JCPOA and imposed **"maximum pressure"** sanctions. Iran waited about a year, then began exceeding the deal's limits, eventually enriching uranium to 60%.

In January 2020, a U.S. drone strike in Baghdad killed **Qassem Soleimani**, commander of the IRGC's Quds Force. Iran retaliated with a missile strike on U.S. troops at Ain al-Asad in Iraq. In the tense hours that followed, Iranian air defenses mistakenly shot down **Ukraine International Airlines Flight 752**, killing 176 people — a grim mirror of Flight 655.

The non-interventionist verdict: the U.S. had a working, verified agreement limiting Iran's nuclear program and threw it away. Everything after was a choice.

### 2025–2026: Open War

In June 2025, Israel launched a major air campaign against Iran, and on June 22 the United States bombed Iran's nuclear sites at Fordow, Natanz, and Isfahan. A ceasefire followed after twelve days.

The current war, which brought a U.S. blockade, a crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, and a still-disputed memorandum of understanding signed in June 2026, is in this view the logical endpoint of seventy years of policy: each act of pressure producing the hostility that justifies the next one.

## Part II: The Wider Pattern — Other Lessons That Should Have Been Learned

The non-interventionist argument isn't only about Iran. It's that the same mistake keeps repeating across the region.

### Lebanon, 1982–1983: The Birth of Hezbollah

Israel invaded Lebanon in 1982, besieging Beirut. **Hezbollah** emerged in the Shi'ite south largely in response to the Israeli occupation, with Iranian support. U.S. Marines deployed to Beirut as peacekeepers but came to be seen as taking sides, especially after U.S. warships shelled Druze and Shi'ite positions.

In October 1983, a truck bomb destroyed the Marine barracks, killing **241 U.S. servicemen**. President Reagan withdrew the Marines a few months later — a decision non-interventionists often cite approvingly as the rare time a president cut losses.

Hezbollah has been in conflict with Israel ever since. Israel's occupation of southern Lebanon lasted until 2000, and Hezbollah credits itself with ending it.

### Afghanistan, 1979–2001: Arming the Mujahideen

Beginning under Carter and expanding massively under Reagan, the CIA funneled billions of dollars, through Pakistan's intelligence service, to Islamist fighters resisting the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan. Carter's national security adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski later said the aid began before the Soviet invasion and that he was unbothered by its long-term consequences.

The Arab volunteers drawn into that war formed the network that would become **al-Qaeda**. When the Soviets left, Afghanistan collapsed into civil war, and the Taliban emerged from it.

### 9/11 and Osama bin Laden's Stated Reasons

Horton returns again and again to this point: **bin Laden told us why.** In his 1996 and 1998 declarations, he cited three main grievances:

1. U.S. troops stationed in Saudi Arabia after the 1991 Gulf War
2. The sanctions and bombing of Iraq through the 1990s, which UN agencies linked to large numbers of child deaths **⚑5**
3. U.S. support for Israel against the Palestinians

The goal, in Horton's reading, was to provoke the U.S. into wars across the Muslim world that would bleed it and radicalize the region. The U.S. obliged.

Political scientist **Robert Pape**'s research on suicide attacks supports the broader point: he found that the common thread in suicide terrorism is less religion than military occupation of territory the attackers consider their homeland.

### Libya, 2011

NATO's intervention to topple Muammar Gaddafi, sold as a humanitarian mission, left Libya a failed state, with rival governments, militias, and open-air slave markets. Weapons from Gaddafi's arsenals spread across North Africa and the Sahel. The 2017 Manchester Arena bomber was the son of a Libyan family linked to anti-Gaddafi fighters.

The lesson that should have been learned: having already watched Iraq collapse, the U.S. did it again.

### Syria, 2011–2024

Through the CIA program **Timber Sycamore** and allied Gulf states, the U.S. helped arm Syrian rebels in an effort to topple Bashar al-Assad. Many of those weapons and fighters ended up with jihadist factions, including al-Qaeda's Syrian affiliate. Horton has called this one of the most indefensible episodes of the War on Terror: the U.S. effectively on the same side as the group that attacked it on 9/11.

### Yemen, 2015–Present

The U.S. backed Saudi Arabia's war in Yemen with weapons, intelligence, and, for years, midair refueling. The Saudi blockade and bombing campaign helped produce one of the world's worst humanitarian crises. It also strengthened the very group the war was meant to defeat: Ansarallah (the Houthis) emerged as a regional military power capable of striking Saudi oil facilities and shipping.

## Part III: The Cooper Angle — Stories and Mythmaking

Daryl Cooper's contribution to this conversation is less about policy and more about **narrative**. Across his *Martyrmade* series, his recurring theme is that nations and movements act according to the stories they tell about themselves, and that the most dangerous conflicts happen when two peoples hold incompatible stories about the same events.

Applied to Iran, the Cooper-style question would be: what is the story each side tells?

- **The American story** begins in 1979: a hostage crisis, a fanatical regime, "Death to America," terrorism, nuclear ambitions. Iran is a threat that must be contained.
- **The Iranian story** begins in 1953, or earlier with British oil concessions: a coup, a dictator imposed from abroad, a war in which America helped Saddam gas them, a civilian airliner shot down, sanctions, assassinations, bombings. America is an empire that never stopped trying to control them.

Each side's story is built from real events, and each leaves out the events that complicate it. The chant in Trump's post is, in this reading, the product of the second story, and it can't be understood without it.

Cooper also tends to emphasize the human texture beneath the geopolitics: the generation of Iranian commanders now running the country — Ghalibaf among them — were young men in the trenches of the Iran–Iraq War. Their worldview was formed by a war in which, as they see it, the whole world sided with Saddam and Iran survived alone. That experience shapes how they read American promises today.

## Part IV: The Counterarguments

The non-interventionist case is powerful, but it has serious critics. Any honest reading should weigh these.

**Iranian agency.** The Islamic Republic is not simply a reaction to America. It's a revolutionary theocracy with its own ideology, which from the start rejected both the U.S. and the Soviet Union. Critics argue that blowback framing turns Iranians into objects of American action rather than actors responsible for their own choices.

**The regime's record.** Iran's government has executed thousands of political prisoners (notably in the 1988 mass executions), crushed repeated protest movements (2009, 2019, 2022), and is among the world's leading executioners. Many Iranians who suffered under the Shah suffered just as much or more under the Islamic Republic.

**Terrorism and proxies.** Iran and Hezbollah are linked by investigators to attacks far removed from any U.S. provocation, such as the 1994 AMIA bombing of a Jewish community center in Buenos Aires, which killed 85 people. The U.S. has also tied Iran to the 1996 Khobar Towers bombing. Iran's support for armed groups across the region — Hezbollah, Iraqi militias, the Houthis, Hamas — has fueled wars that killed many civilians.

**The 1953 debate.** Historians like Ray Takeyh argue that the coup succeeded mainly because of Iranian actors, including the clergy, the army, and royalists who opposed Mossadegh, and that the CIA's role, while real, has been overstated. **⚑6**

**The 1979 debate.** Similarly, many historians see the Shah's fall as driven chiefly by domestic forces — rapid modernization, inequality, religious opposition — rather than by resentment of the U.S. alone.

**Deterrence and nuclear risk.** Supporters of pressure argue that Iran's expansion of enrichment to 60%, which is close to weapons-grade, shows the danger was real, and that sanctions and military action, not restraint, slowed it down. Critics of the JCPOA argued its time-limited "sunset clauses" would have let Iran expand its program legally within a decade. **⚑7**

**"Why do they hate us" cuts both ways.** Critics note that bin Laden's grievances also included broad religious and civilizational claims, and that withdrawing from the region doesn't necessarily end hostility. Some point to the rise of ISIS after the 2011 U.S. withdrawal from Iraq as evidence that disengagement can also produce blowback.

**Selective history.** Just as hawks start the story in 1979, critics say non-interventionists sometimes start it in 1953 and minimize everything after. Both are choosing where the story begins.

Whatever one concludes, the core non-interventionist challenge stands as a question worth asking: if the goal is fewer enemies, have seventy years of coups, sanctions, wars, and assassinations produced more of them or fewer?

## ⚑ Flagged for Your Attention

**⚑1 — The communist-threat rationale.** Whether Mossadegh posed a real risk of a communist takeover (the Tudeh Party was active) is still debated. Most historians see it as an exaggerated justification, but not all do.

**⚑2 — U.S. knowledge of chemical weapons use.** The 2013 *Foreign Policy* report on declassified CIA files is well sourced. What's debated is how much U.S. intelligence directly enabled chemical attacks, versus general battlefield support.

**⚑3 — Vincennes details.** The ship's commander, Will Rogers III, received the Legion of Merit for his overall tour, not specifically for the shootdown, though critics see the timing as telling. A famous line attributed to George H.W. Bush about never apologizing for America was said in 1988 but in a different context; it's often misquoted as a direct response to the shootdown.

**⚑4 — The 2003 "grand bargain."** It's disputed whether the proposal had the full authority of Iran's Supreme Leader or reflected one faction's (and possibly the Swiss intermediary's) drafting. Its significance depends on that question.

**⚑5 — Iraq sanctions death tolls.** Early UNICEF-linked estimates of around 500,000 child deaths have been challenged by later research suggesting Saddam's government manipulated survey data. The suffering was real; the scale is contested.

**⚑6 — Who really made the 1953 coup.** A live scholarly debate (Kinzer and Abrahamian vs. Takeyh and others), not a settled question.

**⚑7 — JCPOA effectiveness.** Whether the deal was the best available outcome or a flawed one that delayed the problem is one of the most politically contested foreign policy debates of the last decade.

**⚑8 — On the sources.** Scott Horton and Daryl Cooper are both influential and both polarizing. Cooper in particular drew wide criticism in 2024 for remarks on World War II history in an interview with Tucker Carlson. If you present this piece publicly, it's worth being clear that it reflects their analytical tradition on U.S. foreign policy, not an endorsement of everything either has said.
