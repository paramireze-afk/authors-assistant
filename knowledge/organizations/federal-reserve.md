# The Federal Reserve System

## What Griffin Says It Actually Is

Griffin opens the book with a claim designed to unsettle the reader before any argument has even been made: the Federal Reserve is not federal, it holds no reserves, and the Federal Reserve Banks are not, in any meaningful sense, banks. His full definition, stated bluntly, is that the Federal Reserve System is **a legal, private banking cartel** — a group of competing banks that agreed to stop competing with each other, dressed the arrangement in the language of government, and had Congress make that arrangement enforceable by law. Everything else in the book is Griffin building the evidentiary case for that one sentence.

He is explicit that a "cartel" is not a loaded word chosen for effect — it is a precise description. A cartel, in his usage, is a group of businesses that agree to (1) maximize profits by minimizing competition among themselves, (2) make it difficult for new competitors to enter the field, and (3) use the police power of government to enforce the arrangement. He argues the Federal Reserve satisfies all three conditions exactly, and that this — not the stabilization of the economy — was its actual design purpose from the beginning.

## Origins: The Journey to Jekyll Island

Griffin's account of the Fed's creation is deliberately told as a detective story, and it opens the book for a reason: he believes the *circumstances* of its founding are themselves the strongest evidence of what the institution really is.

**The setting.** In November 1910, Senator Nelson Aldrich's private railcar picked up a group of men at a New Jersey station late at night. The passengers had been instructed to use only first names, to arrive separately so as not to be seen together, and to pretend not to know each other if they crossed paths. They travelled under this secrecy for a thousand miles to Jekyll Island, Georgia — a private hunting resort co-owned by J.P. Morgan — where they remained for roughly a week, guarded by a specially screened staff so that no one present would later be able to identify them.

**Who was there.** Griffin identifies the seven attendees and their affiliations:

| Attendee | Affiliation |
|---|---|
| Nelson W. Aldrich | Senate Republican "whip"; Chairman, National Monetary Commission; associate of J.P. Morgan; father-in-law to John D. Rockefeller, Jr. |
| Abraham Piatt Andrew | Assistant Secretary of the U.S. Treasury |
| Frank A. Vanderlip | President, National City Bank of New York; representing William Rockefeller and Kuhn, Loeb & Co. |
| Henry P. Davison | Senior partner, J.P. Morgan Company |
| Charles D. Norton | President, First National Bank of New York (a Morgan bank) |
| Benjamin Strong | Head of Bankers Trust Company (a Morgan company) |
| Paul M. Warburg | Partner, Kuhn, Loeb & Co.; representative of the Rothschild banking dynasty; brother of Max Warburg, head of the German/Dutch Warburg banking consortium |

Griffin notes that these seven men were estimated to represent **one-fourth of the total wealth of the entire world**.

**What they actually did.** Contrary to the "duck hunting" cover story given to local reporters, Griffin states plainly that the purpose of the meeting was to draft the blueprint for a banking cartel. He identifies **five objectives** the group agreed on at Jekyll Island:

1. Stop the growing competition from the nation's newer banks.
2. Obtain a franchise to create money out of nothing for the purpose of lending.
3. Get control of the reserves of all banks, so that the more reckless ones would not be exposed to currency drains and bank runs.
4. Get the taxpayer to pick up the cartel's inevitable losses.
5. Convince Congress that the purpose was to protect the public.

Griffin's verdict, decades later: judged against these five actual objectives rather than its official rhetoric, the Federal Reserve "has been an unqualified success."

## From Secret Plan to Federal Law

### The Aldrich Bill fails — in public

The plan drafted at Jekyll Island was first introduced publicly as the **Aldrich Bill**. Griffin recounts that Paul Warburg, whom he portrays as the meeting's real strategist, wanted the legislation named something reassuring like the "National Reserve Bill," precisely to evoke government and safety in the public mind. Aldrich, out of personal vanity, insisted his own name be attached instead. Warburg's judgment proved correct: the Aldrich name was too closely tied to Wall Street, and the bill became an easy political target. Congressman Charles Lindbergh, Sr. denounced it on the House floor as "the Wall Street Plan." The Aldrich Bill never came to a vote.

### The same plan, repackaged

Griffin's account of the 1912 election is central to his argument that the outcome was engineered rather than democratic. He documents that Wall Street financiers backed *all three* major candidates simultaneously — Republican incumbent William Howard Taft, Progressive challenger Theodore Roosevelt (bankrolled substantially by J.P. Morgan lieutenants), and Democrat Woodrow Wilson (whose campaign was heavily funded by figures connected to Kuhn, Loeb and the National City Bank). Whichever candidate won, Griffin argues, the financial interests behind the Jekyll Island plan would have a friendly president. Taft, who had declined to champion the Aldrich Bill, lost. Wilson, already publicly committed to the cause, won.

The legislation was then reintroduced under Democratic sponsorship — Congressman Carter Glass and Senator Robert Owen — as the **Glass-Owen bill**, carefully repackaged with populist-sounding "compromises" that, in Griffin's assessment, changed the substance very little while giving reform-minded Democrats (notably William Jennings Bryan) political cover to support it.

### Final passage

Griffin gives the exact mechanics of passage: the bill was released from the joint House-Senate conference committee on **December 22, 1913**, timed so that Congress — eager to leave for Christmas recess — would not linger over debate. It passed the House **282 to 60** and the Senate **43 to 23**. President Wilson signed the **Federal Reserve Act** into law the next day, December 23, 1913.

Griffin closes the chapter with the line that gives it its title: **"The Creature had swallowed Congress."**

## What the Fed Is, Structurally

Griffin devotes an appendix ("Structure and Function of the Federal Reserve System") to laying out the legal architecture, because he considers the structure itself proof of the cartel thesis — a hybrid designed to look like a public institution while functioning as a private one.

| Component | Function, per Griffin |
|---|---|
| **Board of Governors** | Seven members appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, serving staggered 14-year terms (so no single president can pack the Board). Sets national monetary policy. The Chairman, serving a 4-year term, is "the single most powerful influence within the system." |
| **12 Regional Reserve Banks** | Located in Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, and San Francisco. Legally structured as corporations whose stock is held by the member commercial banks in each district. |
| **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)** | The Board of Governors plus five of the twelve regional bank presidents on a rotating basis — except the President of the **New York** Reserve Bank, who has a permanent seat. This is the body that actually creates and extinguishes money through the buying and selling of securities. |
| **Member (commercial) banks** | Hold the stock of the regional Reserve Banks and elect most of their directors — but the stock "carries no proprietary interest, cannot be sold or pledged as collateral, and does not carry ordinary voting rights." Each member bank gets one vote regardless of size. |

Griffin's summary of the ownership question is deliberately paradoxical: the federal government owns no stock in the System, which technically makes it "privately owned" — but the private stockholders don't really own or control it in any ordinary sense either. His conclusion: "it is a cartel, and its organizational structure is uniquely structured to serve that end." He also notes that FOMC policy meetings are held in secret, with only a brief summary released to the public six weeks later and full transcripts historically destroyed — a level of secrecy he points out exceeds that of the CIA.

## How It Creates Money: The Mandrake Mechanism

*(For the full moral and mechanical breakdown of this process, see the companion note "Usury.")* In brief, Griffin identifies three methods by which the Fed expands or contracts the money supply:

1. **The Discount Window** — direct loans to member banks.
2. **The Open Market Committee** — buying and selling government bonds and other securities; Griffin calls this the most powerful of the three tools.
3. **Reserve requirement changes** — altering how much member banks must hold against deposits, which changes how much they can lend.

The core mechanism, which he names the **Mandrake Mechanism** after the comic-strip magician who creates things out of nothing: the government issues bonds; the Fed buys them with a check backed by nothing; those bonds are relabeled "reserves"; the banking system then creates roughly nine additional dollars in loans for every one dollar of reserves. Griffin's one-sentence summary of the entire System: "If you understand this paragraph, you understand the Federal Reserve System."

## The Incentives That Drive the Cartel

### The bailout game

Griffin frames the Fed's actual operating incentives through an extended sporting-event analogy he calls **"The Name of the Game Is Bailout."** Because banks can create loanable money at almost no real cost to themselves, the incentive is always to make loans as *large* as possible — a billion-dollar loan to a foreign government is no harder to process than a small loan to a local merchant, and it's far more profitable. He lays out the recurring "plays" he says characterize this system:

- **The Perpetual-Debt Play** — banks don't actually want loans repaid, since repayment just means finding a new borrower; they prefer collecting interest indefinitely while the principal is continuously "rolled over."
- **The Debt Roll-Over Play** — when a borrower can't pay, the bank simply extends a new loan to cover the old one, keeping the original "asset" on the books.
- **The Up-the-Ante Play** — the bank lends still more, ostensibly to cover interest, but also enough extra to keep the borrower compliant.
- **The Rescheduling Play** — lower rates, longer terms, cosmetic relief that makes real repayment of principal even less likely.
- **The Protect-the-Public Play** — when default finally becomes unavoidable, the bank and the government jointly warn Congress that letting the loan fail would harm "the public," securing a taxpayer-funded bailout instead.

He quotes Walter Wriston, then chairman of Citicorp, effectively admitting this dynamic regarding government debt: he proposed that, in an honest world, every Treasury note would have to state plainly that it will be repaid only with the proceeds of a new note sold when the old one comes due.

### Case studies

Griffin documents this "protect the public" pattern with specific bailouts, most drawn from Chapter 3 ("Protectors of the Public" — a deliberately ironic title):

| Case | Year | Outcome, per Griffin |
|---|---|---|
| **Penn Central Railroad** | 1970 | Banks whose officers sat on Penn Central's board drove it into bankruptcy while extending further loans so it could pay dividends and prop up its stock price — which bank insiders then sold before the collapse became public. Congress granted $125 million in loan guarantees. |
| **Lockheed** | 1971 | Congress guaranteed $250 million in new loans; Lockheed was then granted non-competitive defense contracts to ensure it could repay them. |
| **New York City** | 1975 | Congress authorized $2.3 billion in direct loans, more than doubling the city's existing debt, on the argument that default would embarrass America internationally. |
| **Chrysler** | 1978–79 | Congress guaranteed $1.5 billion in loans; the banks converted part of their exposure into stock, whose value rose on the news, largely offsetting their write-offs. |

His conclusion from this record, stated as the **Second Reason to Abolish the Fed**: "Far from being a protector of the public, it is a cartel operating against the public interest."

## Its Relationships to Other Actors

### To politicians and government

Griffin frames the Fed–Congress relationship as a mutually convenient partnership: the banking cartel gets a permanent, compounding claim on the nation's wealth in the form of interest on money it creates from nothing; Congress gets unlimited spending power without the political cost of raising taxes, since the resulting inflation functions as a hidden, undebated tax on the public.

### To commercial banks

Member banks are the Fed's actual constituency, in Griffin's account — the System exists to protect them from the two things that most threaten a fractional-reserve bank: competition from newer, more conservative banks, and currency drains caused by a loss of public confidence. He describes state-chartered "non-national" banks growing to hold a majority of deposits by 1913, a trend the Jekyll Island group saw as an existential threat that had to be reversed.

### To taxpayers and the public

The public's role, in Griffin's framework, is to absorb the System's losses (through bailouts) and its costs (through inflation) while being told, at every stage, that the arrangement exists to protect them. The chapter title "Protectors of the Public" is used pointedly — Griffin's evidence throughout is that the public is the one constituency the System is *not* structured to protect.

### To financial markets and the international banking community

Through the figure of Benjamin Strong — one of the original seven at Jekyll Island and the first, dominant Governor of the New York Fed — Griffin traces a direct institutional link between the Federal Reserve and the Bank of England. He describes Strong coordinating closely with the Bank of England's Montagu Norman, including a 1925 decision to expand the U.S. money supply specifically to help Britain defend the pound and fund its social programs, at the expense of American economic stability.

## Role in Economic Cycles: Boom, Bust, and "The Great Duck Dinner"

Griffin illustrates his central claim about the business cycle with a parable that gives Chapter 23 its title. A farmer, unable to catch wild ducks by force, instead spends a season scattering free corn by his pond. The ducks grow trusting, complacent, and too fat to fly. When winter comes and the pond freezes, they can't escape — and the farmer eats well all season. Griffin states directly: "That is the story of America's Great Depression of the 1930s."

He supports this with two specific episodes:

- **The 1920–21 Agricultural Depression.** Griffin cites Congressman Charles Lindbergh, Sr.: "Under the Federal Reserve Act, panics are scientifically created; the present panic is the first scientifically created one, worked out as we figure a mathematical problem." He also quotes Senator Robert Owen — a co-author of the Federal Reserve Act who later regretted his role — describing a secret Federal Reserve Board meeting on May 18, 1920, after which a deliberate contraction of credit destroyed $15 billion in national production and $20 billion in land values, wiping out heavily indebted farmers and the small country banks that had lent to them.

- **The Crash of 1929.** Griffin presents what he calls circumstantial evidence that the Federal Reserve and the Bank of England concluded at a secret February 1929 meeting that a market collapse was both inevitable and desirable, after which advisory warnings quietly went out to preferred insiders to exit the market while the public was reassured the economy was sound. On August 9, 1929, the Fed raised the bank-loan rate and began selling securities — both money-supply-tightening moves. The market collapsed on October 29.

His conclusion, stated as the **Sixth Reason to Abolish the Fed**: it destabilizes the economy — not as an occasional failure, but as the predictable result of any small group attempting to override the natural forces of supply and demand across an entire monetary system.

## Role in Financing War: The Rothschild Formula

In Part III of the book, Griffin argues that the ability to create fiat money is not incidental to war financing — it is what makes prolonged, large-scale war financially possible in the first place. He constructs a hypothetical doctrine he calls **the Rothschild Formula**, built from the historical behavior of European central banking:

1. War is the ultimate test of any government — everything else, including solvency, will be sacrificed for survival.
2. A government's debt can always be expanded simply by involving it in war or the threat of war.
3. If a credible enemy doesn't already exist, one can be created by financing the rise of a hostile regime.
4. A government that refuses to finance war through debt must be replaced — by insurrection, revolution, or assassination if necessary — with one that will.
5. No side should be allowed a decisive victory; financing *both* sides preserves perpetual instability and perpetual debt.

Griffin is careful to frame this as a hypothetical construct rather than a documented conspiracy — he explicitly notes it's doubtful the formula was ever written down or passed along as doctrine — but argues the historical record behaves exactly as if it had been:

- **England, 1689–1815:** Following the founding of the Bank of England (created explicitly to finance a war), England was at war 63 of the next 126 years.
- **World War I:** Griffin documents J.P. Morgan & Co. financing England and France, and argues that America's entry into the war (following the sinking of the *Lusitania*) served to protect those loans once German victory began to look likely.
- **The Bolshevik Revolution:** Griffin presents evidence that Wall Street financiers — including Jacob Schiff of Kuhn, Loeb — funded the Bolshevik faction, with a Red Cross mission in Moscow serving as cover, arguing this fits the Formula's logic of manufacturing a durable "credible enemy" for the twentieth century.

His conclusion, stated as the **Fifth Reason to Abolish the Fed**: it encourages war, because the Mandrake Mechanism is what makes war debt possible on a scale that direct taxation never could support.

## Role in Political Power: The "New World Order"

Griffin extends his argument beyond economics into what he presents as a coordinated push toward global governance, tracing this through the **Council on Foreign Relations (CFR)** and its intellectual roots in Britain's **Fabian Society**. He states that CFR membership runs through American presidents, cabinet officials, Federal Reserve board members, major bank and media leadership — describing the group, without exaggeration in his view, as "the hidden government of the United States."

He documents this theme through:

- **Bretton Woods (1944):** The IMF and World Bank were architected by Fabian socialist John Maynard Keynes and U.S. Treasury official Harry Dexter White — the latter simultaneously a CFR member and, per Griffin, a member of a Soviet espionage ring.
- **CFR figures on record:** Griffin quotes CFR founder John Foster Dulles (1939) calling for a "dilution... of the sovereignty system" and a common global currency, and National Security Adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski (1970) calling for "greater American sacrifices" toward a "new world monetary structure."
- **JFK's 1963 IMF speech**, in which Kennedy described the "conscious and deliberate" redistribution of the world's financial resources away from the United States.

For Griffin, the Federal Reserve is the domestic engine that makes this larger project possible — it is the mechanism by which American wealth and sovereignty can be transferred abroad "disguised as loans," setting the template later extended globally through the IMF and World Bank.

## History Before the Fed: America's Three Prior Central Banks

Griffin insists the Federal Reserve is not America's first experiment with central banking — it is the **fourth**, and he argues each of the first three ended for reasons that should have served as a warning.

| # | Institution | Dates | Key figures | How it ended, per Griffin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bank of North America** | 1781–1783 | Robert Morris | Public distrust of its notes and Morris's declining political power forced its conversion into an ordinary state-chartered commercial bank. |
| 2 | **First Bank of the United States** | 1791–1811 | Alexander Hamilton | Massive inflation (42% of savings confiscated in its first five years, per Griffin). Charter renewal defeated by **one vote** in the Senate and one in the House, January 24, 1811. Griffin notes the largest single influence in the bank came from Rothschild interests in Europe. |
| 3 | **Second Bank of the United States** | 1816–1836 | Nicholas Biddle vs. President Andrew Jackson | Jackson campaigned against and vetoed its recharter; Biddle deliberately contracted credit to trigger a depression and pressure Congress into restoring the Bank, provoking Jackson's famous outburst — "You are a den of vipers... I intend to rout you out." Jackson won; the Bank's charter expired. |
| 4 | **Federal Reserve System** | 1913–present | The Jekyll Island group | Still standing — the subject of the rest of the book. |

The First Bank's demise is notable for a detail Griffin returns to elsewhere: it was defeated by a coalition of Jeffersonians who wanted sound, gold-backed money and frontier "wildcat" bankers who wanted no central restraint at all — strange allies united only in wanting the central bank gone.

## Griffin's Seven Reasons to Abolish the Federal Reserve

This list, stated in the Preface and repeated verbatim as the book's closing summary, is the organizing spine of Griffin's entire argument. Each reason is tied to a specific chapter:

| # | Reason | Chapter(s) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | It is incapable of accomplishing its stated objectives. | Ch. 1 |
| 2 | It is a cartel operating against the public interest. | Ch. 3 |
| 3 | It is the supreme instrument of usury. | Ch. 10 |
| 4 | It generates our most unfair tax (inflation). | Ch. 10 |
| 5 | It encourages war. | Ch. 14 |
| 6 | It destabilizes the economy. | Ch. 23 |
| 7 | It is an instrument of totalitarianism. | Ch. 5, Ch. 26 |

On the first, Griffin's evidence is the System's own track record against its stated goal of economic stability: the crashes of 1921 and 1929, the Great Depression, recessions in 1953, '57, '69, '75, and '81, "Black Monday" in 1987, and roughly a 1000% cumulative inflation that he calculates destroyed about 90% of the dollar's purchasing power by the early 1990s.

On the seventh, Griffin draws the full causal chain that closes the book: fiat money created by a central bank leads to government debt, which causes inflation, which erodes prosperity, which is then used to justify ever-larger government power — an escalating cycle he argues terminates in totalitarianism unless the starting mechanism, the Fed itself, is removed.

## The "Creature" Metaphor

The book's title is not incidental. Griffin consistently personifies the Federal Reserve as a living, growing predator — something summoned into being at a specific place and moment (Jekyll Island) and then released to grow beyond the control of those who created it. In his closing passage he writes that the Creature "has grown large and powerful since its conception on Jekyll Island," has come to "roam across every continent," and warns that if it is not stopped, it will become mankind's "eternal lord and master." The metaphor is doing real argumentative work throughout the book: it frames the Fed not as a neutral technical institution subject to policy adjustment, but as an entity with its own survival imperative — one that must be starved of the power that sustains it, or it will continue to grow.

---

## Griffin's Conclusion

Griffin's remedy is not reform but **abolition**. Because he frames every one of the seven reasons as structural — arising from what the Fed *is*, not from who happens to be running it — he argues that adjusting interest-rate policy, changing personnel, or increasing oversight cannot fix the underlying problem. His proposed alternative, developed in the book's final chapters, centers on ending the government's legal-tender monopoly and fiat-money privilege and returning the country to money whose supply cannot be manipulated by any central authority — paired with a sixteen-step legislative plan for winding the System down. He is candid that this plan is a starting proposal rather than a finished blueprint, but insists the core conclusion is not in doubt: "We do know that the Federal Reserve System must be abolished."
