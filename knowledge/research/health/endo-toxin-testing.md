# Blue Blood and False Alarms: The LAL Test

> **In short:** The world's main test for bacterial endotoxins is made from horseshoe crab blood. It works very well for medicines, but it can be fooled by other substances, and cannabis is full of them. That's why, according to Kevin McKernan, regulators haven't required endotoxin testing for cannabis yet.

## Quick Pronunciation Guide

| Term | Say it like | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| LAL | "L-A-L" | Short for *Limulus amebocyte lysate* |
| *Limulus* | lih-MYOO-lus | The scientific name of the Atlantic horseshoe crab |
| Amebocyte | uh-MEE-buh-site | The crab's blood cell that reacts to bacteria |
| Lysate | LY-sate | What you get when you break cells open |
| Endotoxin | EN-doh-TOK-sin | An inflammatory molecule from the outer layer of certain bacteria |
| Beta-glucan | BAY-tuh GLOO-kan | A sugar-like molecule found in fungus cell walls |

## Why This Matters for Cannabis

In a [previous article](https://tinyurl.com/28ed4prm), we looked at Kevin McKernan's concerns about endotoxins on cannabis flower. One question came up: no state requires endotoxin testing on cannabis, so is that on purpose, or do regulators just not know?

When asked directly on X, McKernan said it's neither. He called it an analytical quality-control problem:

- His team has run LAL tests on cannabis, and most samples came back **off the charts**.
- But cannabis contains many compounds that could trigger **false positives** on the LAL test.
- Until the LAL results are checked against a **mass spectrometry** method, he doesn't think regulators can require a test that might give lots of false alarms.
- That kind of validation is **expensive**, and the cannabis industry has little access to normal **banking**, so the money to do it is hard to find.

To understand his answer, it helps to understand the test itself.

## What Endotoxin Testing Is For

Endotoxins come from the outer layer of Gram-negative bacteria. Even when the bacteria are dead, endotoxin can cause fever and inflammation if it gets into the body. In large amounts in the bloodstream, it can cause dangerous shock.

That's why anything injected into people, such as vaccines, IV fluids, and injectable drugs, has to be checked for endotoxin. Medical devices that touch blood are tested too.

Before the LAL test, the standard method was to inject a sample into **rabbits** and watch whether they developed a fever. It worked, but it was slow, costly, and used live animals.

## An Accidental Discovery

In the early 1950s, Frederik Bang, a researcher from Johns Hopkins, was studying horseshoe crabs at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, Massachusetts. He noticed something strange: one of his crabs had died from an infection that turned nearly all of its blood into a semi-solid clump.

About a decade later, Bang teamed up with a young blood researcher, Jack Levin. Levin showed that the clotting depended on the crab's blood cells, called amebocytes, and that it was triggered by bacterial endotoxin. The pair published their findings in 1964.

Levin saw the potential right away: crab blood could become a fast, sensitive, and cheap test for endotoxin. And it did:

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| Early 1950s | Bang notices unusual clotting in an infected horseshoe crab |
| 1964 | Levin and Bang show the clotting is triggered by endotoxin |
| 1970s | The FDA begins regulating the test and approves it as a replacement for the rabbit test |
| 1990s | LAL has largely replaced rabbit testing for medicines |
| Today | Tens of millions of LAL tests are run worldwide each year |

Bang and Levin never patented their discovery, so others built the businesses that made it into a global industry.

## How the Test Works

Horseshoe crabs live in water full of bacteria, and their blood evolved a fast defense: when it touches endotoxin, it clots around the invader to trap it.

That defense works like a row of dominoes. Endotoxin knocks over the first domino, a protein called **Factor C**. That sets off the next one, and the next, until the final step forms a gel. Because each step amplifies the one before it, even a tiny amount of endotoxin produces a clear reaction.

Labs use this reaction in three main ways:

- **Gel-clot:** Does the sample form a gel or not? A simple yes or no.
- **Turbidimetric:** How cloudy does the sample get, and how fast? Cloudier means more endotoxin.
- **Chromogenic:** A dye is added that changes color as the reaction runs. More color means more endotoxin.

## The Cost to Horseshoe Crabs

To make LAL, companies collect wild horseshoe crabs, drain up to about 30% of their blood, and return them to the ocean. Their blood is blue because it uses copper instead of iron to carry oxygen.

Not every crab survives. The Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission, which manages horseshoe crabs on the East Coast, estimates that about **15%** of bled crabs die from the process. Studies have produced estimates anywhere from about **4% to 30%**, and the industry argues the real number is at the low end.

The concern goes beyond the crabs themselves. A migratory shorebird called the **red knot** stops in the Delaware Bay during its long spring migration to feast on horseshoe crab eggs. Fewer crabs can mean less food for the birds. New Jersey placed a moratorium on horseshoe crab harvesting in 2008 with the red knot in mind, though scientists still debate how much crab numbers drive the bird's decline compared with other factors.

## The Lab-Made Alternatives

Scientists eventually figured out how to make the key crab protein without the crab. There are now two main lab-made versions:

- **Recombinant Factor C (rFC)** copies only the first domino, Factor C, and measures when endotoxin triggers it.
- **Recombinant cascade reagent (rCR)** rebuilds the whole row of dominoes in the lab.

For years, drug makers were slow to switch because U.S. rules treated these as special-case alternatives. That changed in 2024, when the U.S. Pharmacopeia, which sets drug testing standards, approved a new chapter covering both rFC and rCR. It became official in May 2025. The new chapter sits alongside the traditional LAL chapter rather than replacing it, so companies can use either.

## Where the Test Gets Fooled

The LAL test is very good at detecting endotoxin, but it isn't perfect.

**Beta-glucans.** Crab blood contains a second trigger, called **Factor G**, that responds to beta-glucans, sugar-like molecules found in the cell walls of fungi. Factor G sets off the same clotting reaction as endotoxin, so the test can't tell the difference. The result looks like endotoxin, even when it isn't.

Labs have two main fixes:

1. Add a **glucan blocker** that switches off the Factor G pathway.
2. Use **recombinant Factor C**, which doesn't contain Factor G at all, so beta-glucans don't affect it.

**Messy samples.** The test was designed for clean liquids like medicines. Messy materials like dust or plant matter are much harder. In a 2005 study comparing five labs testing farm dust, the labs got significantly different results from the same samples, partly because each lab pulled out a different amount of endotoxin from the dust during preparation. Endotoxin stuck to particles may not register properly at all.

**Why this matters for cannabis:** Cannabis is often contaminated with mold, which brings beta-glucans with it. It's also a complex plant material, packed with sticky resins and many other compounds. That's exactly the kind of sample where the LAL test is most likely to be fooled, which is McKernan's point.

## Checking the Results with Mass Spectrometry

McKernan says the LAL results need to be "benchmarked" against a mass spectrometry method. Here's what that means.

A **mass spectrometer** is an instrument that identifies molecules by weighing them very precisely. Instead of watching for a biological reaction, it looks for a chemical fingerprint.

Endotoxin contains special fats called **3-hydroxy fatty acids**, which are a unique part of the endotoxin molecule. Measuring them with mass spectrometry shows how much endotoxin is really there, without being fooled by beta-glucans.

The two methods answer slightly different questions:

| | LAL test | Mass spectrometry |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | How strongly the sample triggers a biological reaction | How much endotoxin is chemically present |
| Strengths | Fast, cheap, extremely sensitive, standard in medicine | Specific, not fooled by beta-glucans |
| Weaknesses | Can be fooled by other substances; struggles with messy samples | Slower, more expensive, needs specialized equipment and expertise |

"Benchmarking" means running both methods on the same cannabis samples to see whether they agree. If they line up, LAL can be trusted as the fast everyday test. If they don't, the LAL numbers can't be taken at face value.

## What Would It Take for Cannabis?

Putting it together, a reliable endotoxin test for cannabis would probably need:

1. **A validation study** comparing LAL results with mass spectrometry on many cannabis samples
2. **Testing ways to reduce false positives**, such as glucan blockers or recombinant Factor C, to see whether they bring LAL results in line with mass spectrometry
3. **Standard sample preparation**, so every lab extracts endotoxin from cannabis the same way
4. **Funding to pay for all of it**, which is hard to come by while cannabis remains federally illegal and most cannabis businesses lack access to normal banking

Until that work is done, the high endotoxin readings on cannabis are a warning sign worth investigating, not a settled measurement.

## Conclusion

The LAL test is one of medicine's great accidental discoveries: a crab's ancient defense against bacteria turned into the world's main safeguard for injectable drugs. But a test built for clean medicines doesn't automatically work on a moldy, resin-coated plant. McKernan's answer shows the real holdup on cannabis endotoxin testing isn't a lack of concern. It's that nobody has yet proven which numbers to trust.
