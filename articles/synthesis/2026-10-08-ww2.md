# Who Paid for the War? World War II Through G. Edward Griffin's Eyes

## Introduction

Most histories of World War II ask who started it. G. Edward Griffin asks a question that turns out to be just as revealing: who paid for it?

World War II was the most expensive event in human history. Tanks, ships, planes, and the food and fuel for millions of soldiers, year after year, on a scale no nation had ever attempted. So where did all that money come from? And who came out ahead when it was over?

Griffin's answer, laid out in his 1994 book *The Creature from Jekyll Island: A Second Look at the Federal Reserve*, is that modern total war runs on a money machine, and that the machine was built by and for a small group of bankers. The book became a phenomenon, a founding text for the movement to audit or end the Federal Reserve and a major influence on figures like Ron Paul. Here's how Griffin sees it.

## Part One: The Secret Meeting at Jekyll Island

Griffin's story opens like a spy novel. In November 1910, a handful of the most powerful men in American finance slipped away in secret to the Jekyll Island Club, a private resort off the Georgia coast owned by some of the richest families in the country.

Look at who was in the room. Senator Nelson Aldrich, chairman of the National Monetary Commission. Paul Warburg of the investment bank Kuhn, Loeb. Frank Vanderlip, president of National City Bank. Henry Davison, a partner of J.P. Morgan. Benjamin Strong of Bankers Trust. And A. Piatt Andrew of the Treasury. They traveled using only first names to avoid reporters. Over about ten days, they drafted the plan that became the foundation of the Federal Reserve Act, passed in December 1913.

This isn't speculation; participants later wrote about the trip themselves. For Griffin, the secrecy says everything. The men who would be regulated by the new system wrote the rules for it, behind closed doors.

His conclusion: the Federal Reserve is, at heart, a cartel. It's a system designed by the biggest banks to protect themselves from competition and from failure, with the government as their partner and the public footing the bill.

## Part Two: The Mandrake Mechanism

The core of Griffin's book is what he calls the Mandrake Mechanism, named after the comic-strip magician Mandrake. It's his term for how the modern banking system creates money out of nothing.

It works like this. When the government spends more than it takes in, it issues bonds. When the central bank and commercial banks buy those bonds, brand-new money is created to pay for them. Nobody gets a tax bill. Instead, the cost arrives later, as inflation, quietly eating away at the value of everyone's savings and wages. Griffin calls inflation a hidden tax.

And here's why this matters so much for war. Before central banking, Griffin argues, a government that wanted to fight a long war had two options: raise taxes or borrow real savings. Both put natural brakes on how long and how hard a war could be fought, because people felt the cost directly and could push back. A central bank takes those brakes off. It lets a government spend almost without limit and spreads the cost invisibly across the whole population. In Griffin's view, central banking is what made total war possible.

## Part Three: The War Cycle

Griffin describes what he calls the "Rothschild Formula," drawn from the history of the great banking houses of the Napoleonic era. The idea is simple: lending to governments at war is one of the most profitable businesses there is. Financiers who serve rival governments can profit from both sides of a conflict, then profit again from rebuilding after it. Griffin presents it as a pattern in how war finance works, and he applies the same logic to American banks in the twentieth century.

He also draws heavily on the Georgetown historian Carroll Quigley, whose 1966 history *Tragedy and Hope* described a network of international bankers whose aim was a world system of financial control, run in coordination by central banks, with the Bank for International Settlements in Switzerland at the top. Quigley was largely sympathetic to that network. Griffin, using Quigley's own account, saw it as a warning.

## Part Four: The Test Run: World War I

Griffin's treatment of World War I is where his framework gets concrete, and it sets up everything about World War II.

When war broke out in Europe in 1914, the United States stayed neutral until 1917. But American finance didn't. J.P. Morgan & Co. served as Britain's purchasing agent in the United States and arranged enormous loans to Britain and France, dwarfing anything lent to Germany. Griffin's point: by 1917, an Allied defeat would have meant catastrophic losses for the American banks that had bet on the Allies. That gave them a powerful stake in getting America into the war.

Then there's the *Lusitania*. In May 1915, a German U-boat sank the British liner off the coast of Ireland, killing 128 Americans and turning American opinion against Germany. The ship was carrying munitions, including rifle cartridges and shrapnel shells listed right on its cargo manifest. And Griffin points to a letter Churchill wrote as First Lord of the Admiralty in early 1915, saying it was important to attract neutral shipping to British shores, especially in the hope of embroiling the United States with Germany. For Griffin, that letter is a window into how great powers and their financiers set up the conditions for war.

And notice the timing. The Federal Reserve opened its doors in 1914, just as the war began. Within a few years, America was fighting a war financed on a scale that would have been impossible without it.

## Part Five: The Doctrine of War

Griffin also took seriously a strange document called *Report from Iron Mountain* (1967). It was presented as a secret government study concluding that war is necessary for the stability of modern societies, because it gives governments authority, absorbs surplus production, and keeps populations disciplined. Its author, Leonard Lewin, later said he wrote it as satire. Griffin's response was that, whatever its origins, it captured the real logic of what he called the "war system" with uncomfortable accuracy.

## Part Six: World War II: The Mandrake Mechanism at Full Power

Griffin's book spends more time on the Fed's founding and World War I than on World War II. But apply his framework to the second war and the financial record fits it remarkably well.

**The debt explosion.** American federal debt went from roughly $43 billion in 1940 to around $260 billion by 1945, a sixfold jump in five years. No tax system on earth could have raised that kind of money that fast.

**The unlimited checkbook.** Starting in 1942, the Federal Reserve committed to holding interest rates on government debt at very low fixed levels and to buying whatever bonds it took to keep them there. That arrangement lasted until the Treasury–Federal Reserve Accord of 1951. In plain terms, the central bank stood behind the war with a blank check. This is the Mandrake Mechanism in its purest form.

**The hidden tax arrives.** During the war, wage and price controls kept much of the inflation out of sight. Then, after 1945, the controls came off and prices surged. The public paid for the war twice: once in taxes and war bonds, and again as the dollar lost value.

**Business across the front lines.** Griffin's picture of finance that operates above national loyalties fits a series of documented wartime ties. The Bank for International Settlements, the very institution Quigley placed at the top of the system, kept doing business with Nazi Germany throughout the war, with an American as its president for part of it. Standard Oil of New Jersey had a close partnership with the German chemical giant IG Farben. General Motors owned the German carmaker Opel, and Ford had a big German subsidiary, both of which built vehicles for the German army. And in 1942, the U.S. government seized the Union Banking Corporation in New York under the Trading with the Enemy Act, because of its ties to the German industrialist Fritz Thyssen, an early backer of Hitler.

**The new order.** Before the war was even over, the Allied powers met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in 1944 and built a new international financial system, creating the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank and placing the dollar at the center of world finance. Through Griffin's eyes, this is the war cycle completed: a war of unprecedented scale, fought on money created by the banking system, paid for by the public through debt and inflation, and followed by a financial order with the same interests at its center, now on a global scale.

## Part Seven: The Pushback

To understand Griffin fully, it helps to know what his critics say.

Economists broadly agree that central banks make it easier for governments to finance wars, and the Fed's wartime role is on the record. But most reject Griffin's larger claim that the Fed is a cartel run for bankers. They point out that governments fought enormous wars long before modern central banks existed, from Napoleon's campaigns to the American Civil War, using taxes, borrowing, and printed paper money. The Union printed "greenbacks" to fight the Civil War with no central bank at all.

Historians who have studied the *Lusitania* and the J.P. Morgan loans for a century mostly conclude that German submarine warfare, culminating in the return to unrestricted attacks in 1917 and the Zimmermann Telegram, did more to bring America into World War I than the bank loans did. On World War II, most historians see the causes of the war in the decisions of governments in Berlin, Tokyo, London, and Washington rather than in the needs of finance, and they see the wartime business ties as companies protecting profits and investments rather than a coordinated plan. *Report from Iron Mountain* is regarded as a hoax.

And because the "Rothschild Formula" uses a Jewish banking family as its emblem, the phrase has been picked up in antisemitic circles in ways that go far beyond Griffin's argument about how war finance works. That's worth knowing when the phrase comes up.

## Conclusion

Griffin's great contribution is the question itself. Most of us never ask how a war gets paid for. Griffin puts that question at the center and follows it from a secret meeting on an island in Georgia to the bond markets of World War II and the conference rooms of Bretton Woods.

A lot of what he points to is a matter of record: the Jekyll Island meeting, the Fed's role in financing both world wars, the debt explosion, the postwar inflation, and the business ties that ran across the front lines. Where people divide is on what it all adds up to: a financial system that makes war easier to fight, or a system built to profit from it.

Either way, Griffin leaves you with a question worth asking about any war, then or now: who pays, how do they pay, and who comes out ahead?
