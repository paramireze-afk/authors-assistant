# "Greater Israel" and the "Greater Israel Project"

> **Entry type:** Conceptual/historical-political reference — maps a contested idea rather than a single document or institution
> **Core subject:** A territorial and ideological concept within Zionist thought ("Eretz Yisrael HaShlema" / the "Whole Land of Israel"), and separately, a much more expansive and contested claim — sometimes called the "Greater Israel Project" — that this concept functions as an active, coordinated strategic program for regional territorial expansion pursued through war, proxy conflict, and the deliberate fragmentation of neighboring Arab states.
> **Related entries:** `clean-break-1996.md` (the 1996 Perle/Feith/Wurmser policy paper) and `pnac.md` (the Project for the New American Century). Read this entry alongside both; it cross-references rather than repeats their content, including the existing treatment of the Oded Yinon essay in the Clean Break entry's §6.5.
> **A note on how to read this entry:** This is the single most epistemically fraught topic in this knowledge base cluster so far. It sits at the intersection of (a) well-documented internal Zionist debate stretching back over a century, (b) real, quotable statements by senior sitting Israeli officials as recently as 2025, (c) a body of geopolitical analysis of genuinely contested rigor, and (d) a large volume of material that treats "Greater Israel" as proof of a unified, secret master plan encompassing everything from Zionism's founding to the Iraq and Syrian wars to events unfolding as this entry is written. This entry tries to keep those four categories visibly separate throughout rather than blending them into a single narrative, and flags source quality explicitly wherever it bears on the claim being made.

## 2. Two Different Claims That Get Conflated

Before tracing the history, it's worth stating plainly what this entry treats as two analytically separate claims, because almost every source in this space blurs them:

**Claim A — the documented one:** A current within Zionism, dating to the 1920s and running through today, has held that the Jewish state's rightful or ideal territory extends beyond Israel's pre-1967 or even current borders — variably including the West Bank, Gaza, the Golan Heights, parts of southern Lebanon, and (in the movement's original 1920s form) both banks of the Jordan River, i.e., present-day Jordan. This current has had real, nameable adherents, real organizations, real Knesset representation, and real (if disputed and never fully realized) influence on Israeli settlement and territorial policy since 1967. It is documented in primary sources, party platforms, and — as recently as August 2025 — the sitting prime minister's own recorded words.

**Claim B — the far more contested one:** That "Greater Israel" is not merely an ideological current with real adherents but an actively pursued, secretly coordinated strategic *project*, executed by the Israeli state and/or a transnational network of Zionist actors, using engineered regional wars (Iraq 2003, Syria's civil war, and by some accounts the 2023–2025 Gaza war and beyond) as instruments to fragment neighboring Arab states and annex or dominate the resulting territory — a program understood by proponents of this view as continuous from Theodor Herzl's own writings, through the 1982 Yinon essay, through Clean Break and PNAC, to the present day.

Claim A is historically documented and this entry treats it as established fact where the sourcing supports it. Claim B is a real, circulating interpretive framework with genuine adherents across a range of political traditions (see §7–§9) — but it is also, in some of its more totalizing forms, a framework that overlaps heavily with, and in some cases directly recycles, older antisemitic conspiracy tropes about coordinated secret Jewish world-control, and readers should approach any single source making the strongest version of Claim B with real skepticism about evidentiary standards, exactly as this knowledge base's Clean Break entry cautions regarding the Yinon Plan specifically (see that entry's §6.5). This entry aims to document what serious, disparate sources actually claim about Claim B — including sources that reject it — rather than asserting Claim B is either true or false as a totalizing thesis.

## 3. Origins: From Herzl to Jabotinsky to 1948

**Theodor Herzl and early Zionist territorial thinking.** Founder-of-Zionism Theodor Herzl's own diaries have been repeatedly cited — including by Global Research's Michel Chossudovsky — as describing the prospective Jewish state's territory as extending, in a phrase widely attributed to Herzl's *Complete Diaries* (Vol. II, p. 711), "from the Brook of Egypt to the Euphrates." This entry has not independently verified the precise wording or context of this specific citation against a scholarly critical edition of Herzl's diaries, and readers should treat it with appropriate caution pending that verification — it circulates overwhelmingly through secondary and tertiary sources sympathetic to Claim B rather than through mainstream Zionist historiography, which generally treats early Zionist territorial thinking as considerably more fluid, contested, and geographically vague than a single settled maximalist claim. What is not in dispute is that early Zionist congresses and figures did discuss a range of possible territorial extents for a future Jewish homeland, reflecting the movement's genuinely unsettled and internally divided character in its first decades — a normal feature of a nascent nationalist movement rather than, on its own, evidence of a coordinated later "project."

**Ze'ev Jabotinsky and Revisionist Zionism.** The clearer, better-documented origin point is the 1920s split within the Zionist movement. David Ben-Gurion and Chaim Weizmann's mainstream "practical Zionism" favored incremental settlement and diplomatic accommodation with Britain. Ze'ev Jabotinsky broke from this in founding Revisionist Zionism, which historians characterize as defined by territorial maximalism — the explicit demand for a Jewish state and eventual Jewish majority on *both* banks of the Jordan River, and firm opposition to any partition of the Mandate territory, including opposition to the 1937 Peel Commission's partition proposal on both territorial and principled grounds (accepting partition, Jabotinsky argued, would concede a dangerous precedent to Britain and the Arab population alike). Jabotinsky's 1923 essay "The Iron Wall" — arguing Jewish sovereignty could only be secured through demonstrated, unshakeable military strength rather than negotiated accommodation — became foundational to this tradition and, later commentators (Scott Horton and Avi Shlaim among them, from very different traditions) have argued, to a specific realist-through-force logic that echoes through later Revisionist-descended Israeli strategic thought, including in the Likud party Jabotinsky's movement eventually produced.

**The 1921 Transjordan setback.** Britain's 1921 creation of the Emirate of Transjordan (later the Kingdom of Jordan) out of roughly three-quarters of the original Mandate territory was, from the Revisionist perspective, a foundational betrayal — the first partition of what Revisionists considered the promised Jewish homeland, and the direct origin of Revisionist Zionism's insistence that eastern-bank territory (Transjordan/Jordan) remained legitimately part of Eretz Yisrael. The Irgun (Etzel), the Revisionist-aligned paramilitary organization founded in 1931 that fought British rule and, controversially, carried out attacks on Palestinian Arab communities and later joined the 1948 war, used as its emblem a map of the *entire* British Mandate territory including both banks of the Jordan, with a rifle superimposed — a documented, verifiable primary-source visual expression of Claim A, not Claim B, from this period.

**1948 and the term's near-disappearance.** When Ben-Gurion's mainstream Zionist leadership accepted the UN's 1947 Partition Plan and declared statehood in 1948 on a considerably smaller territory than either the full Mandate or the biblical maximum, "Eretz Yisrael HaShlema" briefly took on a different, retrospective meaning — describing the aspiration that the whole of historic Eretz Yisrael, even if not immediately incorporated into the new state, remained part of the Jewish people's rightful patrimony in principle, even as the state itself operated within much narrower internationally recognized borders. The term's political salience receded through the 1950s and early 1960s as the new state focused on consolidation, immigration absorption, and survival across the 1948, 1956, and early Cold War periods.

## 4. 1967 to the Present: The Term's Two Great Resurgences

**After the Six-Day War (1967).** Israel's rapid, decisive victory in June 1967 brought the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, the Sinai Peninsula, and the Golan Heights under Israeli control — suddenly making "Eretz Yisrael HaShlema" a live, practical political question rather than a historical abstraction, for the first time since Revisionist Zionism's founding. A "Movement for Greater Israel" (Tnuat Eretz Yisrael HaShlema) formed shortly after the war, drawing support across an unusually broad ideological spectrum — including figures from the Labor movement, not only the Revisionist right — reflecting the genuine, if temporary, breadth of post-1967 territorial maximalism in Israeli political culture. Religious Zionist settlement movements, most prominently Gush Emunim (founded 1974), gave the concept renewed institutional and theological force, framing West Bank settlement as a religious imperative connected to the biblical land grant rather than merely a strategic or security calculation.

**After the 1977 Likud victory.** Menachem Begin's 1977 election victory — ending nearly three decades of Labor Zionist dominance and installing the direct ideological heirs of Jabotinsky's Revisionism in power for the first time — gave "Eretz Yisrael HaShlema" renewed prominence precisely because so much of Likud's base consisted of Jabotinsky's ideological descendants. Yet the same Begin government also, in 1979, signed the Camp David Accords and returned the entire Sinai Peninsula to Egypt — the single largest territorial withdrawal in Israel's history — demonstrating from the outset that "Greater Israel" rhetoric and actual Israeli state policy have never been simply identical, even under governments closely descended from the movement that coined the term. Yitzhak Rabin's government and, later, Ehud Olmert's, each came close to a negotiated return of the Golan Heights to Syria — further evidence, cited by scholars skeptical of totalizing "Greater Israel" narratives, that Israeli territorial policy across different governments and decades has been considerably more negotiable, internally contested, and responsive to changing strategic circumstances than a fixed maximalist "project" framing would suggest.

**Oslo and its aftermath: the term's near-eclipse.** Through the 1990s Oslo process, "Greater Israel" rhetoric receded from mainstream Israeli political discourse — not because underlying settlement activity slowed (it did not; West Bank settlement expanded substantially across this period under governments of both major blocs) but because the explicit maximalist *framing* became a political liability in a period nominally oriented toward negotiated Palestinian statehood. Commentary reviewed for this entry (the Lebanese outlet *Majalla*, notably) makes the further point that for Israelis and Palestinians born after 2000, the practical distinction between "Greater Israel" as an aspiration and simple ongoing Israeli control "from the river to the sea" has become, in the commentator's phrase, of diminishing practical significance — the territory in question has simply been under continuous Israeli military and administrative control since 1967 regardless of what ideological label attaches to that control in any given decade.

**August 2025: Netanyahu's on-camera remarks.** On August 12, 2025, in a televised interview with Israel's i24News, host Sharon Gal — a former right-wing Knesset member — presented Prime Minister Netanyahu with what she described as an amulet depicting "a map of the Promised Land," reported by the *Times of Israel* to likely be a maximalist map (sold commercially by Gal's own company) depicting territory from the Nile to the Euphrates. Gal asked Netanyahu directly: "Do you connect to the vision [of Greater Israel]?" Netanyahu responded, on camera, "Very much" — and, pressed further, described his own generation's task as a "historic and spiritual mission." He did not elaborate on specific territorial claims, and the exchange — notably — was cut from both the Hebrew and English versions of the interview that i24 itself posted to YouTube, appearing only in the fuller version on i24's own Hebrew-language website. The remark drew immediate, formal diplomatic condemnation from Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Jordan, Egypt, the Arab League, and the Palestinian Authority, variously describing it as reflecting "colonial delusions" and a threat to regional sovereignty and stability. This is Claim A territory — a real, verifiable, on-the-record statement by a sitting head of government, reported across a wide range of outlets with consistent quotation — though it should be read for exactly what it documents (rhetorical affirmation, in a friendly interview setting, of felt personal "connection" to a maximalist symbolic vision) rather than treated as itself a formal territorial claim, annexation announcement, or documented operational plan, none of which followed from the remark as of this entry's writing.

## 5. The Yinon Essay and Its Uses (Cross-Reference)

The 1982 essay by Oded Yinon, "A Strategy for Israel in the Nineteen Eighties," published in the World Zionist Organization-affiliated journal *Kivunim* and later translated into English by dissident Israeli scholar Israel Shahak, is treated at length in the companion Clean Break entry (§6.5), including this knowledge base's caution that the strongest claims linking Yinon directly to a continuous, implemented "Greater Israel" grand strategy rest heavily on sourcing — chiefly Global Research and the Voltaire Network — that mainstream fact-checkers and Middle East scholarship regard as unreliable. That caution applies with equal or greater force here, since Yinon's essay is the single most load-bearing document in nearly every version of "Greater Israel Project" Claim B (§2 above) that this entry's research encountered. Readers should consult the Clean Break entry's fuller treatment rather than have it duplicated here; the short version is: Yinon's 1982 text is real and verifiable; whether it reflects a genuinely adopted, continuously pursued Israeli state strategy across the following four-plus decades, or a single hawkish Israeli journalist's speculative essay that a later, largely non-Israeli commentariat retrofitted into a unified field theory of Middle East conflict, remains a live, unresolved, and legitimately disputed question — not a settled fact in either direction.

## 6. Ralph Peters's "Blood Borders" Map: A Separate, Non-Israeli Source Often Conflated with Yinon

A specific document worth disentangling from the Yinon essay, because the two are frequently merged in "Greater Israel Project" material: retired U.S. Army lieutenant colonel Ralph Peters published a 2006 article in the *Armed Forces Journal*, "Blood Borders," accompanied by a map redrawing much of the Middle East's borders along ethnic and sectarian lines — including an enlarged Kurdistan, a divided Iraq, and various other adjustments. Peters was not Israeli, did not represent Israeli government policy, and has stated the map was a thought exercise about the region's artificial colonial-era borders rather than a U.S. or Israeli policy proposal. Nonetheless, the map is very frequently reproduced in "Greater Israel Project" material as though it were an Israeli or Zionist planning document, or as corroborating evidence for the Yinon thesis. Treat any source that presents the Peters map as an Israeli-authored or Israeli-commissioned document as making a documented factual error, regardless of what other claims that source is making.

## 7. The Realist and Foreign-Policy-Establishment Critique: Mearsheimer and Walt

John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt's 2006 *London Review of Books* essay "The Israel Lobby," and their subsequent 2007 book, are treated at length in the Clean Break entry (§9), including the sustained and unresolved controversy over whether their thesis constitutes legitimate policy critique or, as critics including the ADL, Jeffrey Goldberg, and others have argued, reproduces antisemitic tropes regardless of authorial intent. It's worth noting specifically for this entry: Mearsheimer and Walt's argument is emphatically **not** a "Greater Israel Project" argument in the Claim B sense described above. Their thesis concerns the *domestic American political process* — how a loose coalition of pro-Israel political actors shapes U.S. foreign policy through ordinary democratic lobbying — not a claim about a secret, coordinated, transnational plan for Middle Eastern territorial conquest. Sources that cite Mearsheimer and Walt as supporting evidence for the maximalist "Greater Israel Project" thesis are, in most cases, overstating or mischaracterizing what the two scholars actually argue; this is a real and common conflation worth flagging explicitly, since Mearsheimer and Walt's institutional academic credibility (University of Chicago, Harvard Kennedy School) is sometimes used to lend borrowed authority to a considerably more totalizing thesis than either scholar has endorsed in their published work.

## 8. The Anti-Interventionist and Libertarian Tradition: A Genuinely Internally Divided Camp

This is among the more interesting and least monolithic traditions relevant to this topic, and deserves to be treated as internally contested rather than as a unified bloc.

**Murray Rothbard.** The libertarian economist and Old Right intellectual wrote a widely cited 1967 essay, "War Guilt in the Middle East," arguing — from a classical-liberal, anti-state framework rather than a religious or ethnic one — that the United States had inconsistently applied the principle of respecting existing "territorial integrity" borders, abandoning that principle as soon as it became clear Israel had won the 1967 war and expanded its territory. Rothbard's broader body of writing on Israel-Palestine, collected and discussed in later libertarian scholarship, positioned him as a sustained critic of both Zionism as a nationalist project (which he treated, per later summaries of his position, as analytically similar in kind to other nationalist ideologies he opposed on classical-liberal grounds) and of U.S. foreign aid to and diplomatic support for Israel specifically, as part of his broader Old Right opposition to American global intervention. Rothbard's position was contested even within libertarianism during his lifetime and remains so: economists Walter Block and Alan Futerman published a book-length critique, *The Classical Liberal Case for Israel* (2021), arguing Rothbard misapplied his own libertarian property-rights framework to the Israeli case and that a consistent Lockean homesteading analysis in fact supports Zionist land claims — an unusually direct, sourced intra-libertarian dispute about the correct application of the movement's own first principles to this specific question, worth citing as a clear example of genuine disagreement within a single intellectual tradition rather than a a monolithic "libertarian view."

**Ron Paul.** The former Texas congressman and repeated presidential candidate, a self-described friend and intellectual disciple of both Rothbard and Ludwig von Mises, built decades of congressional advocacy around ending *all* U.S. foreign aid — a position he and his defenders have consistently framed as flowing from general non-interventionist and constitutionalist principles rather than any specific animus toward Israel, since it applied identically to aid recipients worldwide. Critics have nonetheless frequently characterized his position, and that of ideologically adjacent figures, as effectively singling out Israel given that country's outsized share of total U.S. foreign assistance; defenders respond that ending aid "first" to the largest recipient is a mathematical consequence of a universal principle, not evidence of a special exception being carved out.

**Justin Raimondo and Antiwar.com.** Raimondo (1951–2019), co-founder and longtime editorial director of Antiwar.com, argued explicitly and repeatedly — a 2003 column being frequently cited — that Israel exercised what he characterized as a dominant, disproportionate influence over the formulation of American Middle East policy, a position that drew him both praise from paleoconservative allies (who saw him as challenging what they viewed as neoconservative capture of the conservative movement's foreign-policy line, including what they characterized as the "purging" of Iraq War-skeptical writers from outlets like *National Review*) and sustained criticism from pro-Israel commentators who argued his framing revived older tropes about coordinated Jewish political influence. Raimondo maintained this was empirical policy analysis rather than ethnic or religious animus; his critics maintained the framing itself, regardless of stated intent, reproduced a recognizable and dangerous rhetorical pattern. This is a live, unresolved dispute about characterization rather than one with a clean documentary resolution, structurally similar to (and often overlapping with) the broader Mearsheimer-Walt controversy discussed in §7 and in the Clean Break entry.

**Sheldon Richman.** Executive editor of the Libertarian Institute and a contributing editor at Antiwar.com, Richman has written extensively and, by his own account, come to his current sharply critical position on Zionism gradually over decades — informed in part, he has said, by childhood exposure to anti-Zionist critique within his own Orthodox Jewish extended family, and by his subsequent association with the explicitly non-Zionist and anti-Zionist tradition within American Reform Judaism represented by the American Council for Judaism. His 2019 book *Coming to Palestine* compiles decades of essays making a sustained libertarian and historical case against the Israeli state's legitimacy claims and treatment of Palestinians. Notably, Richman's critique — like Rothbard's — proceeds from opposition to nationalism and state power as categories, applied consistently (his defenders would argue) rather than as a claim specific to Jewish nationalism alone; critics of this framing (including fellow libertarians in the Block/Futerman camp cited above) argue the classical-liberal property-rights framework, applied consistently, in fact yields the opposite conclusion.

**Scott Horton.** As documented extensively in the Clean Break and PNAC entries, Horton's *Enough Already* and *The Scott Horton Show* material treat "Greater Israel"-adjacent territorial and strategic ambition as one real strand — among several, alongside the broader neoconservative movement and the ordinary financial incentives of the military-industrial complex — contributing to the Iraq War and subsequent regional conflicts, while explicitly resisting a single-cause "Greater Israel" master-plan framing in favor of his three-forces analysis (see PNAC entry §8). Horton's treatment of the Clean Break and Yinon material is considerably more textually careful and source-disciplined than most Claim-B-oriented material discussed in this entry, even though he shares with that material a broadly critical assessment of the strategic logic he documents.

**Patrick Buchanan.** The paleoconservative commentator, as documented in the Clean Break entry, argued in 2003 that Clean Break's "principle of preemption" had been "imposed... on the United States" by its authors. Buchanan's broader body of work has repeatedly made the case — controversially, and drawing sustained accusations of antisemitism across his career that this entry does not adjudicate but notes as a documented and persistent feature of his public reception — that pro-Israel advocacy exercises outsized influence over U.S. Middle East policy, a position structurally adjacent to Mearsheimer-Walt's academic argument (§7) but delivered in a considerably more polemical register and from a longer-standing, more overtly nationalist and immigration-restrictionist political tradition than either Mearsheimer-Walt's realist academic framework or the Rothbard-Raimondo-Richman classical-liberal tradition.

**Chalmers Johnson and Andrew Bacevich.** Johnson's "Blowback trilogy" (*Blowback*, 2000; *The Sorrows of Empire*, 2004; *Nemesis*, 2006) argued that American global military basing and intervention generate predictable violent backlash, and — in *The Sorrows of Empire* specifically — that U.S. Middle East policy was shaped substantially by what Johnson characterized as a set of dominant domestic lobbies including "the military-industrial complex, Wall Street, oil, and Israel," which he described as chief architects, promoters, and beneficiaries of American imperial overreach in the region. Bacevich, a retired U.S. Army colonel and historian (and, notably, a politically conservative Catholic writing from a very different starting tradition than Johnson's), has written extensively — across *The Limits of Power*, *America's War for the Greater Middle East*, and subsequent work — critiquing the scope and premises of sustained U.S. military engagement across the broader Middle East, generally treating Israel's role as one significant factor among several (alongside oil, Cold War legacy commitments, and bureaucratic momentum within the U.S. national-security establishment) rather than positioning Israeli influence as the primary or sole causal driver — a notably more measured emphasis than either Johnson's framing or the anti-interventionist libertarian tradition surveyed above, and worth noting as one more data point in how differently even writers broadly skeptical of American Middle East interventionism weight Israel's specific causal role.

## 9. The "Greater Israel Project" as an Explicit, Named Thesis: Chossudovsky and the Global Research Network

The specific phrase "Greater Israel Project," capitalized and treated as a proper-noun program, is most closely associated with economist Michel Chossudovsky and the Global Research (Centre for Research on Globalization) publishing network, in material dating substantially from a September 2015 article (subsequently updated multiple times through 2019 and republished as recently as June 2026) and reproduced widely across a network of ideologically aligned outlets (The Millennium Report, Just International, LewRockwell.com, and others). This material argues explicitly that the U.S.-NATO-led wars in Iraq and Syria constitute "part of the process of Israeli territorial expansion," draws directly on the Yinon essay's fragmentation logic (§5 above and the Clean Break entry's fuller treatment), and situates the entire thesis within a continuous line running from Herzl's diaries through Yinon to the present.

**A significant complication worth noting explicitly:** in a June 2026 piece co-authored with Drago Bosnic and titled, notably, "The Greater Israel Project is a Fraud," Chossudovsky himself substantially revised or at minimum sharply reframed his own earlier thesis — arguing that treating "Greater Israel" as evidence of independent Zionist agency inverts the actual power relationship, and that Israel functions instead as a "de facto NATO member" and U.S. proxy rather than a state independently driving American policy toward its own expansionist ends. This is a genuine and significant internal shift (or at least reframing) within the specific intellectual tradition most responsible for popularizing the "Greater Israel Project" as a named thesis, and it undercuts, in the author's own most recent formulation, a considerable portion of the framework's earlier and more widely circulated form — worth flagging clearly, since much secondary material citing "the Greater Israel Project" continues to circulate the earlier (2015–2019) version of Chossudovsky's argument without accounting for his own later reframing.

**On source quality:** Global Research and its affiliated outlets are widely regarded by academic Middle East studies scholars, mainstream fact-checking organizations, and journalism-standards organizations as an unreliable source with a documented history of publishing content later assessed as conspiratorial, including material overlapping with antisemitic tropes about coordinated Jewish or Zionist global influence, alongside genuinely mixed content on other subjects (economic and foreign-policy commentary that some readers and even some mainstream critics have found more defensible in isolation). This entry does not assert that every claim originating from this network is false, but it flags, consistent with the standard already applied in the Clean Break entry regarding the same broader source cluster, that claims sourced predominantly or exclusively to this network should be treated with substantially more skepticism than claims independently corroborated by mainstream academic Middle East scholarship, established investigative journalism, or primary documentary sources — and that the network's own internal reframing of its signature thesis (the paragraph above) is itself evidence that even sympathetic insiders regard the earlier, more totalizing version of the claim as overstated.

## 10. Mainstream Middle East Scholarship: A More Cautious Middle Ground

Between the "Greater Israel Project" maximalist thesis and its flat dismissal sits a substantial body of mainstream Middle East scholarship — represented in the sources reviewed for this entry by outlets like the pan-Arab *Majalla* and academic Middle East policy analyses (e.g., MEPEI's 2020s-era work referencing Saul Cohen's geopolitical scholarship) — that treats "Greater Israel" as a real, historically documented ideological current within Israeli politics with genuine, ongoing influence on settlement policy and the Israeli right's territorial ambitions, while explicitly declining to endorse the stronger claim that this current constitutes a unified, secretly coordinated master plan executed continuously since 1948 or since 1982. This middle position generally holds that: settlement expansion, the 1982 Yinon essay, the Clean Break paper, and Netanyahu's 2025 remarks are all real, individually documented data points reflecting a genuine current of Israeli territorial maximalism; that this current has had disproportionate influence within specific Israeli governing coalitions (especially post-2022 ones incorporating figures like Bezalel Smotrich, who has spoken from podiums bearing maps depicting Jordan and the West Bank as part of a "Greater Israel" vision) relative to Israeli public opinion as a whole, which polling has generally shown to be more divided and often more accommodationist than the maximalist policy positions actually pursued by recent governing coalitions; and that treating these documented data points as evidence of a single continuous, deliberately executed 70-to-100-year "project" — rather than as a recurring ideological current that gains and loses institutional power depending on Israeli domestic politics, regional conditions, and international constraints in any given period — overstates the coherence and continuity the documentary record actually supports.

## 11. Open Questions for Further Research

1. **The precise sourcing and context of the Herzl "Nile to Euphrates" diary quotation (§3)** deserves independent verification against a scholarly critical edition of Herzl's *Complete Diaries* rather than continued reliance on secondary citation through Chossudovsky and similar sources; this entry flags but does not resolve that gap.
2. **A systematic comparison of Israeli public opinion polling on territorial maximalism versus actual governing-coalition policy** across different periods (1967–77, 1977–92, Oslo era, post-2000, post-2022) would substantially sharpen §10's claim that maximalist rhetoric and governing practice have never been simply identical — this entry asserts that gap exists based on the general shape of the sourced material but has not independently compiled the polling data that would make the claim rigorous.
3. **Chossudovsky's 2026 reframing (§9)** deserves closer tracking as an ongoing development — worth revisiting this entry once more time has passed to see whether the reframing represents a durable shift in that specific intellectual tradition or a one-off framing choice in a single co-authored piece.
4. **The relationship between the 2025 Netanyahu remarks and any subsequent formal Israeli government territorial policy** should be updated in this entry as events develop; as of this entry's writing (drafted with a knowledge cutoff extending into 2026), no formal annexation or border-change policy had followed directly from the August 2025 remarks, and readers consulting this entry later should search for updates before treating that as still current.
5. **A dedicated, source-disciplined treatment of Gush Emunim and the religious-Zionist settlement movement specifically** — distinct from the Revisionist/Jabotinsky political-Zionist tradition covered here — would meaningfully deepen this entry's Claim A material, since religious and explicitly nationalist-secular versions of "Greater Israel" advocacy rest on genuinely different justificatory grounds (biblical covenant versus strategic depth and historical grievance) that this entry has compressed more than ideal.
