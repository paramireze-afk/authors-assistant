Anthony Fauci is what I would call a “Judas Goat.”
A story how the internet reshaped history
Paul Ramirez
Jun 17, 2026





If you’ve never heard the term, a Judas goat is a term to describe the role of an animal that leads other animals to the slaughter. If you’re ever looking to go down a rabbit hole, read about Fauci’s role during the AIDS crisis and the controversy surrounding AZT (a chemotherepy drug). One thing that stood out to me when I began researching that period was the use of phrases like “safe and effective”, “Misinformation”, and “Conspiracy theory”.

The real story, however, is not Anthony Fauci.

The real story is the internet.

Without the internet, I suspect Fauci would be remembered as a hero of sorts, and celebrated among future generations. His legacy would likely be shaped almost entirely by official biographies, media coverage, academic institutions, and government records. Future generations would inherit a largely uniform narrative about who he was and what he accomplished, and no one would know about his bio weapons background.

But the internet changed that.

For the first time in history, people can crowdsource information in a way that wasn’t possible. Someone living in Maine that has some obscure background can contribute to someone’s understanding of a historic/current event just by opening up a web browser.

History is no longer filtered through a handful of gatekeepers.

This has profound implications.

Just look who is the most popular people are on the internet right now? Look at Candace Owens, why is she so popular (please don’t say because Brigitte Macron penis ;-)). I would argue that she found a new niche, an emergent niche that comes from an open internet. For the first time, you are seeing a market for whistle blowers and if you look at her work, a lot of her work is inside information within organizations/governments from whistle blowers.

Evolution of the Collective Consciousness
For most of human history, knowledge flowed from a small number of institutions to the public. Information moved slowly, and narratives were shaped by governments, universities, publishers, and the media.

The internet changed that.

For the first time, billions of people can participate in a real-time conversation about reality itself. Information is no longer simply transmitted from the top down—it is debated, challenged, investigated, and reinterpreted by ordinary people.

I experienced this firsthand.

In 2020, I knew very little about virology, foreign policy, social movements, or the broader political landscape. Then came one crisis after another: COVID, lockdowns, the vaccine debate, wars, social unrest, and economic instability. Each event sent me down a new rabbit hole, forcing me to reexamine what I thought I knew.

Whether my conclusions were right or wrong is almost beside the point. What mattered was that my understanding evolved, and I suspect the same thing happened to millions of others.

The internet is far from perfect. It spreads misinformation as easily as truth and can trap people in echo chambers. Yet despite its flaws, I remain optimistic.

Beneath the noise is something unprecedented: a global network of minds constantly exchanging ideas, questioning assumptions, and updating their understanding of the world.

Perhaps what we’re witnessing is more than a technological revolution.

Perhaps it is the early stages of an evolution in collective consciousness.
