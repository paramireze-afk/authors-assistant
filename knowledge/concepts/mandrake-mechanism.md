# Mandrake Mechanism

## Quick Definition

The **Mandrake Mechanism** is G. Edward Griffin's name for the process by which the Federal Reserve and commercial banks create money "out of nothing" by converting **debt** into **money**. Griffin argues that in the modern U.S. system, money isn't backed by gold, labor, or savings — it is created the instant someone (usually the government) borrows, and it vanishes the instant the debt is repaid. He treats this as the master key to understanding everything else in the book: the Fed's cartel structure, inflation, boom-bust cycles, and the "hidden tax" paid by ordinary citizens.

> "If you understand this paragraph, you understand the Federal Reserve System." — Griffin, summarizing the overview diagram below.

---

## Where the Name Comes From

Griffin names the mechanism after **Mandrake the Magician**, a 1940s comic-strip character whose specialty was conjuring things out of nothing — and making them disappear again. Griffin picks the name deliberately: he wants the reader to see money creation not as a technical banking process but as a **magic trick**, complete with misdirection ("technical jargon"), props (bonds, reserves, checks), and an audience (the public) that doesn't realize a trick is being performed at all.

---

## The Core Claim: Modern Money *Is* Debt

Griffin's starting point is that U.S. money today is **100% fiat** — the gold/silver backing isn't reduced, it's gone entirely (0%). What bankers call "reserves" are not precious metal but Treasury bonds and other debt certificates.

From this, Griffin draws his central and most counterintuitive claim:

- Money is **not** created and then loaned out.
- Money is **not** simply "created from debt" as if debt exists first.
- Instead, **the act of borrowing is what causes money to spring into existence** — and the act of repayment causes it to vanish again.

This leads to a striking conclusion Griffin returns to repeatedly: **if every debt in the country were paid off, the entire money supply would disappear.** Not shrink — disappear. No coins, no paper currency, no balances in any checking account.

### The Fed's Own Words

Griffin builds this claim on the Federal Reserve's own publications, which he treats as surprisingly candid admissions:

- A New York Fed booklet states that currency "cannot be redeemed... for Treasury gold or any other asset," and that what "backs" Federal Reserve notes has little more than bookkeeping significance.
- A Chicago Fed booklet (*Modern Money Mechanics*) states plainly that neither paper currency nor deposits have value as commodities — a dollar bill is "just a piece of paper," and deposits are "merely book entries."
- Banks, in the Fed's own language, "create money by 'monetizing' the private debts of businesses and individuals."

Griffin's point in citing these isn't that the Fed is hiding anything technically — it's that the plain meaning of these admissions ("our money is backed by nothing but debt") rarely reaches the public, because it's buried in footnotes and jargon.

### The Eccles–Patman Exchange (1941)

Griffin's centerpiece evidence is a 1941 congressional exchange between **Marriner Eccles**, then-Governor of the Federal Reserve, and **Congressman Wright Patman**, chairman of the House Banking Committee, on how the Fed obtained $2 billion to buy government bonds in 1933:

> PATMAN: Out of what?
> ECCLES: Out of the right to issue credit money.
> PATMAN: And there is nothing behind it, is there, except our government's credit?
> ECCLES: That is what our money system is. If there were no debts in our money system, there wouldn't be any money.

Griffin treats this as the clearest possible confirmation, from the system's own architect, that the money supply and the debt supply are the same thing viewed from two angles.

He pairs this with a quote from **Robert Hemphill**, Credit Manager of the Atlanta Fed, who called it a "staggering thought" that if all bank loans were repaid, there would not be a dollar of coin or currency left in circulation anywhere in the country.

### Money as Illusion, Not Asset

Griffin extends this to argue that money, viewed in aggregate, is not really an asset at all. An individual's $1,000 loan looks like an asset to *them*, but it's offset by a $1,000 liability — net position zero. Scale this up to the entire economy: add up every bank account in the nation, and every dollar of it is owed by someone. The national net position is zero. **"What we think is money is but a grand illusion. The reality is debt."**

## Redefining Usury

Griffin argues that the traditional concept of interest needs to be re-examined once you accept that banks aren't lending out real, earned, saved money — they're lending out money conjured on the spot.

- An **honest loan**, in Griffin's framing, is one where a lender temporarily surrenders the use of an asset they actually possess (their savings, their car, etc.), and interest is fair rent for that surrendered use.
- A loan of freshly created fiat money is different: the bank has *nothing* to surrender. It types a number into an account.

From this, Griffin proposes a new definition:

> **Usury:** The charging of any interest on a loan of fiat money.

He illustrates the scale of this with a mortgage example: on a $70,000 loan at 11% over 30 years, total interest paid comes to **$167,806** — about 2.5 times what's paid to the people who actually supplied the labor and materials for the house. Griffin's question: what is the "time-value" of money that cost the lender nothing to create?

He calls this **"usury of the highest magnitude"** and treats it as **the third reason he offers in the book for abolishing the Federal Reserve System** — that it functions as *"the supreme instrument of usury."*

---

## Where Does the Money to Pay the Interest Come From?

Griffin raises and answers a classic objection to fiat-money critiques: if a bank creates $10,000 for a loan but requires $10,900 back, where does the extra $900 come from, if it was never created?

His answer: **it comes from labor**, not from more borrowed money. Using an example of doing part-time work (in his illustration, literally waxing the bank's floors) to earn the $900 in interest, Griffin argues that the *same dollars* cycle continuously — paid as interest, spent as wages, and redeposited as loan repayment. The essential point, for Griffin, isn't the arithmetic — it's that **all interest is ultimately paid through human effort**, and that effort flows, in aggregate, toward those who create the fiat money in the first place. He describes this as **"a form of modern serfdom in which the great mass of society works as indentured servants to a ruling class of financial nobility."**

## How the Mechanism Works: The Overview

Griffin gives a compressed, one-paragraph version of the entire mechanism:

1. The Fed buys government bonds that the public doesn't buy, paying with a check backed by nothing.
2. These bonds are relabeled "reserves."
3. On top of those "reserves," the banking system creates **9 additional dollars for every 1 dollar** created for the bonds themselves.
4. The money created for the bonds funds government spending; the money created *on top of* the bonds becomes the source of all commercial bank loans.

The net effect, in Griffin's words, is the same as running a printing press — except the trick is done through **accounting** rather than a literal printing press, which makes it much less visible to the public. Congress gets unlimited funding without directly raising taxes; the banking cartel gets **"a perpetual override on every American dollar that exists in the world."**

---

## How the Mechanism Works: Step by Step

Griffin walks through the detailed mechanics as a chain reaction, where each stage relabels the same value with a new, more innocuous-sounding accounting term:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Government Debt** | The federal government issues a bond/Treasury note — a promise to pay, backed by its taxing power. |
| **2. Securities Asset** | The bond is given to the Fed, which books it as an "asset." |
| **3. Federal Reserve Check** | The Fed writes a check to the government against this "asset" — with no actual money behind it. |
| **4. Government Deposit** | The government deposits the check. |
| **5. Government Checks** | The government spends it — this is the "first wave" of new fiat money entering the economy. |
| **6. Commercial Bank Deposits** | Recipients deposit these checks into their own banks. |
| **7. Bank Reserves** | The deposits are relabeled "reserves" at the commercial bank. |
| **8. Excess Reserves** | Since banks must hold only ~10% in reserve, the other ~90% becomes "excess" and available to lend. |
| **9. Bank Loans** | This "excess" is lent out as **brand-new money**, not the original depositors' money — the money supply itself has now grown. |
| **10. More Commercial Bank Deposits** | The new loan money is deposited again, and the cycle (7→9) repeats — around **28 times**, with each pass slightly smaller — until it plays out. |
| **11. Bank Fiat Money ≈ 9× Government Debt** | The cumulative effect of the bank-lending cycle is roughly 9 times the original bond amount. |
| **12. Total Fiat Money ≈ 10× Government Debt** | Add back the original amount created for the bond itself, and total new money is about 10 times the underlying government debt. |
| **13. Hidden Tax** | As this new money floods the economy relative to available goods and services, purchasing power falls — prices rise. Griffin treats this as functionally identical to a tax, just an invisible one. |
| **14. Booms, Busts, Depressions** | Because the money supply expands and contracts along with debt levels (people borrowing more vs. paying down and refusing to re-borrow), the system inherently produces cycles of expansion and contraction. |

### The Three Levers the Fed Uses

Griffin identifies three distinct methods by which the Fed drives this process:

1. **The Discount Window** — direct loans to member banks. Griffin walks through an example: a bank borrows $1 million from the Fed at 8% ($80,000 annual cost), treats it as a deposit, and on a ~10% reserve requirement can lend out $9 million more at 11% (~$990,000 gross return) — turning a borrowed $1 million into roughly $900,000 in net profit within a year.
2. **Open Market Operations** — the Fed's purchase and sale of government securities on the open market. Griffin calls this the *most important* of the three methods, and warns the reader that the terminology involved ("reserves," "assets," etc.) is "not meant to explain but to deceive."
3. **Reserve Ratio changes** — since the reserve requirement (e.g., 10%) is arbitrary and not tied to any physical backing, raising or lowering it directly changes how many dollars of new money each dollar of "reserves" can generate.

## Debt That Can Never Be Repaid

Griffin argues the system creates a structural trap: because the money supply is pyramided roughly 10-to-1 on top of government debt, **paying down that debt would cause the money supply to shrink dramatically** — he estimates that even eliminating the ~7% of debt the Fed itself holds would shrink the money supply by about 70 cents for every dollar of debt eliminated. His conclusion: **"as long as the Federal Reserve exists, America will be, must be, in debt."** Debt reduction, in this framework, isn't just politically unpopular — it's economically destabilizing to a money supply built entirely on IOUs.

He also notes that national debt and inflation, while usually linked, aren't strictly the same thing:
- Bonds sold to **private buyers** (using money that already exists) are *not* inflationary.
- Bonds monetized by the **Fed/banking system** (creating new money) *are* inflationary — this was, in Griffin's account, the actual mechanism behind the money-supply expansion leading into the 1929 crash, even while the government was running surpluses at the time.

---

## "Taxes Not Even Necessary" — Griffin on Beardsley Ruml

Griffin points out that, in principle, the federal government could fund all its spending purely by monetizing debt through the Fed, without levying any taxes at all — and in practice, this is largely already how it works. So why does the government still tax?

He cites **Beardsley Ruml**, then-Chairman of the New York Fed, writing in a 1946 article titled *"Taxes for Revenue Are Obsolete."* Per Ruml (as Griffin presents him), taxes serve two purposes once a government controls a central bank and a non-gold-backed currency:

1. **Controlling inflation** — pulling money back out of public hands to offset the inflationary effect of government spending.
2. **Redistribution of wealth** — using progressive income, estate, and gift taxes to reshape the distribution of wealth in society, justified in the name of "social justice" or "equality."

Griffin frames this as evidence that taxation, in the modern system, isn't primarily about funding government — it's a **tool of social planning**, working in tandem with the Fed's money-creation power. He also floats a second, more cynical reason for keeping taxes around even though they're not strictly necessary: if the government relied *purely* on monetized debt, he suggests, the public might start asking uncomfortable questions about where the money is really coming from.

---

## Expansion Leads to Contraction

Griffin closes the mechanical explanation by noting the process runs in reverse too: when the Fed **sells** bonds/debt instruments (rather than buying them), money is extinguished the same way it was created — it "disappears into the inkwell or computer chip from which it came." The same contraction can also happen organically, without the Fed's help, if the public simply becomes reluctant to borrow (due to recession fears, political uncertainty, war, etc.) — old debts get paid down and not replaced, and the money supply shrinks along with them.

Griffin considers this expansion/contraction cycle — driven by nothing more than confidence and borrowing behavior, rather than supply and demand for real goods — to be **the root mechanical cause of boom-bust cycles** throughout history wherever fiat money has existed.

## Griffin's Stated Conclusions (Chapter Summary)

From the chapter's own summary, the key takeaways Griffin wants the reader to walk away with:

- The American dollar has **no intrinsic value**; it is fiat money with no cap on the quantity that can be produced.
- It is more accurate to say money is created **out of debt** than "out of nothing" — meaning, in Griffin's phrasing, money is created out of *less than nothing*.
- The **entire money supply would vanish** if all debts were repaid — which is why, in his view, the system cannot tolerate serious debt reduction.
- Charging interest on money that was never really lent (in the sense of being surrendered by someone who earned it) is, by Griffin's redefinition, **usury**, and it has become institutionalized.
- The Mandrake Mechanism is deliberately confusing — not because the underlying process is complex, but because **the terminology is designed to obscure rather than clarify**.
- The end result of the Mechanism is the **artificial expansion of the money supply**, which Griffin identifies as the actual root cause of inflation (the "hidden tax"), and the alternating expansion/contraction of that same supply is what produces **boom-bust cycles**.
