# Did a Supreme Court Case About a Hillary Clinton Documentary Give Us "Woke" Corporations?

## How *Citizens United v. FEC* and the rise of stakeholder capitalism became tangled together in the public imagination — and what the evidence actually shows

If you've spent any time in the last decade reading about corporate America, you've probably run into two stories that get told separately but increasingly feel like they're about the same thing.

Story one: in 2010, the Supreme Court decided *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* and "opened the floodgates" for corporate money in politics.

Story two: sometime around the same decade, big companies started talking less about shareholders and more about "stakeholders" — employees, communities, the planet, customers, "society." BlackRock's Larry Fink started writing letters about "purpose." The Business Roundtable renounced shareholder primacy. Companies started taking positions on voting laws, climate change, and social movements. Critics on the right, most prominently the entrepreneur-turned-politician Vivek Ramaswamy, started calling this "woke capitalism" and arguing it was corroding both democracy and capitalism itself.

The tempting move is to connect story one to story two: *Citizens United* freed corporations to act as political actors, and stakeholder capitalism is what that political activism looks like when it's dressed up in the language of sustainability and social responsibility.

That connection is intuitive. It is also, on close inspection, mostly wrong — or at least much weaker and more indirect than it first appears. The honest answer is that these are two largely separate historical currents that happened to crest in the same cultural moment, and untangling them is more interesting than collapsing them into one story.

This piece walks through both currents in detail, shows where they do and don't actually intersect, and tries to represent fairly the people who think they're deeply connected and the people who think that's a category error.

## Part One: What *Citizens United* Actually Was

### What was Citizens United, the case?

*Citizens United* wasn't originally about corporate political spending in some abstract, big-picture sense. It started as a narrow dispute over one film.

Citizens United is a nonprofit conservative advocacy organization. In 2008, it produced a documentary called *Hillary: The Movie* — a scathing critique of Hillary Clinton, then a candidate for the Democratic presidential nomination — and wanted to air it on video-on-demand and advertise it on television close to the primaries.

The problem was the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 (better known as McCain–Feingold), which banned corporations and unions from using their general treasury funds to pay for "electioneering communications" — broadcast ads that named a candidate — within 30 days of a primary or 60 days of a general election. Citizens United was organized as a nonprofit corporation, and this law reached nonprofit advocacy corporations, not just for-profit ones. The organization argued the film wasn't really "electioneering" but political commentary, and even if it was, the ban was unconstitutional.

### Why was the case brought?

The case grew out of two earlier precedents the government relied on: *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* (1990), which upheld a state law banning corporations from using treasury funds for independent campaign expenditures, and *McConnell v. FEC* (2003), which upheld McCain–Feingold's electioneering-communication rules. Citizens United's lawyers ultimately asked the Court to do something much bigger than rule on one film: overturn *Austin* itself. The Supreme Court took the unusual step of ordering the case reargued specifically on that broader question.<cite index="9-1">On January 21, 2010, the Supreme Court issued a ruling in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission overruling an earlier decision, Austin v. Michigan State Chamber of Commerce, that allowed prohibitions on independent expenditures by corporations.</cite>

### What exactly did the Supreme Court decide?

In a 5–4 decision, the majority — Justice Anthony Kennedy writing, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Alito, and Thomas — <cite index="2-1">concluded that limiting "independent political spending" by corporations and other groups violated the First Amendment.</cite> Two things are worth being precise about, because popular summaries often blur them.

**What changed:** Corporations and unions could now spend unlimited amounts of money from their own general treasuries on *independent expenditures* — ads and other communications advocating for or against a candidate, so long as that spending is not coordinated with a candidate's campaign or a party. <cite index="6-1">The Supreme Court ruled that laws restricting the political spending of corporations and unions are inconsistent with the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment.</cite>

**What didn't change:** <cite index="9-1">The Court's ruling did not affect the ban on corporate contributions</cite> directly to candidates or parties — that older prohibition, dating to the Tillman Act of 1907, stayed in place. And the Court actually <cite index="4-1">upheld provisions of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act that required disclosure of who funded the speech</cite>, with only Justice Thomas dissenting on that point.

### What arguments did the majority make?

The majority's reasoning centered on a First Amendment principle rather than an economic or corporate-governance one: that the government generally may not restrict speech based on the *identity* of the speaker. Kennedy's opinion held that <cite index="1-1">"speech restrictions based on the identity of the speaker are all too often simply a means to control content."</cite> If Congress can't ban an individual citizen from spending money to criticize a candidate, the majority reasoned, it can't ban a group of citizens organized as a corporation from doing the same thing — because corporations are, in this view, simply associations of people exercising a collective right they already hold individually.

The majority also rejected the government's core justification — that unlimited independent spending would lead to corruption or the appearance of corruption. Kennedy wrote that <cite index="5-1">"the anticorruption interest is not sufficient to displace the speech here in question,"</cite> largely because *independent* expenditures, by definition uncoordinated with a candidate, don't create the same quid-pro-quo risk as direct contributions. The majority's institutional answer to the risk of corporate influence wasn't spending limits — it was transparency: as Kennedy put it, <cite index="5-1">"disclosure is a less restrictive alternative to more comprehensive regulations of speech."</cite> Chief Justice Roberts, in a separate concurrence, emphasized that the Court was following its own principle of not disturbing precedent lightly — but that *Austin* itself had been a significant break from prior First Amendment law, which made it fair game to reconsider.

### What arguments did the dissent make?

Justice John Paul Stevens wrote the principal dissent, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor. Its core objection was that the majority's speaker-neutral framing ignored a real, functional difference between corporations and natural persons: <cite index="52-1">corporations can "amass and deploy financial resources on a scale few natural persons can match."</cite> The dissent argued the majority had answered a much broader question than the case required, reaching out to overturn *Austin* and part of *McConnell* when a narrower ruling about the specific film could have resolved the dispute.

Stevens warned about consequences, writing that the ruling <cite index="2-1">"threatens to undermine the integrity of elected institutions across the Nation."</cite> The dissent's deeper theoretical claim was that corporations are not simply voluntary associations of citizens exercising individual speech rights collectively — they are legal constructs, created by the state, with structural features (limited liability, perpetual life, the ability to aggregate enormous capital) that make them different in kind, not just degree, from citizens' groups, and that a democracy is entitled to treat that difference as constitutionally relevant.

It's worth being clear that this is a genuine, long-running disagreement in First Amendment jurisprudence, not simply a partisan split invented for this case. Versions of both positions predate *Citizens United* by decades — the majority's view traces back through *First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti* (1978), which had already established some corporate speech rights, while the dissent's view traces back through *Austin* and earlier campaign-finance jurisprudence rooted in *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976).

## Part Two: What Actually Happened to Corporate Political Spending

### How did the ruling change corporate political spending, mechanically?

The most direct and immediate effect wasn't corporations writing checks from their own treasuries — it was the creation of a new legal vehicle: the **Super PAC**. Because *Citizens United* (combined with a related D.C. Circuit case, *SpeechNow.org v. FEC*, decided weeks later) established that independent-expenditure-only committees could accept unlimited contributions from individuals, corporations, and unions, a new kind of political committee sprang up that could raise unlimited sums as long as it didn't coordinate with candidates.<cite index="2-1">This led to the creation of "Super PACs," political action committees with immense power, while traditional PACs could donate directly to a candidate's campaign but were subject to contribution limits.</cite>

### Did corporations actually become more politically active — as direct corporate spenders?

Here the honest answer is more complicated than either side's slogans suggest, and this is one of the more genuinely contested empirical questions in the whole story.

**The case that spending surged:** Independent expenditure spending overall rose sharply and kept rising. Academic researchers studying the ruling as a natural experiment — since it invalidated corporate-spending bans that had existed in 23 states — found real effects: <cite index="53-1">spending increased in all states post-Citizens United, and the increase in independent expenditures was twice as large in states that had previously restricted corporate spending</cite>, and separate research found the ruling <cite index="53-1">led to significant increases in political advertising.</cite> A 2025 analysis from the advocacy group Public Citizen found that <cite index="45-1">corporations have spent $1.58 billion on federal elections since Citizens United, with nearly one third of that spending occurring in a single recent election cycle</cite> — evidence, in their reading, of an accelerating trend.

**The case that direct corporate spending has actually been fairly restrained:** A large share of researchers and even watchdog groups have pointed out something that complicates the "corporations took over elections" narrative: most of the *dramatic* growth in outside spending has come not from publicly traded operating companies spending out of their own treasuries, but from wealthy individuals, privately held companies, trade associations, and "dark money" nonprofits that don't have to disclose their donors. As the government watchdog group CREW has put it, <cite index="48-1">while Citizens United did loosen the reins on direct corporate spending, the decision's biggest impact may be empowering donors to use small, unknown corporations to obscure and hide influence-peddling</cite> — rather than household-name public corporations spending openly under their own brand.

There's a real reason for this caution, and it's a market-based one rather than a legal one: publicly traded companies answer to diverse shareholder bases, customers, and employees who may disagree with any specific political position, and overt partisan spending carries brand and boycott risk that a privately held company or a single billionaire doesn't face in the same way. Several corporate-governance scholars, including Lucian Bebchuk and Robert Jackson (the latter later an SEC commissioner), have written that most large public companies have in fact remained fairly conservative about direct, visible treasury spending on candidates — precisely because shareholders in diversified portfolios often don't want the reputational risk, and because such spending is subject to little internal governance oversight, which itself became a shareholder-activism issue (pushing for more *disclosure* of corporate political spending, not more of it).

**What the tax-and-policy research finds:** For those hoping to trace concrete downstream *policy* effects, the results are genuinely mixed. One widely cited study using the state-law natural experiment found states forced to lift spending bans <cite index="52-1">cut corporate tax rates by 4 to 8 percent and passed more business-friendly tort laws</cite> relative to unaffected states. But a separate, more recent peer-reviewed study using similar methodology reached a more cautious conclusion, finding <cite index="47-1">no statistically significant effects of corporate independent expenditures on most state tax policy outcomes</cite> — with the exception of some effect on corporate tax rates and revenues specifically. Reasonable, well-credentialed economists using similar quasi-experimental designs have reached different conclusions here, which should temper any confident claim about exactly how much concrete policy influence flowed from the decision.

**The reasonable synthesis:** Total independent expenditures did rise substantially and Super PACs are now a permanent fixture of American elections; that part isn't seriously disputed. What's contested is how much of that increase reflects public corporations *as such* becoming meaningfully more politically assertive, versus wealthy individuals and privately held entities using the same legal architecture. Both things are true simultaneously, and which one you emphasize tends to track which broader story you're trying to tell about *Citizens United*'s legacy.

## Part Three: The Separate Story of Shareholder and Stakeholder Capitalism

Now set campaign finance aside almost entirely, because the second story has its own, much older lineage — one that traces back decades before 2010 and has almost nothing to do with election law.

### What is shareholder capitalism?

Shareholder capitalism (sometimes called "shareholder primacy") is the view that a corporation's fundamental purpose is to maximize returns to its shareholders, within the bounds of law and basic ethical norms, and that pursuing other social goals is either not the corporation's job or should only be done insofar as it ultimately serves shareholder value.

Its most influential single statement is Milton Friedman's 1970 *New York Times Magazine* essay, "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits." <cite index="19-1">Friedman opened by comparing businessmen who talk about social responsibility to a man who discovered he'd been speaking prose his whole life without realizing it — suggesting they don't understand what they're actually doing.</cite> His central argument was definitional and almost legalistic: <cite index="19-1">only people, not abstractions like "business," can have responsibilities, and a corporate executive's responsibility is to the people who employ him — the shareholders — to conduct the business in accordance with their wishes, which generally means making as much money as possible while conforming to society's basic rules, both law and ethical custom.</cite> An executive who spends corporate money on social causes, in Friedman's framing, is essentially spending other people's money (shareholders', employees', or customers') without their consent — a kind of unlegislated taxation. Friedman didn't invent the underlying idea (it has roots in agency theory in economics and finance going back further), but his essay became, and remains, the doctrine's canonical text — often just called "the Friedman Doctrine."

### What is stakeholder capitalism?

Stakeholder capitalism holds that a corporation's management has genuine obligations not just to shareholders but to a broader set of "stakeholders" — employees, customers, suppliers, communities, and sometimes the environment or society at large — and that these interests should be actively weighed in corporate decision-making, not treated merely as constraints on profit-seeking.

The theory's foundational academic text is R. Edward Freeman's 1984 book *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach*. <cite index="33-1">Freeman defined a stakeholder as any group or individual "who can affect or is affected by" an organization's success</cite> — deliberately a play on the word "stockholder," designed to broaden strategic thinking beyond shareholders alone. Freeman's original motivation was less a moral crusade than a practical management problem: he observed that <cite index="31-1">"our current theories are inconsistent with both the quantity and kinds of change that are occurring in the business environment of the 1980's… A new conceptual framework is needed,"</cite> arguing companies navigating a turbulent 1980s business environment (oil shocks, consumer advocacy, regulatory change) needed a framework that accounted for pressure from groups beyond just capital markets. Notably, <cite index="35-1">when the book was first published, Freeman didn't expect it to make much of a splash — in his own words, "no one cared."</cite>

### How are the two actually different?

The disagreement is not primarily about whether companies should obey the law or treat employees decently — both camps generally agree on that baseline. The real dispute is about **the proper objective function of the firm** and, relatedly, **who corporate managers are ultimately answerable to.**

- Shareholder-primacy theorists see the corporation as, at bottom, a nexus of contracts serving the residual claimants (shareholders bear the risk of the business failing, so they get the reward when it succeeds, and management's fiduciary duty runs to them).
- Stakeholder theorists see the corporation as a social institution embedded in a web of relationships, whose long-run value creation actually *depends on* balancing multiple constituencies, and whose managers have some independent duty of care toward those constituencies, not merely an instrumental one.

A more moderate, middle position — sometimes called "enlightened shareholder value" — holds that attending to stakeholders is usually good *for* shareholders in the long run (happy employees, trusted communities, and loyal customers tend to produce more durable profits), collapsing much of the apparent disagreement into a question of time horizon rather than ultimate values. Whether that reconciliation actually works, or just papers over a real conflict that emerges whenever stakeholder and shareholder interests genuinely diverge, is itself one of the more contested questions in the business-ethics literature.

### Why did stakeholder capitalism become more prominent during the 2010s specifically?

This is where several independent trends converged, and it's worth listing them separately because collapsing them into one cause is exactly the kind of oversimplification this article is trying to avoid.

**1. The 2008 financial crisis.** Trust in the "shareholder value above all" model of Wall Street and corporate governance took a severe hit when that model appeared to produce reckless risk-taking, a housing collapse, and a bailout paid for by ordinary taxpayers. This created fertile ground for arguments that pure shareholder-value maximization had gone wrong somewhere.

**2. The rise of index investing and the "Big Three" asset managers.** This is a distinct and underappreciated economic trend. BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street grew enormously as passive index investing displaced active stock-picking, and by the mid-2010s these three firms collectively held large stakes across nearly every major public company in America — meaning they couldn't simply sell a stock they disagreed with (the traditional shareholder response, sometimes called the "Wall Street walk"). Instead, they had strong incentives to engage directly with company management on long-term, systemic risks — including climate change, governance quality, and workforce issues — because as universal, permanent owners of the whole market, negative externalities that hurt one company but help another within their portfolio don't actually benefit them the way they might benefit a single active stock-picker. <cite index="26-1">As BlackRock's own 2018 letter noted, "investors' increasing use of index funds is driving a transformation in BlackRock's fiduciary responsibility and the wider landscape of corporate governance."</cite> This is arguably the single most underdiscussed structural driver of the stakeholder-capitalism trend in popular commentary — it's a story about market structure and ownership concentration, not ideology.

**3. Larry Fink's CEO letters.** Since 2012, BlackRock's chairman and CEO Larry Fink has published <cite index="21-1">an annual letter to the CEOs of companies BlackRock invests in on behalf of its clients</cite>. His 2018 letter, titled "A Sense of Purpose," became a watershed moment in the corporate world, arguing <cite index="26-1">that in the current business environment, stakeholders "are demanding that companies exercise leadership on a broader range of issues," and that a company's ability to manage environmental, social, and governance matters "demonstrates the leadership and good governance that is so essential to sustainable growth."</cite> Fink explicitly connected this to financial performance and risk management rather than altruism — he argued companies without a long-term "sense of purpose" would <cite index="22-1">"succumb to short-term pressures" and "remain exposed to activist campaigns," ultimately providing "subpar returns to the investors who depend on it."</cite> Legal commentators quickly framed this as a direct break from the older orthodoxy: as one law firm's governance blog put it, <cite index="27-1">Fink had "firmly rejected Milton Friedman's shareholder-primacy governance and embraced sustainability and stakeholder-focused governance."</cite>

**4. Klaus Schwab and the World Economic Forum.** Schwab, founder of the WEF, has been promoting "stakeholder capitalism" as an explicit label and organizing framework since the WEF's founding in 1971 — predating even Freeman's academic work — and pushed it heavily in the 2010s and especially after his 2021 book *Stakeholder Capitalism: A Global Economy that Works for Progress, People and Planet*, which argued for reorienting global business and government cooperation around the interests of all stakeholders rather than narrow shareholder or national returns. The WEF's Davos meetings became a symbolic staging ground where CEOs, government officials, and NGO leaders discussed this vision, making "Davos Man" both a real network and a potent cultural symbol for critics.

**5. The Business Roundtable's 2019 statement.** <cite index="35-1">In 2019, more than 200 CEOs affiliated with the Business Roundtable officially adopted a new "Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation,"</cite> explicitly moving away from the organization's own prior 1997 statement, which had endorsed shareholder primacy, and committing instead to delivering value to customers, employees, suppliers, and communities alongside shareholders.

### What role did ESG and corporate political/social activism play?

ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) investing is best understood as the *financial-industry operationalization* of stakeholder thinking — a set of metrics, ratings, and disclosure frameworks that let asset managers, pension funds, and analysts try to quantify how well companies manage stakeholder-relevant risks (climate exposure, labor practices, board diversity, executive pay ratios, and so on) and factor that into investment decisions. It grew rapidly through the 2010s alongside the broader stakeholder trend, becoming both a genuine risk-management tool for some investors and, for critics, a vehicle for injecting political and cultural preferences into supposedly neutral capital allocation.

Corporate political and social activism — companies weighing in publicly on issues like voting rights legislation, LGBTQ rights, gun policy, or racial justice, especially visible after events like the 2020 murder of George Floyd — is a related but distinct phenomenon: less about investment metrics and more about brand positioning, employee and consumer expectations, and executives responding to (or trying to get ahead of) public pressure campaigns and social media dynamics. It's this strand — companies as vocal, visible participants in cultural and political debates — that draws the sharpest public backlash and that critics most often label "woke capitalism."

## Part Four: Vivek Ramaswamy's Critique in *Woke, Inc.*

### How does Ramaswamy interpret these developments?

Ramaswamy's 2021 book *Woke, Inc.: Inside Corporate America's Social Justice Scam* makes a specific and fairly aggressive causal argument. His account, as summarized by reviewers and the book's own marketing, holds that <cite index="38-1">Wokenomics emerged when companies discovered they could boost profits by claiming to fight for social justice — a discovery that came after the 2008 financial crisis, when public blame for American inequality shifted from economic policy toward narratives about systemic injustice, and companies badly needing to repair their battered reputations learned to capitalize on the simultaneous rise of stakeholder capitalism and progressive social movements.</cite> In Ramaswamy's telling, this isn't sincere moral conviction on the part of corporate leadership — it's a strategic performance. He argues large companies have <cite index="43-1">"come to pretend like they care about something other than profit and power, precisely to gain more of each."</cite>

Ramaswamy's argument is explicitly about the *fusion* of two things he treats as mutually reinforcing but analytically distinct: stakeholder capitalism as an economic ideology, and progressive cultural politics (he specifically names Critical Race Theory) as a moral vocabulary that stakeholder capitalism borrows to justify itself and insulate itself from criticism.

His book also makes a direct connection to political spending and campaign-finance-adjacent themes, though not primarily through *Citizens United* itself — he frames it more broadly as a threat to democratic accountability: <cite index="39-1">he argues that stakeholder capitalism demands that corporations use money to influence politics according to values of their choosing, and that while every vote counts equally in an election, in stakeholder capitalism dollars influence outcomes instead — a dynamic he says "poisons democracy," just as he argues partisan politics in turn "poisons capitalism."</cite>

### Does Ramaswamy argue stakeholder capitalism is a departure from traditional shareholder capitalism?

Yes, explicitly and centrally — this is essentially the spine of the book's economic argument, not an incidental point. <cite index="40-1">A significant part of the book is structured around the debate between the virtues of stakeholder capitalism and shareholder capitalism</cite>, and Ramaswamy comes down firmly on the Friedman side: he wants a return to a model where executives run companies to maximize shareholder returns and keep personal or institutional political and social convictions out of corporate decision-making, treating the shareholder-primacy era prior to roughly the 2010s as the healthier and more honest baseline from which stakeholder capitalism represents a significant, and largely unwelcome, departure.

Ramaswamy is not, notably, defending the *legal* reasoning of *Citizens United* — his book isn't primarily a campaign-finance-law argument. His objection is closer to a *cultural and governance* one: that corporate leaders shouldn't be using the *economic power* they hold as fiduciaries — power over other people's capital, jobs, and consumer choices — to advance personal or institutional political and cultural preferences, regardless of whether the specific mechanism is a Super PAC ad, a public statement, an HR policy, or an ESG-linked investment decision.

### Do other scholars and commentators agree or disagree with this framing?

This is genuinely contested, and it's worth laying out the range of serious disagreement rather than picking a winner.

**Sympathetic and partially sympathetic views:** Some corporate-governance and legal scholars share Ramaswamy's underlying discomfort with concentrating unaccountable social and political power in corporate management and large asset managers, even if they don't share his specific "scam" framing. Critics of the "Big Three" asset managers' influence — including some antitrust scholars and legal academics across the political spectrum — have separately raised concerns that BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street's ability to vote enormous blocks of shares on ESG-related shareholder proposals concentrates a remarkable amount of unelected, unaccountable influence over corporate behavior and, by extension, public policy outcomes in a small number of unelected executives — a concern that exists independently of whether one thinks the *content* of that influence (climate risk management, workplace diversity policies) is good or bad.

**Skeptical and critical views:** Other observers argue Ramaswamy's framing conflates too many distinct things under one label. A review in the *LSE Business Review* argued <cite index="43-1">Ramaswamy "loses the plot in equating the stakeholder philosophy with 'wokeism,'"</cite> and suggested his more sweeping claims about a "marriage between wokeism and stakeholder capitalism" work well as rhetoric but obscure the underlying, decades-old management-theory debate. Some stakeholder-theory scholars, following in Freeman's tradition, argue the theory long predates and is analytically separable from the specific 2010s-era cultural fights over race, gender, and identity that Ramaswamy focuses on — that stakeholder theory is fundamentally a claim about *whose interests matter in corporate decision-making*, applicable in principle to a company weighing supplier relationships or local environmental impact just as easily as to a company issuing a statement on a hot-button social issue, and that treating stakeholder theory as inherently "woke" mistakes one, culturally salient application of the theory for the theory itself.

**A more structural/economic counter-framing:** Other economists and business historians would push back on Ramaswamy's "scam" framing not on ideological grounds but on incentive grounds: they'd argue that if consumer and employee expectations really have shifted — younger workers reportedly caring more about employer values, certain consumer segments rewarding or punishing brands for public stances — then companies responding to those preferences isn't fundamentally different from companies responding to any other market signal, and calling it a "scam" imports a normative judgment about whether those preferences are legitimate rather than describing the underlying economic mechanism, which in this reading is just ordinary demand-responsiveness.

### Are there alternative explanations for the rise of corporate activism that don't center Citizens United, stakeholder theory, or a top-down corporate "scam" at all?

Yes — several, and they deserve to be taken seriously in their own right rather than folded into either the "campaign finance" story or the "corporate scam" story.

- **Employee power and the talent market.** In a tight labor market for skilled and educated workers, especially through the 2010s tech and professional-services boom, employees gained real leverage to push employers on values-related issues (walkouts, open letters, internal advocacy), and companies competing for talent responded to that pressure directly — this is a labor-market story, not fundamentally a political-spending or investment-theory story.
- **Social media and reputational risk dynamics.** The rise of platforms that could rapidly organize boycotts, viral criticism, or public pressure campaigns changed the cost-benefit calculation around public silence versus public statements for consumer-facing brands in ways that predate and are independent of both *Citizens United* and formal stakeholder theory.
- **Generational shifts in consumer preferences**, documented (imperfectly) in various market research, toward valuing brand "authenticity" and perceived values-alignment, particularly among younger consumers — again, an ordinary market-responsiveness story.
- **Genuine, sincere belief among some executives and boards.** It's worth taking seriously the possibility — not universally true, but not universally false either — that at least some corporate leaders hold sincere views on issues like climate risk or workplace equity independent of any calculated brand strategy, simply because they are individuals embedded in the same broader cultural shifts as everyone else.

Any complete explanation for the 2010s rise in corporate social and political visibility probably has to weave several of these threads together, alongside the ESG/index-investing story above — which is exactly why reducing it to a single cause, whether that cause is "Citizens United" or "a corporate scam," tends to oversimplify.

## Part Five: So Are These Two Stories Actually Connected?

This is the question the article opened with, and it's worth answering directly and carefully rather than hedging indefinitely.

**The direct legal and mechanical connection is weak.** *Citizens United* is about a corporation's right to spend money on *independent political advertising for or against a candidate*. Stakeholder capitalism, ESG investing, and corporate statements on social issues are, legally and mechanically, almost entirely unrelated activities — they don't involve election law, the FEC, PACs, or campaign contributions at all. A company issuing a statement supporting or opposing a piece of state legislation, adjusting its supply-chain labor standards, or setting internal diversity targets needed no permission from *Citizens United* and would have been just as legal before 2010 as after. Freeman's stakeholder theory (1984) and Friedman's shareholder doctrine (1970) both predate the case by decades, as does the WEF's stakeholder framing (1971). The causal arrow from the case to the broader corporate-purpose debate simply isn't there in any direct legal sense.

**There is, however, a plausible indirect and cultural connection, which is different from a legal one.** Several threads worth taking seriously:

1. *Citizens United* substantially raised the *public salience* of the question "how much power should corporations have over public life?" — even though the case was narrowly about campaign spending, it became a broad cultural touchstone and shorthand for corporate power generally, priming public attention (and suspicion) that made both later corporate political activism *and* stakeholder-capitalism rhetoric read, to much of the public, as part of one continuous story about corporations acquiring outsized influence — regardless of whether the underlying mechanisms were actually related.
2. The ruling arguably lowered the perceived *cost* of corporate political visibility broadly conceived — normalizing corporations as legitimate, vocal participants in public debate — even though the specific legal permission it granted (independent expenditures) is a narrow slice of what "corporate political activism" now covers.
3. Both trends are, at a deep level, symptoms of the same broader phenomenon: an expanding conception of what corporations are entitled or expected to do beyond their traditional core economic function — one legal (spend on elections), one managerial/cultural (advocate on issues, weigh stakeholder interests). They may share a common root in a decades-long trend of corporations becoming more central, powerful, and visible actors in American public life generally, of which both *Citizens United* and stakeholder capitalism are downstream effects rather than one being the cause of the other.

**The most defensible summary:** *Citizens United* and the rise of stakeholder capitalism are best understood as parallel, largely independent developments in the broader story of expanding corporate power and visibility in American public life, connected more by shared historical moment and public perception than by direct causation. People who conflate them — treating stakeholder capitalism as simply "*Citizens United*'s political spending, laundered through ESG language" — are telling a simpler and more rhetorically powerful story than the evidence supports. But people who insist the two are *completely* unrelated understate the real, if diffuse, way that one high-salience legal battle over corporate power helped set the cultural stage on which a second, mostly independent debate over corporate purpose later played out.

## Synthesis: The Major Schools of Thought

It's useful to lay out where different camps actually land, since "sides" here don't map neatly onto a single left-right axis.

| School of thought | Core claim |
|---|---|
| **Legal formalists (majority view in *Citizens United*)** | Corporate political speech restrictions are a First Amendment problem regardless of downstream corporate behavior; the case should be evaluated on speech-rights grounds alone. |
| **Campaign-finance reformers** | *Citizens United* meaningfully increased the political power of concentrated capital (corporate and individual) and should be curtailed or overturned, largely independent of the stakeholder-capitalism debate. |
| **Shareholder primacists (Friedman tradition)** | Corporate purpose should remain narrowly economic; stakeholder capitalism represents mission creep that both under-serves shareholders and illegitimately expands corporate power into domains — political and cultural — where it doesn't belong. |
| **Stakeholder theorists (Freeman tradition)** | Corporate purpose was always broader than pure profit maximization in practice; formalizing attention to stakeholders produces both more durable value and a more ethically defensible model of the firm, and predates recent political controversies by decades. |
| **Index-investor/market-structure realists** | The real driver of ESG and stakeholder rhetoric is the structural rise of universal, diversified ownership by a few giant asset managers — a market-structure story with its own logic, not primarily an ideological or campaign-finance-driven one. |
| **Ramaswamy/"woke capitalism" critics** | Stakeholder capitalism and progressive cultural politics have merged into a mutually reinforcing, largely performative alliance that concentrates unaccountable power in corporate and financial elites while undermining both authentic capitalism and democratic accountability. |
| **Stakeholder-theory defenders and critics of the "woke capitalism" framing** | The "woke capitalism" critique conflates a decades-old, analytically distinct management theory with a narrower and more recent set of culturally charged corporate statements, overstating both the coherence and the novelty of what's being criticized. |

## Unanswered Questions

A few genuinely open questions remain, where reasonable, well-informed people still disagree:

- **How much did *Citizens United* actually change corporate behavior**, as opposed to simply changing which vehicles (Super PACs, dark-money nonprofits) wealthy individuals and organizations use to spend money that was already flowing into politics through other channels?
- **Is stakeholder capitalism, as practiced, actually different from sophisticated long-run shareholder value maximization**, or does "enlightened shareholder value" fully absorb the stakeholder critique once you extend the time horizon — making the whole debate more semantic than substantive in most real-world cases?
- **Is corporate ESG and social activism primarily investor-driven (top-down, from asset managers like BlackRock), employee/consumer-driven (bottom-up, from market pressure), or genuinely values-driven (from individual executives and boards)** — and does the answer vary so much by company and by issue that a single unified theory is the wrong ambition to begin with?
- **Does concentrated index-fund ownership by BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street represent a legitimate, market-driven form of corporate engagement, or an under-scrutinized concentration of unaccountable power that deserves its own separate regulatory conversation** — one that, notably, cuts across the usual left-right lines of the "woke capitalism" debate?
- **Will the apparent retreat from explicit "stakeholder" and "ESG" branding by many companies and asset managers in the mid-2020s (amid political and legal backlash) represent a genuine reversal of the underlying practices, or mostly a rhetorical retreat while the substance persists under quieter language** — and what would count as evidence either way?

None of these questions have settled answers, which is itself the most honest place to end: two real, important, and only loosely connected stories about the growing power of the modern corporation — one about speech and elections, one about purpose and ownership — that got tangled together in public debate because they arrived, coincidentally, in the same decade.
