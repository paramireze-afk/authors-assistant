# Usury: A Scriptural, Ethical and Economic View — Knowledge Base

## Purpose of This Document

This is not a summary but a structured extraction of the book's full intellectual apparatus: its arguments, its evidentiary base (biblical, patristic, historical, economic), its recurring rhetorical devices, and the network of people, institutions, and events it draws on. It is written to be searched, cross-referenced, and built upon — by a human or an AI — rather than read start to finish. Where a claim is Elliott's own (rather than an established or verifiable historical fact), this is flagged explicitly. Where this document adds an observation, comparison, or cross-reference not present in the book, it is marked **[Note:]**.

---

## About the Book and Its Author

Calvin Elliott's *Usury: A Scriptural, Ethical and Economic View* was published in 1902 by **The Anti-Usury League** of Millersburg, Ohio — a single-issue reform organization, part of the broader current of American Populist-era economic reform movements (contemporaneous with the Greenback and Free Silver movements, agrarian debt-relief agitation, and early Progressive-era trust-busting). Elliott states in his preface that this is, to his knowledge, **the first attempt ever made to fully present the anti-usury argument** as a systematic book-length treatise, rather than a pamphlet, sermon, or scattered commentary. He asks readers to proceed chapter by chapter in order, since the argument is cumulative — "the way it developed in my own mind."

**[Note:]** The book sits at the intersection of three older traditions that had, by 1902, become unfashionable in mainstream American Protestant and economic thought: (1) the medieval/patristic Christian prohibition of *all* interest-taking, (2) classical (Aristotelian) economic hostility to interest as "unnatural," and (3) Populist-era suspicion of concentrated financial power. Elliott is explicitly aware he is fighting a rear-guard action — by his own account (Ch. XXXVI, XXXVIII), organized Christian opposition to interest had been essentially defeated as public policy by the mid-17th century, and he is trying to revive it.

## Elliott's Central Thesis

**Usury is not excessive interest — it is *any* interest whatsoever charged on a loan.** Elliott spends Chapter I establishing this as a purely linguistic/definitional claim (the word "usury" meant "any premium for a loan" in the King James Bible's era, and "interest" is a 14th-century euphemism invented specifically to distinguish *legal* increase from *illegal* increase). But the rest of the book is an attempt to show that this old, blanket definition is also the *correct* one — morally, economically, and theologically — and that the modern narrowing of "usury" to mean only "illegally excessive interest" is a centuries-long act of linguistic and moral drift, not a discovery of new truth.

From this single definitional move, Elliott builds a three-pronged case:
1. **Scriptural** — the Bible, read consistently from Moses through the apostles, condemns all interest-taking without exception.
2. **Ethical** — interest-taking violates a basic principle of justice (compensation is owed only for service actually rendered), and in fact *inverts* the true relationship between lender and borrower.
3. **Economic/social** — interest-taking is not economically necessary, and its actual social effects are debt slavery, poverty, wealth centralization, corporate/political corruption, war, and the corruption of individual character.

**[Note: Comparison to Griffin's redefinition.]** This knowledge base also contains notes on G. Edward Griffin's *The Creature from Jekyll Island* (1994/1998), which also proposes a "redefinition" of usury — but in the *opposite direction* from Elliott's. Griffin narrows the definition, arguing usury should mean specifically "interest charged on a loan of **fiat money**" (i.e., money created out of nothing by a central bank), while conceding that interest on money someone actually earned or saved is a legitimate, honest transaction. Elliott broadens the definition to cover **all** interest on **any** loan, of any kind of property, without exception — he explicitly rejects the idea that interest becomes legitimate merely because the lender "really" owned the principal. See **[[Usury]]** for the standalone treatment of the term, and the "Connections" section below for a fuller comparison of the two books.

## Part I — The Scriptural Argument (Chapters I–XV)

### Chapter I: Definition
Establishes that "usury" originally meant *any* increase on a loan (money, food, or any property), citing period dictionaries (Theological Dictionary, Bible Encyclopedia, Schaff-Herzog, Webster 1890) and noting that "interest" only entered English in the 14th century as the legal-euphemism substitute, introduced "in the first law ever enacted by a Christian nation that permitted the taking of a premium for any loan." Elliott insists on using "usury" and "interest" interchangeably throughout the book to prevent the reader from mentally exempting "legal" interest from the argument.

### Chapter II: The Law by Moses
The foundational texts: **Exodus 22:25**, **Leviticus 25:35–37**, **Deuteronomy 23:19–20**. Elliott notes the Hebrew term for usury, *neshek*, derives from *nashak* ("to bite" — of a serpent), per Prof. George Bush's commentary: interest is compared to snakebite because its damage is imperceptible at first but eventually consumes the victim. Elliott devotes most of the chapter to **rebutting the claim that the prohibition applied only to loans made to the poor** (an argument used by later apologists to permit commercial/business interest). His eight-point rebuttal includes the argument that Hebrew law applied uniformly to rich and poor (citing Numbers 15:15–16) and that the loan-to-the-poor language *describes* rather than *limits* the prohibition.

### Chapter III: Usury and "The Stranger"
Addresses the single explicit scriptural loophole: Deuteronomy 23:20 permits lending on usury to a **"stranger."** Elliott's resolution — one of the book's most historically significant interpretive moves — is to argue there were **three distinct classes** of "stranger" in Hebrew law:
1. Proselytes to the Hebrew faith (to be treated as kin — no usury permitted).
2. Foreign "denizens"/sojourners trading for secular purposes (to be treated kindly — no usury permitted).
3. **The Canaanite nations under active divine condemnation for destruction** (Ex. 23:23–32) — Elliott argues *this* narrow class is the only group to whom usury was permitted, since they had "no rights that Hebrews were permitted to respect."

Elliott argues this narrow, condemned-nations reading was later broadened — by what he calls "avaricious" interpretation among some Hebrews, and subsequently seized on by Christian apologists — into a general license to charge Gentiles or "outsiders" interest. He states this misreading "set every Gentile Christian's hand against the Jews for fifteen hundred years," since medieval Christians who forbade usury among themselves associated interest-taking specifically with the Jewish community that was — due to guild exclusion — often confined to moneylending. **[Note:]** This chapter is historically significant primarily as **evidence of how the "stranger" clause was actually used/interpreted across history** — as a justification, in different eras, for Jewish moneylenders lending to Christians and for Christians later lending to non-coreligionists generally. It documents rather than originates the antisemitic dimension of medieval usury politics; treat this chapter as a primary source for the *history of interpretation* of Deut. 23:20, not as an endorsement of any particular reading.

### Chapter IV: David and Solomon
**Psalm 15** ("He that putteth not out his money to usury... shall never be moved") and **Proverbs 28:8** ("He that by usury and unjust gain increaseth his substance, he shall gather it for him that will pity the poor") — cited to show the prohibition held during Israel's most commercially prosperous era, refuting the claim that usury becomes necessary/acceptable once a society engages in extensive trade.

### Chapter V: Denunciation of Jeremiah and Ezekiel
**Jeremiah 15:10** (his personal protest of innocence — he neither lent nor borrowed on usury) and, most importantly, **Ezekiel 18** and **Ezekiel 22:6–12**, where usury is listed alongside incest, murder, idolatry, and adultery as evidence of a wicked city. Elliott's key interpretive claim here: usury is treated by Ezekiel as **"malum per se"** (wrong in itself, like murder or theft), not merely situationally wrong — a category distinction he returns to repeatedly.

### Chapter VI: Financial Reform by Nehemiah *(major case study)*
**Nehemiah 5** — during the post-exilic reconstruction of Jerusalem, wealthy nobles were charging usury on loans to fellow Hebrews struggling with famine and royal taxation, resulting in foreclosure, debt bondage, and the selling of children as chattel. Nehemiah publicly confronts the nobles, demands (1) full restitution of all pledges/foreclosed property, (2) return or crediting of all interest already collected, and (3) a solemn communal vow to end the practice. This is Elliott's paradigm case for **how usury concretely produces debt slavery and wealth concentration within a single generation**, and for what a moral/political remedy looks like (public confrontation + full restitution, not gradual reform).

### Chapter VII: Teachings of the Master
Argues Jesus did not abolish or relax the Mosaic usury law but "fulfilled" it (Matt. 5:17) by deepening it — extending the old command to help the poor (Deut. 15:7–8) into "lend, hoping for nothing again" (**Luke 6:35**), and dissolving the Hebrew/Gentile/stranger distinctions that had created the Deut. 23:20 loophole ("who is my neighbor?"). Jesus's teachings on wealth (camel through the needle's eye, the rich fool, "lay not up treasures on earth") are read as reinforcing rather than relaxing the anti-usury tradition.

### Chapter VIII: Parables of the Talents and the Pounds *(key interpretive move)*
Directly confronts the passage most commonly cited *for* usury: the master's rebuke in **Matthew 25:14–30** and **Luke 19:12–27**, where the unfaithful servant is told he should at least have "put my money to the exchangers" for interest. Elliott's reading: the master is not endorsing usury — he is turning the servant's own accusation ("I knew thee that thou art an hard man") back on him sarcastically: *if* the master really were as dishonest and grasping as the servant claims, the servant could at least have gotten him unjust gain through the money-changers "with no effort on his part." The reference to usury characterizes the *hypothetical* hard, dishonest master the servant accuses — consistent, Elliott argues, with Jesus elsewhere calling the money-changers thieves and driving them from the Temple. Elliott specifically criticizes a banking-industry text he found that quoted this parable as "a solemn direction of the divine Master to deposit money in the bank," comparing this to the equally absurd misuse of "let him that stole, steal" (Eph. 4:28, quoted out of context) to justify theft.

### Chapter IX: Practice of the Disciples
The early church practiced **gift-based mutual aid, not lending** (Acts 2:44–45 — "had all things common"), avoided debt entirely ("Owe no man anything," Rom. 13:8), and denounced covetousness (James 5:1–6, 1 Tim. 6:6–11 — "the love of money is the root of all evil"). Includes extended quotations from later commentators (Chalmers, the Paulist Fathers) on the moral necessity of debt-avoidance.

### Chapter X: Church History
A patristic and medieval survey: **Chrysostom** ("Nothing is baser in this world than usury"), **Basil**'s vivid literary portrait of a usurer's false friendliness curdling into predation, and unanimous condemnation from **Tertullian, Cyprian, Ambrose, Augustine, and Jerome**. Legal/institutional enforcement: **Charlemagne** banned usury for clergy and laity; the **Council of Westminster (1126)** degraded guilty clergy; the **Council of Vienne (1311)** declared that anyone who "obstinately" affirms usury is not a sin should be "punished as a heretic." **Luther** ("he ought rather to be hanging from the gallows") and later **John Ruskin** are quoted as later witnesses against the practice. Elliott notes there is **no record of any of these prohibitions ever being formally repealed** — the practice simply became tolerated without ever being officially re-approved by any council or creed.

### Chapter XI: Calvin's Letter on Usury *(the pivotal historical document)*
Reproduces **Calvin's letter, "De Usuris Responsum,"** in full — the single most consequential document in the entire history of Christian usury doctrine, since it is the text every subsequent Protestant apologist for interest has cited. Calvin's actual argument, per Elliott's close reading, is far more hedged than its reputation suggests: Calvin argues (a) Luke 6:35 targeted feast-invitation reciprocity abuse, not lending generally; (b) Mosaic law was "political," specific to Israel's circumstances, and not binding on later societies; (c) money used productively (e.g., to buy a farm) can fairly earn a return, just as land rental is fair; and (d) usury should be judged "by the rules of equity," not a specific verse. But Calvin **also** writes: *"It could be wished that all usury and the name itself were first banished from the earth"* — and Elliott argues this, not the hedged permission, is where Calvin is "nearest himself." Elliott's verdict: this is "wanting... timid," "the most favorable [defense] that can be presented from any ecclesiastic," and its very weakness is proof of how thin the pro-usury case actually is.

### Chapters XII–XIV: Permanency, Changed Conditions, American Revision
Argues the prohibition was never ceremonial (so it wasn't abolished with the Old Covenant's ritual law) and was treated as permanently binding by the apostolic fathers. Rebuts the "changed conditions" argument (commerce, technology, credit systems have expanded) by pointing out that human nature, family structure, the moral law, natural law, and the basic function of money are all unchanged, and that the argument "we violate this law so universally that it must not be binding" would equally excuse ignoring the Sabbath or the seventh commandment. Chapter XIV documents that the **American Standard Revised Bible** (the most recent, most scholarly translation as of Elliott's writing) substitutes "interest" for "usury" in nearly every relevant passage — used as evidence that modern scholarship confirms the traditional passages target interest itself, not merely excessive rates.

### Chapter XV: Duty Learned from Two Sources
A transitional chapter arguing Scripture and "the book of nature" (ethics, economics, social relations) are two harmonious sources of the same truth — setting up Part II's shift from biblical to purely rational/economic argument, explicitly so that readers "who have no taste" for scriptural argument can still be persuaded on secular grounds.

## Part II — The Ethical/Philosophical Argument (Chapters XVI–XXI)

### Chapter XVI: Rights of Man Over Things
Foundational premise: **rights belong to persons, never to things.** Man has dominion over all material creation (citing Psalm 8, Isaiah 10:15's ridicule of tool-worship). The usurer and the interest-paying borrower **both** implicitly commit a category error by treating property as though it had a moral claim to "service" — reversing the proper order in which man commands things, not the other way around.

### Chapter XVII: Equal Rights of Men *(the "deposit, not gift" argument)*
Introduces Elliott's key economic reframing: **a loan is a "deposit," not a transfer of ownership.** The borrower never becomes even partial owner of borrowed property (illustrated by the fact that bank "deposits" are universally understood this way, regardless of whether they draw interest). Since ownership never transfers, the lender loses nothing tangible by lending — he merely places his property temporarily in someone else's custody. Includes a memorable illustrative parable: two neighboring "usurers," A (who owns an ax but not a hoe) and B (who owns a hoe but not an ax), each lend their tool to a laborer in exchange for uncompensated work, while retaining full ownership throughout — demonstrating how "lending" a tool while demanding a service in return is functionally identical to enslaving the laborer's time without paying for it.

### Chapter XVIII: A False Basal Principle
Attacks the standard justification for variable interest rates: that the rate should reflect **the benefit/profit the loan generates for the borrower.** Elliott calls this principle absurd via a chain of analogies: a cab fare doesn't rise because the passenger's meeting is important; a postage stamp doesn't cost more because the letter changes someone's life; a farmer who stores a neighbor's lumber doesn't get to charge extra just because he cleverly used it to shelter his own livestock in the meantime. If this were a legitimate principle, Elliott notes, **all usury laws capping interest rates would be illogical** — a point he says free-market apologists like Jeremy Bentham implicitly concede by using this very principle to argue against all interest-rate regulation.

### Chapter XIX: The True Ethical Principle *(the book's central intellectual inversion)*
Elliott's most distinctive and creative argument. The proper ethical principle, he argues, is that **compensation is owed for services actually rendered** — and by that standard, it is the **borrower**, not the lender, who renders the service, because the borrower is the one who must safeguard, preserve, insure against loss, and eventually restore the lender's property. The lender, meanwhile, "does not surrender a particle of his wealth" and "renders no personal service." Elliott systematically runs this logic through custodial-service analogies that normally *charge the owner*, not the custodian — a coach-keeper who exercises a client's horses, an express company that transports valuables, and especially an **insurance company**, which is paid *by the owner* to protect property. His climactic line: "Usury presumes to reverse this ethical order and requires that the insurance company shall pay the owner of the property for the privilege of protecting it." Under this logic, if anyone should be compensated in a lending transaction, it is the borrower, for the service of custodianship — not the lender for merely temporarily giving up something he retains full ownership of.

### Chapter XX: Wealth Is Barren
The classical (Aristotelian) argument, given a literary anchor: **Shakespeare's Shylock**, in *The Merchant of Venice*, defending interest by citing Jacob's sheep-breeding trick, and Antonio's rebuttal ("is your gold and silver ewes and rams?"). Elliott argues only *intelligent living energy* is genuinely productive; money, tools, and machines are all inert and cannot "breed." He extends this into a striking four-party analysis of "productive capital": laborers who build a factory, laborers who operate it, a genuinely active manager/investor (legitimate, since he contributes real oversight), and — critically — **a fourth-party usurer/creditor who stands behind all three, contributes nothing, and takes no risk, yet claims a perpetual, compounding cut**. This fourth party's claim to "productive capital" is, in Elliott's phrase, "the figment... introduced to cover the evident fraud."

### Chapter XXI: Wealth Decays
All man-made wealth requires continuous labor to prevent decay (food spoils, tools rust, buildings crumble, even gold must be guarded against theft and taxation). Since preserving wealth is itself a service, and usury demands the borrower both preserve the principal *and* generate a surplus, usury asks the borrower to perform an unpaid service (preservation) *and* pay extra for the privilege. Revisits the parable of the rich fool (Luke 12), imagining how he could have exploited neighbors "on interest" to be idle "not only he, for many years, but his posterity forever."

## Part III — The Social and Economic Argument (Chapters XXII–XXXII)

### Chapter XXII: The Debt Habit
A sociological chapter on the psychology of consumer debt in turn-of-the-century America — the "installment plan" for furniture, books, and musical instruments; municipal and church bond debt statistics (only 6 of the surveyed American cities in Elliott's data had zero municipal debt). Elliott's provocative claim: **"Debts make no man the richer... paying debts makes no man poorer"** — since debt is merely a transfer, not a creation or destruction of wealth, abolishing usury system-wide would leave the world's total wealth unchanged while destroying "the device by which the usurers appropriate to themselves the productions of others."

### Chapter XXIII–XXIV: The Borrower Is Servant to the Lender / Usury Enslaves the Borrower
Builds on Proverbs 22:7 ("the borrower is servant to the lender") to argue that even an interest-*free* loan creates a psychological subservience (illustrated by a hunter's unpaid "lackey" who carries game so the hunter can keep hunting). Interest *compounds* this into genuine enslavement. **The book's most provocative comparison**: Elliott draws a sustained, explicit parallel between usury-debt and **chattel slavery** — quoting *Timon of Athens* ("No usurer, but has a fool for a slave") — and argues debt bondage is in some respects **more exploitative** than chattel slavery had been, because the usurer bears none of a slaveowner's costs (purchase price, feeding, medical care, personal investment in the debtor's wellbeing) or personal attachment, while extracting comparable or greater labor value. **[Note:]** Written in 1902, less than 40 years after the abolition of American chattel slavery, this comparison would have carried strong contemporary resonance; it is presented here as Elliott's own rhetorical argument, not this document's endorsement of the comparison's proportionality.

### Chapters XXV–XXVIII: Usury Oppresses the Poor *(four-part series)*
The book's most sustained economic argument, rebutting the claim that the biblical prohibition only concerns direct loans to poor borrowers. Key moves:
- **The "tribute" argument**: interest is embedded in the price of everything a poor consumer buys — the mortgaged farm that grew the wheat, the bonded railroad that shipped it, the mortgaged mill that ground it, the loan-financed bakery that baked it — so that "by usury a tribute is levied on his bread from the wheat in the field until it reaches his tables," regardless of whether the poor person personally borrowed anything.
- **The Two Widows parable** (Ch. XXVII): a rich widow lives comfortably off interest paid by mortgages, bonds, and loans held by a "Security Co.," while a poor widow — working for wages depressed by interest obligations on her employer's borrowed capital, riding streetcars whose fares partly service bond interest, buying bread whose price embeds interest at every stage of production — unknowingly *funds* the rich widow's income. "The rich widow has fed her children with the bread which the poor widow earned."
- **Wage-share statistics**: citing 1890 and 1900 U.S. Census data, Elliott calculates labor received roughly 53% and 50% respectively of the value it created, with the wage-earner's real annual income actually *falling* by about $7 over that decade despite a boom period for capital.
- **The corporation critique**: incorporated wealth, Elliott argues, has been stripped of personal moral responsibility while retaining legal "personhood" — allowing owners in distant cities to demand dividends without ever confronting the human costs (Sunday labor, plant closures, no worker protections) of extracting them. "No instrument of oppression ever surpassed in severity the usury of incorporated wealth and retained the pretense of respectability."
- Argues usury also disproportionately loads **taxation** onto the poor (property-tax burden is effectively passed through to renters/consumers, while wealth "protected" by interest income remains undiminished by taxes).

### Chapter XXIX: Usury Centralizes Wealth *(includes the book's most famous illustrative math)*
Argues centralization of wealth under any positive interest rate is mathematically inevitable, regardless of the rate (only the *speed* varies). Illustrative calculations:
- **Manhattan Island**: Peter Minuit's legendary 1626 purchase of Manhattan for $24 — Elliott calculates that if invested at 7% compound interest, this sum would by 1902 exceed the entire value of New York City and County.
- **The "one cent since AD 1" thought experiment**: one cent compounded at 6% annually from year 1 to 1895 would total a number of dollars vastly exceeding the mass-in-gold of the entire visible universe — a hyperbolic illustration of why compound interest cannot continue indefinitely without producing absurd results, meaning some correction (default, war, crisis, or redistribution) is mathematically inevitable.
- **The Bank of England, calculated forward**: Elliott directly narrates the **1694 Bank of England charter** — a "perpetual loan" of £1,200,000 to the crown at 8% interest, never to be repaid — and calculates that, compounded continuously to 1902, this single loan's theoretical value would exceed **fifty times the entire wealth of the world**. **[Note — major cross-reference:]** This is the *same* founding episode (1694, £1,200,000, 8%, "perpetual" loan) that G. Edward Griffin narrates in *The Creature from Jekyll Island* (1998) as the origin of modern central banking — see **[[The Bank of England]]**. Elliott's 1902 moral/mathematical treatment and Griffin's 1998 institutional/conspiratorial treatment of the identical historical event, arrived at independently and nearly a century apart, is one of the most striking connections in this knowledge base.
- Argues recurring American financial panics (**1809, 1818, 1837, 1873, 1893**) are not adequately explained by the specific economic conditions historians cite for each one individually, but share a single underlying cause: the "constant centralizing draft of usury," compared to a chronic disease that makes a patient vulnerable to any passing change in conditions.

### Chapter XXX: Mammon Dominates the Nations *(major historical/geopolitical chapter)*
Elliott's most expansive historical survey, arguing that concentrated interest-bearing debt has repeatedly captured and hollowed out republican government:
- **The Bank of Venice** (est. 1171) bound itself to the Venetian state via a perpetual 4% loan; over centuries this coincided with Venice's transformation from a "pure democracy" into an oligarchy controlled by the "Golden Book" families, then the "Council of Ten," then a secret "tribunal of three" — bank and state, Elliott notes, "when one fell both perished," in 1797.
- **The Bank of England** (1694) — same founding narrative as Ch. XXIX, drawing on **Macaulay's history**, with added detail on the Bank's 1797 self-authorized suspension of specie payment and Sir **Robert Peel's 1844 Act**, which the Bank defied by "open disobedience." Elliott's verdict: the Bank has "more influence than Crown and Parliament combined," dictating British foreign policy toward India, China (opium), and the American slavery question — "a great uncrowned king."
- **American banking history**: the **First Bank of the United States (1791–1811)**, the **Second Bank of the United States (1816–1836)**, and the "thirty years" of anti-bank policy following Andrew Jackson's victory over Nicholas Biddle. Quotes **John C. Calhoun**, **Thomas Hart Benton**, **Thomas Jefferson** ("banking establishments are more dangerous than standing armies"), and **Andrew Jackson** against national banking. **[Note — major cross-reference:]** This is essentially the identical historical narrative and even overlapping quotations (Jefferson's "perpetual debts" line, for instance) found in Griffin's *Creature from Jekyll Island* account of the same period — see **[[The Federal Reserve System]]**.
- The **Civil War national banking fight**: usurers' wartime demands (perpetual loan, tax exemption, coin-not-legal-tender interest payment, private note issuance), passed by a narrow 23–21 Senate vote as a wartime emergency measure. Quotes Secretary McCullough and the dramatic floor speech of **Thaddeus Stevens** lamenting the concession.
- **Geopolitical section**: a sweeping indictment of how bondholder interests, not humanitarian or ideological concern, determine great-power intervention — contrasting the great powers' *inaction* on the Armenian massacres (the Ottoman Sultan paid bond interest) with their *intervention* to stop violence against Crete (Cretan bonds were endangered); British hostility toward the Boer republics; the "vivisection" of China via foreign loans. Personifies this force as **Mammon** (citing Milton's *Paradise Lost* portrayal of Mammon as a fallen angel), concluding: "The scepter wherein lies Mammon's power to sway the nations is usury."

### Chapter XXXI: Effect on Character
A psychological/moral catalogue of usury's corrosive effects on both lender and borrower: (1) discourages industry and rewards idleness; (2) undermines open honesty (encourages secrecy — Elliott notes contemporary moneylender advertisements universally promised "privacy"/"no publicity") and normalizes speculation; (3) destroys self-reliant, egalitarian social spirit, replacing it with cringing dependency; (4) destroys "fraternal sympathy" between producing and non-producing classes (including within churches — "How can the laboring man be attracted to the churches?"); (5) promotes **covetousness as idolatry**, developed through an extended comparison to sun-worship, Nile-worship, and cattle-worship — riches, Elliott argues, are actually a *more* seductive idol than any of these ancient objects of worship, since (via usury) they supply human needs perpetually "without effort on the part of the recipient" and "know no decay"; (6) destroys spirituality by fixing attention on the material rather than the character/spirit of a person.

### Chapter XXXII: Ax at the Root of the Tree
Elliott's proposed universal remedy chapter — arguing a single reform (abolishing usury) would resolve five separate contemporary Progressive-Era social problems, each of which reformers were (in his view) unsuccessfully treating as a distinct, isolated issue:
1. **Wealth polarization** / the shrinking middle class (usury as the mechanism preventing the natural, self-correcting decay/consumption cycle from re-equalizing fortunes).
2. **Industrial trusts and combines** (formed specifically to protect interest/dividend income; "do away with usury and they will fall apart like balls of sand").
3. **Unfair distribution of the gains from new machinery/technology** (the false premise that "the machine is productive," when in fact the machine's owner is simply capturing the surplus productivity of the *worker* who now operates it).
4. **Labor strikes and employer-employee conflict** (no just standard exists for wage disputes until it's recognized capital is "dead" and non-productive, and all earnings beyond mere preservation belong to labor).
5. **War** (proposes financing wars entirely through immediate confiscatory taxation on the wealthy rather than interest-bearing bonds, so that "the rich grow poorer as the war goes on... let there be a facing of utter poverty, as the patriot faces death on the field" — removing the profit motive Elliott believes drives Mammon to encourage conflict).

## Part IV — Answering the Objections ("Per Contra," Chapters XXXIII–XXXV)

### Chapter XXXIII: Per Contra; Christian Apologists
Engages two historical Protestant catechisms directly: the **Wilson Catechism (Dundee, 1737)** and **Fisher's Catechism (1753)**, both of which condemn "unlawful"/excessive usury while defending "moderate" interest as consistent with Scripture. Elliott's rebuttal notes that neither catechism can actually produce a scriptural proof-text *for* permitted interest (Fisher's citation of 1 Cor. 8:13 is, Elliott argues, an absurd non-sequitur). Also cites **Charles Hodge's** *Systematic Theology*, which — while barely discussing usury by name — condemns the principle that "a thing is worth what it is worth to the man who demands it" (Elliott's Chapter XVIII target) and warns that many respectable churchgoers "will be astonished at the last day to find the word 'Thieves' written after their names." Rebuts three standard objections: that abolishing usury is "revolutionary" (Elliott: so was Christianity itself); that modern enterprise is impossible without it (counterexample: the postal system, run as "cash business"); and that abolition would make borrowing harder (Elliform: it *should* be hard to borrow but easy to repay — usury inverts this).

### Chapter XXXIV: Per Contra; Land Rentals
Addresses Calvin's own argument (from Ch. XI) that land rental is an accepted analog justifying money interest. Elliott inverts this: rather than land rental proving interest is legitimate, he argues land rental is **itself frequently usurious**, especially under absentee landlordism — illustrated by two vivid anecdotes: (1) a dispute in which **Omaha Indians** were compelled to pay an absentee landowner for grass they had cut on his unimproved land, which they argued (per Elliott, accurately by natural-law reasoning) belonged to no one since the owner had invested no labor in it; (2) frontier settlers deliberately using local tax/bond powers to force absentee land speculators to help pay for schools and bridges, as informal compensation for the "unearned increment" those speculators stood to gain from settlers' own development efforts. **[Note:]** This "unearned increment" language directly anticipates the **Georgist / land-value-tax** movement that would be popularized shortly after by Henry George's followers — worth a cross-reference. Elliott ultimately argues American land-distribution law (freehold, no entail) was the chief cause of the nation's rapid, egalitarian settlement-era prosperity, and warns against rising farm tenancy as a re-emergence of "usury of land."

### Chapter XXXV: Per Contra; Political Economist
Engages classical economic justifications for interest directly: **risk, time, and abstinence** (the "abstinence theory" of interest, associated with economists like Nassau Senior). Elliott rebuts risk (interest-as-insurance is "the reverse of insurance" — real insurance is paid *by* the property owner *to* the guarantor, not the reverse); time ("time is the great destroyer," not a wealth-creator — quoting the aphorism about eggs, incubation, and chickens); and abstinence, via a clever re-reading of **Jacob and Esau's birthright** (Genesis 25) — Jacob's "abstinence" from eating his pottage, traded for Esau's birthright, is precisely the kind of self-interested, non-benevolent abstinence Elliott says usury apologists are really describing, and the text explicitly treats Jacob's act as *theft*, not a legitimate transaction (he later fled his home for twenty years in guilt/fear of Esau's revenge). Quotes Ruskin's "you cannot have your cake and eat it" formulation.

## Part V — Historical Survey (Chapters XXXVI–XXXVII)

### Chapter XXXVI: Usury in History
A condensed comparative civilizational survey:
- **Greece**: no formal legal prohibition, but strong philosophical opposition. **Solon**'s debt-relief reforms (cancelling mortgages marked by boundary stones, freeing enslaved debtors) are treated as a Nehemiah-parallel. **Plato** opposed it; **Aristotle**'s famous formulation is quoted at length: interest is "the worst and most unnatural" mode of accumulation, since it "generates money from money," producing "offspring" (*tokos*) of the "same nature as that from which it proceeds" — i.e., money literally breeding money, which Aristotle considered a perversion of money's proper function as a medium of exchange.
- **Rome**: the Twelve Tables era permitted creditors of insolvent debtors to physically dismember them or sell their children into slavery; usury penalties were harsher than theft penalties (fourfold restitution vs. double for theft) per **Cato**, whose famous reply when asked his opinion of usury was: **"What do you think of murder?"** Legal rate ceilings fluctuated wildly across Roman history (12% early, later 1%, 3% under Sulla, 4% under Antony/Cleopatra, up to 60% in periods of chaos per Horace's Satires, settling at 6% under Justinian).
- **England**: a chronological legal survey — **King Alfred (~900 AD)** confiscated usurers' estates and denied consecrated burial; **Edward the Confessor (~1050)** declared usurers outlaws; **Henry II** disinherited usurers' children; **Richard I** refused usurers protection from mob violence; **King John** reportedly extracted a fine from a Bristol Jewish moneylender by having a tooth pulled daily until he paid (endured seven extractions before relenting); the **three Edwards** made usury an indictable, eventually capital, offense; Attorney General **Noy** under James I said "usurers are well ranked with murderers"; **Henry VIII** capped legal interest at 10%; **Edward VI** revived total prohibition; **Mary I** enforced it severely; **Elizabeth I** restored the 10% cap. This legal framework held until **1854**, when England repealed all remaining usury-rate laws. Elliott identifies **1694 — the Bank of England charter** — as "the turning point in the popular mind," the moment the Crown itself, as head of both church and state, became complicit in permanent usury, after which "ecclesiastics began to shift their ground."

### Chapter XXXVII: Francis Bacon
Reproduces **Bacon's essay "Of Usury"** (from his *Essays*) in full, then critiques it. Elliott's key observation: Bacon's is a **purely pragmatic/economic** case (he "eliminates risk from usury," recognizing that in "the game of certainties against uncertainties" the lender always wins) rather than a moral defense — Bacon frames usury as *concessum propter duritiem cordis* ("allowed by reason of the hardness of men's hearts"), i.e., a necessary evil, not a good. Bacon lists seven "discommodities" of usury (it discourages merchant trade, impoverishes merchants, reduces state customs revenue, centralizes wealth "into a few hands," lowers land prices, dulls innovation, and eventually causes "public poverty") against three "commodities" (it enables young merchants to trade on borrowed capital; it prevents worse outcomes like forced fire-sales of property; borrowing without profit to the lender is "impossible to conceive" as sustainable practice). Bacon's proposed compromise — a dual-rate system, a low general rate (5%) freely available to all, plus a higher licensed rate for merchant lending in designated commercial cities — is presented as the era's most sophisticated attempt at regulation-not-abolition, which Elliott treats as proof the practice was still widely recognized as harmful even by its most articulate defenders.

## Part VI — Conclusion (Chapters XXXVIII–XXXIX)

### Chapter XXXVIII: Why This Truth Was Neglected
Elliott's own sociology-of-error chapter, offering eleven reasons the anti-usury tradition was abandoned: human nature's tendency toward "evil... as weeds grow"; theological attention absorbed by Reformation-era doctrinal disputes (faith/grace) rather than practical ethics; other, more visible ecclesiastical corruptions crowding out attention; the private/secret nature of covetousness as a sin (unlike public vices); **educational conditioning** — Elliott specifically blames arithmetic textbooks for teaching percentage problems exclusively from the creditor's perspective, never the debtor's; America's vast unclaimed natural-resource wealth temporarily masking the costs of debt; the unprecedented modern *scale* of accumulated capital and corporate combination; the false appearance of early prosperity that debt-financed improvement creates; intemperate rhetoric by earlier opponents (bitter invective rather than reasoned argument) that made the position easy to dismiss; and the self-reinforcing way legalized usury dulls the conscience over time ("nothing in history more shocked the conscience of Christendom than the compact of William and Mary with usurers in 1694... it has taken two hundred years... to first dull the Christian conscience and then secure its approval"). Includes two memorable illustrative anecdotes: **Peter Cooper**'s lesson to a merchant borrowing at 3%/month (offering to "discount" a $10,000 note for three years by simply handing over $10,800 upfront — demonstrating how abstractly-stated interest rates conceal their true compounding force), and a wise uncle's tongue-in-cheek letter to his nephew personifying interest as "about the most industrious animal of my acquaintance... working nights and Sundays as well."

### Chapter XXXIX: Crushed Truth Will Rise Again
The closing, hopeful chapter, arguing by historical analogy that entrenched, seemingly permanent social evils have been successfully overturned before: the **Salem witch trials** (defended even by Harvard's president's grandson, Cotton Mather; two hundred arrests and twenty executions in 1692 alone, now unthinkable); **dueling** among American political leaders (Hamilton/Burr, 1804; still practiced by congressmen into the 1840s, now considered moral cowardice rather than honor); **lotteries and gambling**, once state-sanctioned and church-fundraising-adjacent, later banned nationwide; and above all, **chattel slavery**, once defended by scripture-citation, claims of paternalistic benevolence, and deep-seated prejudice, yet fully abolished within living memory of Elliott's readers. Elliott offers eight reasons for optimism specific to usury reform, the most concrete of which is a proposed **Postal Savings Bank system** — the government accepting citizens' surplus savings on deposit (as secure as government bonds), investing accumulated funds in public utilities for general benefit, thereby capturing "the blessings of Socialism" (collective benefit from capital) while preserving individual ownership and access to one's own savings — eliminating the need for private usurious lending intermediaries entirely. **[Note:]** The actual U.S. **Postal Savings System** was established by Congress in **1910–1911**, within a decade of this book's publication — Elliott's proposal reflects a real contemporaneous policy current, not an isolated fantasy. The book closes with Elliott casting his own effort as a **David-and-Goliath** confrontation: "Mammon, whose head is usury, is the giant Philistine who now stalks forth to defy 'the armies of the living God'... with a grain of David's faith, he flings this stone."

## Recurring Patterns Across the Book

Reading across all 39 chapters, several structural patterns recur regardless of which biblical, ethical, or economic register Elliott is working in:

1. **The "malum per se" framing.** Elliott repeatedly insists usury belongs to a category of acts wrong *in themselves* (like theft or murder), not merely wrong when done to excess — this framing appears in the Ezekiel chapter, the "false basal principle" chapter, and the historical survey (Cato's "usury = murder" quip).
2. **Ownership never transfers.** The "loan is a deposit, not a gift" principle (Ch. XVII) underlies the slavery comparison (Ch. XXIV), the true-ethical-principle inversion (Ch. XIX), and the wealth-decay argument (Ch. XXI) — it's arguably the single load-bearing premise of the entire economic argument.
3. **Scale/degree does not change the moral category.** Repeated refrain: "petit larceny and grand larceny differ only in the amount stolen" — used against both low-vs-high interest rate distinctions and rich-vs-poor borrower distinctions.
4. **Financial power converts republics into oligarchies.** The Bank of Venice and Bank of England narratives (Ch. XXX) are explicitly presented as parallel case studies in the same underlying process, later implicitly extended as a warning to the United States.
5. **The "reversed order" motif.** Recurs literally dozens of times: things ruling persons instead of persons ruling things (Ch. XVI), insurers being paid by the insured instead of paying them (Ch. XIX), the tool credited with the worker's productivity (Ch. XX, XXXII), the borrower serving the lender instead of vice versa (Ch. XXIII–XXIV).
6. **Compound growth as inherently unsustainable / eventually absurd.** The Manhattan Island and "one cent since AD 1" calculations (Ch. XXIX) are the clearest statement, but the same logic underlies the recurring-panics theory and the critique of "perpetual" national debts (Ch. XXX).
7. **Debt precedes and enables war**, not the reverse — an argument made independently in the "Ax at the Root" chapter (Ch. XXXII) and the geopolitical section of "Mammon Dominates the Nations" (Ch. XXX).
8. **Precedent from other overturned "permanent" evils.** Slavery, dueling, witch-hunting, and gambling are invoked cumulatively across Chapters IX (indirectly), XXXVIII, and especially XXXIX as evidence that entrenched, seemingly natural social practices can and do get abolished.

## Key Terminology / Glossary (Elliott's Usage)

| Term | Elliott's meaning |
|---|---|
| **Usury** | Any premium or increase charged for the loan of money or any other property — deliberately used interchangeably with "interest" throughout the book |
| **Interest** | A 14th-century legal euphemism for usury, coined specifically to distinguish state-sanctioned from state-forbidden increase |
| **Neshek** | Hebrew root for usury, from *nashak* ("to bite"), evoking the delayed, escalating harm of a snakebite |
| **Malum per se** | Latin for "wrong in itself" — Elliott's category for usury, as opposed to "malum prohibitum" (wrong only because prohibited) |
| **The false basal principle** | Elliott's term for the standard justification of variable interest rates: that rates should reflect the borrower's potential profit from the loan |
| **The true ethical principle** | Elliott's counter-principle: compensation is owed only for services actually rendered — which, in a loan, is rendered by the custodian/borrower, not the lender |
| **Productive capital** | A term Elliott calls a "figment" — he denies capital/money/tools are ever literally "productive"; only living intelligent labor produces |
| **Mammon** | Personification (via Milton) of concentrated financial/material power as a rival, corrupting god |
| **Deposit (vs. gift)** | Elliott's technical description of every loan — ownership never transfers to the borrower, regardless of whether interest is charged |
| **The debt habit** | The psychological/cultural normalization of borrowing as respectable, contrasted with the older "independent, self-reliant" ideal of debt-avoidance |
| **Usury of land** | Elliott's extension of the concept to exploitative land rental/absentee landlordism, distinct from legitimate rent for landlord-added improvements |

## Author's Claims vs. This Document's Observations — A Methodology Note

Throughout this document, claims presented as Elliott's own interpretive or historical arguments (e.g., the three-classes-of-"stranger" reading, the reinterpretation of the Talents/Pounds parables, the "true ethical principle" inversion, the attribution of recurring panics to usury) are **his framework, not established consensus historical or theological positions** — they represent one specific, coherent, and now largely marginal 1902 argument, not the mainstream view of biblical scholarship, economics, or history either in 1902 or today. Sections marked **[Note:]** are this document's own additions — cross-references, comparisons to other works in this knowledge base, or brief factual context (e.g., the actual 1910–1911 Postal Savings System) not present in Elliott's text.
