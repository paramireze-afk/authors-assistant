# Quotas, Gods, and Dung Smoke: Cheese in Bronze Age Mesopotamia

It is midsummer in southern Iraq, a little over four thousand years ago. The heat is brutal, well past a hundred degrees by midday. The air smells of river mud, animal dung, and the smoke of fires fed with dried manure, because in a land with almost no trees, dung is fuel. Inside a low hut woven from marsh reeds, a herdsman is working milk. His hands are cracked, flies crawl over everything, and his stomach is tight with worry, because somewhere in the city a scribe is waiting with a lump of wet clay. That scribe will record exactly how much butter and cheese this man delivers this year. If the amount falls short, the herdsman will owe the difference.

Welcome to Bronze Age Mesopotamia, where cheese stopped being simply something families made to get through the lean months. Here it became a product of the state, counted, taxed, and handed to the gods.

## From Villages to Cities

In our last episode, we saw how the earliest herders of the Fertile Crescent probably made simple, sour, fresh cheese from milk that curdled in the heat. Over the following thousands of years, farming villages along the Tigris and Euphrates rivers grew larger. Then, roughly between four thousand and three thousand BC, something new emerged in the far south, in the land the Sumerians called home: the city.

Places like Uruk swelled into some of the largest settlements the world had ever seen, home to tens of thousands of people. Growing that many people's food in a hot, dry land required large-scale irrigation, with canals cut from the rivers to water fields of barley. And organizing that kind of labor required something else that had never existed before: a bureaucracy.

At the center of each city stood the temple. A Mesopotamian temple was not just a place of worship. It was a giant household that belonged, in theory, to the city's god. It owned fields, orchards, workshops, and enormous herds of sheep, goats, and cattle. It employed farmers, weavers, brewers, and herdsmen, and it fed them with rations. In many ways the temple was the economy.

## Why Writing May Owe Something to Dairy

Here is where the story gets surprising. When you are managing thousands of animals and thousands of workers, memory is not enough. You need records. And in the city of Uruk, around five thousand years ago, administrators began pressing marks into clay tablets to keep track of goods. This was the beginning of writing.

Those earliest tablets were not poetry or law. They were accounts. Barley, beer, textiles, livestock, and yes, dairy products. Scholars studying the oldest tablets from Uruk have identified signs for different dairy products and for the herds that produced them. The very first writing systems on Earth were partly invented to keep track of things like butter and cheese. It is one of the great reminders that civilization was built on bookkeeping.

I'll be honest that reading these earliest tablets is extremely difficult. The script was still taking shape, and experts don't always agree on exactly what each sign means or which product is which. But the broad picture, that dairy was carefully tracked from the start, is well established.

## A Picture Carved in Stone

We don't just have numbers. We have pictures. One of the most famous is a decorated panel found at a temple site called Tell al-Ubaid, near the ancient city of Ur, dating to around twenty-five hundred BC. Archaeologists call it the milking frieze. Made of white shell and limestone figures set against dark stone, it shows a dairy operation from start to finish.

On one side, a man sits behind a cow, milking her from the rear, while calves stand nearby, muzzled or tied so they can't drink the milk meant for the temple. Further along, workers pour milk into tall jars, and others appear to be churning or straining it. Next to them sits a reed cattle shed, the same kind of hut those herdsmen worked in. It is like a comic strip of ancient dairy production, and it was mounted on a temple wall, which tells you how closely dairy was tied to the gods.

That frieze is now held in the Iraq Museum in Baghdad. Other carvings from the period show calves stepping out of reed byres and jars of milk lined up beside them. The Sumerians clearly thought dairy was worth depicting in their most sacred buildings.

## Ghee and Hard Cheese: Beating the Heat

So what were they actually making? The Sumerian texts mention two main dairy products again and again. One was a kind of clarified butter, very much like the ghee still used across South Asia today. The other was cheese.

There's a solid scientific reason for that pairing, and it's one Kindstedt emphasizes. In a climate this hot, fresh milk spoils within hours, and soft, moist cheese doesn't last much longer. To store dairy for months, you need to drive out the water. Clarified butter is almost pure fat, with nearly all the water and milk solids removed, so it keeps for a remarkably long time even in the heat. Cheese, to survive, would most likely have been made from soured milk, drained hard, salted, and dried until it was tough and dense.

The texts also distinguish different kinds of cheese, with words that translators render along the lines of fresh, white, or flavored cheese. Some popular sources claim the Sumerians had around twenty varieties. I would treat that number with caution. The words are hard to translate, and we often can't tell whether two terms describe truly different cheeses or just different grades, sizes, or uses of the same product.

Whether these cheeses were made with rennet, the enzyme from young animals' stomachs, is also uncertain. Given the climate and the technology, many scholars, including Kindstedt as best I understand his argument, think sour, acid-curdled cheese is the more likely picture. But the tablets record quantities, not recipes, so nobody can say for sure.

## The Herdsman's Burden

Now let's go back to that worried herdsman, because this is where the story turns gritty.

Around twenty-one hundred BC, a powerful dynasty based in the city of Ur united southern Mesopotamia. Historians call it the Third Dynasty of Ur, or Ur Three. It was one of the most obsessively bureaucratic states in the ancient world, and it left behind tens of thousands of clay tablets, including detailed dairy records.

Scholars who have studied these records, particularly the Assyriologist Robert Englund, found that state herdsmen operated under strict production quotas. A herdsman was entrusted with a herd of cows belonging to the state or temple. Based on the number of cows, the administration calculated how much clarified butter and cheese he owed each year, along with a set number of calves. The calculations were standardized, almost like a factory target.

And if the herdsman fell short, the shortfall didn't disappear. It was recorded as a debt that he was responsible for. Some herdsmen apparently carried these debts from year to year. Imagine your livelihood depending on cows that might fall sick, give less milk in a drought, or die in a hot summer, while a scribe kept a running tally of what you owed the gods.

Most of that butter and cheese flowed into temple storerooms and state warehouses. Some was offered to the gods and then redistributed. Some went out as rations to workers and officials. It was food, but it was also wealth that moved through the system, carefully counted at every step.

## Cheese and the Gods

Dairy had a special place in Sumerian religion. Some of the most important deities were closely linked to herds and milk. The goddess Inanna, one of the great powers of the Sumerian world, took as her consort a shepherd god named Dumuzi.

In one Sumerian poem, Inanna must choose between two suitors, a farmer and a shepherd. The shepherd Dumuzi argues his case by boasting about everything his flocks can produce, including milk, cream, and cheese, setting them against the farmer's grain. It's essentially a contest between bread and cheese, with a goddess as the prize.

There's a darker side, too. In other poems about Dumuzi's death, his sheepfold is destroyed. The images of that destruction are domestic and heartbreaking: the churn knocked onto its side, no milk poured, the fold empty and silent. For the Sumerians, a broken churn was a symbol of death and ruin. When the dairy stopped, the world had ended.

## A Land Turning to Salt

There is one more gritty chapter to this story, and it involves salt, though not the kind you sprinkle on cheese.

When you irrigate fields in a hot, dry place, the water evaporates and leaves behind dissolved salts. Over centuries, those salts build up in the soil. In the nineteen-fifties, the scholars Thorkild Jacobsen and Robert Adams argued that this slow poisoning of the land contributed to the decline of southern Mesopotamia. They pointed to records suggesting that farmers shifted away from wheat toward barley, which tolerates salt better, and that crop yields fell over time.

Kindstedt draws on this idea when considering what happened to dairying in the region, because herds depend on the land too. However, I want to be clear that the salinization theory has been seriously challenged. Some historians argue that the evidence was overstated and that politics, war, and shifting trade played bigger roles. It's a genuine, ongoing debate, and I'm not certain exactly how much weight Kindstedt places on it.

What we do know is that the Ur Three state collapsed around two thousand BC, when invaders sacked the city of Ur. The Sumerians wrote laments for their ruined cities, mourning empty fields and abandoned herds. The great bureaucratic dairy machine broke apart.

## Why It Matters

Bronze Age Mesopotamia changed what cheese meant. In the Neolithic, cheese was a clever way for families to survive. In Sumer, it became part of a system: counted on clay, demanded as a quota, owed as a debt, offered to the gods, and handed out as wages. It helped feed the first cities, and the need to keep track of it helped push humanity toward writing.

The next time you see a cheese label with a weight, a date, and a producer's name, remember that the habit of recording cheese is about five thousand years old, and it started with a scribe and a lump of clay.
