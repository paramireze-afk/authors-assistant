# Oil Markets, Manipulation, and Geopolitics

*Notes from a conversation between host Chris Martenson and macroeconomist Philip Pilkington, recorded July 31, discussing oil price behavior, alleged market manipulation, and its connections to bonds, the dollar, gold, and broader geopolitics.*

## Overview

The conversation centers on a puzzle: despite an escalating Middle East war and a major disruption to oil supply, Brent crude prices did not spike the way historical precedent would suggest. The hosts walk through a theory of deliberate price suppression, then widen the lens to bond yields, the dollar, gold, China's strategic position, and long-term forecasts for Western living standards.

## Price Behavior That Doesn't Add Up

Pilkington traces his suspicion of market manipulation back to the Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping during the Biden administration. His baseline expectation, drawn from how markets reacted to the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, was that a real supply shock should move prices sharply. Instead, he and Martenson point to a chart showing Brent spiking after the war's outbreak but then sliding back down even as the conflict intensified — a pattern they argue is inconsistent with a genuinely free market.

Historically, they note, oil hasn't been a fully "free" market since OPEC's formation, since the cartel has effectively set prices since the 1973 embargo. Saudi Arabia's role as swing producer made watching Saudi policy a reliable proxy for price direction for decades.

## The Manipulation Theory

Pilkington's argument is that after the 2022 shock, and again during the 2023–24 Red Sea disruptions, U.S. authorities effectively learned they could suppress oil prices the way central banks influence bond yields — using a precedent he draws from the 2022 spike in Dutch TTF natural gas prices in Europe, which he says was brought under control through intervention.

His core mechanical claim is that any market priced through futures/paper contracts is inherently vulnerable to this kind of suppression, a idea he says dates back to his own graduate research and to debates among 1930s-era economists. He suggests the method could involve wash trades — a firm and its subsidiary trading against each other so losses stay contained — and references a March *Financial Times* piece in which oil traders speculated about a mystery large seller, which he nicknames the "whale of Hormuz."

## Inventories as a Warning Sign

Both hosts point to declining U.S. crude and product inventories, and specifically low levels at the Cushing, Oklahoma storage hub, as evidence that supply is tightening even though prices haven't reflected it. They compare current storage-capacity utilization unfavorably to prior low points in 2004 and 2008, arguing today's cushion is thinner in percentage terms despite larger nominal storage capacity.

## The China Angle and a 60-Day Ceasefire Theory

Pilkington offers a speculative narrative: that U.S. Treasury officials, worried about political fallout before the midterms, brokered an arrangement with China (discussed around a May 13 meeting) in which China would draw down its oil imports and tap reserves for roughly 60 days to help the U.S. manage the price shock. He connects this to a visible drop in Chinese crude imports beginning in April, and cites later data showing China's imports fell several million barrels per day versus 2025 averages, while South Korea's imports rose — which he interprets as China absorbing the brunt of the shock to help other regional partners maintain supply.

## Bond Yields, the Dollar, and Gold

A significant portion of the discussion links oil prices to bond markets. Pilkington's framing: long-term bond yields are essentially forward-looking inflation indicators, and since oil is the dominant driver of forward inflation expectations, suppressing oil prices is functionally a way of managing the yield curve without the Fed formally attempting yield curve control.

He argues that a historically reliable link — between U.S. real interest rates and the price of gold — broke down after the U.S. froze Russian sovereign reserves in February 2022, and that a related relationship between inflation-protected bond yields and the dollar has also decoupled since roughly the same period. His interpretation is that the traditional "flight to safety" dynamic (crises drive foreign capital into U.S. Treasuries, strengthening the dollar) may no longer hold, with gold — bolstered by sustained central bank buying — taking over as the safe-haven asset instead.

## The AI Investment Boom

The hosts discuss the AI capital expenditure boom as a parallel bubble narrative, describing large circular financing arrangements between AI firms and their partners/suppliers as difficult to fully untangle. They point to a same-week divergence between a large tech company's strong earnings and a smaller AI-focused fund's collapse as evidence of "market fragmentation" reminiscent of the dot-com bust, where some large incumbents survive a sector-wide correction while speculative names don't. They also raise a concern that AI data centers are being built on natural gas power rather than longer-term nuclear investment, and suggest this could create competition for energy resources, including with European gas supply.

## China, Taiwan, and Iran

Pilkington lays out a theory of Chinese strategic calculation: that China has largely avoided intervening on Iran's behalf partly because of Iran's historical reluctance to integrate Chinese air-defense systems, but more importantly because China may see long-term strategic benefit in the broader regional realignment — including a belief that a demonstrated inability of the U.S. military to achieve air superiority in a missile-dense environment (drawing on the Iran conflict as an analogue) reduces the credibility of any future U.S. defense of Taiwan. He also argues Taiwan's energy dependence makes it vulnerable to a Chinese blockade strategy over time, potentially leading to negotiated integration rather than open conflict.

## Forecasts for Living Standards

Toward the end, the conversation turns to a forecast of declining living standards in the West, driven by sustained high energy and food costs. Pilkington offers what he calls a conservative estimate: using the prior five to six years of inflation-driven living-standard erosion (which he estimates at roughly 10–15%) as a baseline, and assuming the current shock is roughly twice as severe, he projects a further decline that could bring cumulative living-standard losses to 40–50%. He and Martenson also discuss the possibility of formal "energy lockdowns" or rationing — prioritizing fuel and resources for logistics and production over consumer use — drawing comparisons to COVID-era restrictions and essential-worker designations.

## The WEF 2016 Video Reference
 
Near the end of the conversation, Martenson brings up a 2016 World Economic Forum video that he says predicted Western values would be "tested to the breaking point" by 2030. He frames this as notable given the episode's broader theme — that the West now appears to be facing exactly that kind of stress test. His point isn't a detailed breakdown of the video's contents, but a brief aside suggesting that when an institution states an intention or forecast years in advance and events later align with it, that alignment is worth noting rather than dismissing outright.
 
Pilkington responds by saying he senses there was some degree of advance preparation for the global shift they've been describing throughout the episode, and that much of what has unfolded over the past decade makes more sense if one assumes this kind of trajectory was anticipated by relevant institutions well in advance. Neither host goes further into specifics about the video itself — the reference functions more as a rhetorical capstone to their broader thesis than as a documented, evidenced claim.
 
It's worth noting this is a very brief, undetailed reference in the transcript — the hosts don't cite the video by name, quote it directly, or describe its production or sourcing. Given how often WEF videos and statements get cited in speculative or conspiratorial contexts online, it may be worth independently verifying what the video actually said and who produced it before treating this claim as established.

## Closing Notes

The episode closes with reflections on the geopolitical realignment toward BRICS nations, comparisons between U.S. and European vulnerability (Europe's energy and fertilizer dependence versus U.S. relative self-sufficiency), and personal remarks from Pilkington about relocating to a neutral country for energy and food security. Pilkington closes by promoting his own podcast, Multipolarity, co-hosted with Andrew Collingwood.
