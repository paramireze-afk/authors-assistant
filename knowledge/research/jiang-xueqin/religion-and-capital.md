# Jiang Xueqin on Religion and Capital

This note documents how Jiang Xueqin discusses religion in relation to capital, materialism, and political power, as presented in the July 2026 "Emergency Discussion" transcript. All claims below are Jiang's own, attributed throughout. Where he speculates or draws inference rather than stating something as established, that is marked explicitly.

## Overview

Across the discussion, Jiang returns repeatedly to religion as a variable in his broader theory of capital and empire (see the separate tracking note on his use of "transnational capital"). He does not present a single consistent position — instead, **he argues religion functions in two opposed ways simultaneously**: as a constraint that limits capital's ability to organize human behavior, and as a tool that makes certain groups more effective at accumulating and exercising power than others. Both roles recur throughout the session, sometimes in the same answer.

## Calvinism: The Origin of Disciplined Accumulation

Jiang's starting point for connecting religion to capital is the Protestant Reformation, specifically Calvinist theology.

- **He argues** that before the Reformation, the Catholic Church controlled salvation through the threat of excommunication and the promise of heaven or hell, giving it near-total authority over believers' behavior.
- **He describes** Calvinism's doctrine of "double predestination" as a direct challenge to that authority: in this account, God had already decided who would be saved *before* creation, meaning the Church had no actual power to determine anyone's fate.
- **He argues** this created a psychological problem — if salvation is already decided, how does a believer know whether they've been chosen? **His claim** is that worldly success became interpreted as a possible sign of divine favor, creating pressure to accumulate wealth as informal evidence of election.
- Critically, **he argues** this wealth could not simply be spent or displayed — spending it would look like vanity, which would undercut the appearance of virtue. The only acceptable use of wealth, in his account, was **reinvestment**.
- **He contrasts** this with what he describes as more typical historical patterns of wealth use — his example is Viking raiders who, after accumulating gold, redistributed it to followers and family to build social status through feasts and gift-giving. **He argues** Calvinism inverted this logic: status came not from spending wealth but from *not* spending it.

**He identifies** this dynamic as the origin point of a distinct culture of accumulation that, in his broader historical narrative, produced Dutch commercial wealth and eventually the Bank of England (covered in more detail in the transnational capital note). In this section of his argument, religion is the *engine* that makes disciplined capital accumulation possible in the first place — not yet a rival to capital, but its theological precondition.

## Religion versus Money as Competing Sources of Value

Elsewhere in the discussion, **Jiang shifts to treating religion and money as opposed value systems**, particularly in response to a participant (Anthony) who raises the topic of refugee resettlement.

- **He argues** that if someone is religious, they do not prioritize material accumulation — he states plainly that a religious person "doesn't care about having gold" or "driving a Ferrari."
- **He argues** more broadly that migration and refugee systems function as a kind of "modern day slavery," in the sense that displaced people are removed from language, family, and culture and become dependent on a new host society — and **he connects** this dependency to the interests of what he calls "international capital," which he says treats human beings as "energy to be harnessed."
- **He states**, discussing his own family history (his father's emigration from China to Canada), that the labor-exploitation dynamics he's describing are not abstract to him personally.
- **He offers a general maxim**: that prioritizing religion over money produces *more* wealth than prioritizing money over religion — stating, in response to a participant's paraphrase, that "if you put religion first, then you make the most money." This is presented as a direct claim, though he does not fully reconcile it with his earlier point that genuinely religious people are indifferent to wealth (see "Contradictions," below).

## Religion as a Coordinating Mechanism

A separate strand of Jiang's argument treats religion not as capital's opposite but as one of its most effective *tools* — specifically, as a mechanism for coordinating actors across borders and generations.

- **He argues** that within the broad pool of global capital (he lists pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, hedge funds, and major asset managers as examples), the actors capable of *secretly coordinating* with one another gain a decisive advantage over those acting independently.
- **He attributes** this coordinating capacity to what he calls secret societies, which he characterizes as being organized around what he terms "Zionist" eschatology.
- **He argues** that shared religious belief is more durable than shared financial interest alone, because a group bound only by profit can fracture when incentives shift, whereas a group bound by belief, identity, and historical narrative can act cohesively over long periods.
- **He connects** this specifically to the Jewish diaspora, which he describes as having been, in his words, historically effective at coordinating for trade, communication, and shared interest across dispersed populations — and, in this transcript, **he extends the claim further**, arguing that antisemitism has been deliberately fostered by these networks as a strategy to encourage Jewish emigration to Israel, tied to an eschatological belief in a coming conflict he calls "the war of Gog and Magog."

This is the most speculative and least evidenced portion of his framework in the transcript — he presents it as established analysis rather than as a hypothesis, but does not offer independent support for the existence or coordination of the secret societies he describes. This note attributes the claim to him without treating it as demonstrated.

## Religious Nationalism as a Political Force

Jiang extends his framework into predictions about contemporary and near-future politics, arguing that religious identity — specifically nationalist forms of religion — will be a decisive factor in which societies survive coming instability.

- **He argues** that Russia's embrace of Orthodox Christianity, Iran's use of **Shia eschatology**, and the growth of **Christian nationalism** in the United States are all examples of religion re-emerging as a force capable of resisting or challenging what he calls "transnational capital."
- **He predicts** that nations or regions with strong, cohesive religious and ethnic identity — he names Hungary, Poland, Austria, and the American South (Texas specifically) — will "survive" and "prosper" through coming instability, while regions he associates with multiculturalism and liberal secularism (he names California, New York City, and Boston) will decline.
- **He argues**, in response to a question about Germany, that Germany's future depends on whether it can "resummon" what he calls its historical nationalist energy, and **he suggests** — describing this explicitly as serving external interests — that the American and Israeli powers he describes elsewhere in the discussion may find it strategically useful to encourage German nationalist revival specifically so Germany will fight against Russia.
- **He states** a general framework in which religious nationalism functions as a source of resilience and cohesion, in direct contrast to what he calls "advanced western civilizations" that have become, in his words, "complacent," "corrupt," and "full of hubris."

## Religion, Empire, and Transnational Capital

Jiang connects religion to his broader theory of empire and capital migration (covered fully in the separate transnational capital note) in two distinct ways:

1. **As a constraint capital must overcome or route around.** He argues that historically, family loyalty, monarchy, and religion have all functioned as obstacles to capital's expansion, because each offers people a competing source of meaning, loyalty, and obligation that resists financial incentive.
2. **As a vehicle empire itself can adopt.** In discussing what he calls "Pax Judaica" — his term for a hypothesized future shift of global power toward Israel — **he argues** that this transition is bound up with religious eschatology specifically, not just financial or military calculation. In his account, the actors he describes are motivated in part by genuine eschatological belief (the "ingathering" of Jews to Israel, the anticipated "war of Gog and Magog"), not solely by material interest.

**He explicitly frames** this as a key mechanism by which certain actors "win" within the larger, uncoordinated pool of global capital: religious motivation, in his account, produces a level of long-term coordination and sacrifice that purely financial incentive cannot sustain.

## Material Progress versus Spiritual Development

In a later exchange (with a participant referred to as "Peter Thiel"), Jiang draws an explicit distinction between two ways of evaluating human progress:

- **He characterizes** one framework — which he attributes to the questioner's position — as fundamentally materialistic: judging progress by rising living standards, technological development, and the transfer of wealth to poorer regions (he uses the example of capital potentially shifting to Argentina and the "Global South").
- **He argues**, in contrast, for a framework centered on **spiritual enlightenment, human creativity, and connection to what he calls "the monad"** (a term he uses without extensive definition in this transcript) as the true measure of progress, independent of material conditions.
- **He argues** that a wealthier "Global South" achieved through centralized, AI-driven control would not represent genuine progress in his framework, because it would come at the cost of decentralization and creativity, which he associates with authentic human flourishing.
- **He frames** centralized elites — even when acting with stated benevolent intent — as inherently in tension with this spiritual/creative model of progress, describing them as "parasitic forces" regardless of their professed goals.

## Contradictions and Unresolved Tensions

Several tensions in Jiang's treatment of religion are not resolved within the transcript:

- **Religion as indifferent to wealth vs. religion as a wealth-maximizing strategy.** He states that genuinely religious people don't care about material wealth, but separately offers a maxim that prioritizing religion "first" produces more wealth than prioritizing money "first." These two claims sit uneasily together and he does not reconcile them explicitly.
- **Religion as a check on capital vs. religion as capital's most effective tool.** He argues both that religious conviction limits people's susceptibility to financial incentive (a constraint on capital) and that religiously-motivated coordination is what allows certain actors to out-compete and dominate less-coordinated capital (an instrument of capital's success). He does not specify what determines which role religion plays in a given case.
- **Universal principle vs. specific attribution.** His general claims about religion and cohesion (e.g., regarding Russian Orthodoxy, Shia Islam, Christian nationalism) are offered as a broadly applicable pattern, but his account of *why* this pattern currently favors a shift of power toward Israel rests on the more specific, unevidenced claims about secret societies and diaspora coordination discussed above. The transcript does not clarify whether he sees the general pattern (religion as cohesion) as independent of that specific claim, or as dependent on it.

## Central Tension: Rival and Tool

The organizing tension in Jiang's treatment of religion, as presented across this transcript, is that he does not treat religion as occupying one stable position relative to capital. Instead, **he presents it as capital's most significant rival and, simultaneously, one of capital's most effective organizing instruments** — with the same underlying property (the capacity of shared belief to override short-term material incentive) explaining both roles.

Where religion limits an individual's dependence on money, status, and institutional approval, Jiang treats it as a constraint on capital's power to organize human behavior. Where religion binds a *group* together across generations and borders more durably than shared financial interest could, he treats it as exactly the kind of coordinating mechanism that allows certain actors to accumulate disproportionate power within his broader "transnational capital" framework. The transcript does not resolve which effect dominates, or under what conditions religion shifts from restraining capital to serving it — this appears to remain an open, undeveloped part of his framework rather than a settled position.
