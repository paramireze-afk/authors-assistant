# The Paper Trail in the Sky

*What Jeffrey Epstein's pilots wrote down — and what they left blank — became one of the strangest pieces of evidence in the case.*

The document is almost comically mundane. It reads like a rental-car slip: a grid of eight blank lines, a trip number, a departure and arrival time. At the top it says **HYPERION AIR, INC. — PASSENGER MANIFEST**, and under *Pilots* two names are typed: Dave Rodgers, Larry Visoski. The aircraft is a Gulfstream, registration N909JE.

On the night of August 8, 2005, that plane flew from Santa Fe to Aspen. In the passenger column, someone wrote two real names — Jeffrey Epstein and G. Maxwell — and then six entries that are simply redacted, "1 Female," or "1 Male." A few hours later the same plane turned around and flew Aspen to Las Vegas; again, Epstein, Maxwell, and a handful of blanks.

That is the entire evidence system, in miniature. The richest man on the manifest gets a name. The women around him get a box.

## The ledger in the cockpit

The pilots kept their own record. According to Larry Visoski's FBI interview, the captain filled out a manifest for every leg of every flight, either in the air or "about 30 or 45 minutes after" landing. If Visoski knew a passenger, he wrote the name down — "it was usually the same people that the pilots knew." But the men in the cockpit were, by their own account, paying attention to something else entirely:

> "The pilots were more concerned about the head count and the weight of the persons."

Put Visoski on a witness stand, under cross-examination at Ghislaine Maxwell's 2021 trial, and he said it again, in the plainest possible terms. The prosecutor asked about the notation "female," and Visoski answered: "Female if we didn't catch a name." When he did know the name, he put it down. "Absolutely." And the point of the list? Head count. And weight.

So the document that would later be read as a record of who Epstein flew with was, by the pilots' own testimony, a weight-and-balance sheet that happened to have names on it. It was never meant to catch a crime. It was meant to make sure the plane would take off.

## A document that forgot to record the crime

That distinction is the whole story. Because a weight-and-balance sheet doesn't need names — and the Federal Aviation Administration didn't demand them either. A law-enforcement note in the file spells out the loophole:

> "Private jet flights May/may not file flight plan they don't have to… FAA does not require to keep name just # of those on plane."

The result was a record that was voluminous and nearly useless at the same time. The logs run to thousands of flights between 1995 and 2013, kept by David Rodgers — "just one of at least six pilots," alongside Bill Hammond, Pete Rathgeb, Gary Roxburgh and Bill Murphy. But lawyers who fought over them in the civil suits found the paper trail had holes you could fly a 727 through. In one filing, attorneys for a Jane Doe wrote that the logs "do not contain evidence of all of the flights that she was on and that they are grossly incomplete" — even though they did confirm she was flown to Florida, New York, London, and elsewhere while under the age of 18.

A document kept by the people who worked for Epstein, on a plane owned by Epstein, faxed to Epstein's office, was somehow the weakest possible witness.
