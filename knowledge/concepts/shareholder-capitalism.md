# Shareholder Capitalism: A Plain-English Guide

## The basic idea

Every business has to answer a simple-sounding question: **who is the company actually for?**

There are two classic answers.

**Shareholder capitalism** says: the company exists to make money for the people who own it. Employees, customers, and the community matter, but mostly because keeping them happy is *good for profits* — not because the company owes them anything directly.

**Stakeholder capitalism** says: the company owes something to everyone it touches — shareholders, yes, but also employees, customers, suppliers, the communities it operates in, and sometimes the environment and society at large. Profit is one goal among several, not the only one.

That's it. That's the whole debate in one paragraph. Everything else — ESG, DEI, "woke capitalism," BlackRock, Davos — is really just people arguing about how far to take that second idea, and who gets to decide.

---

## Where the "shareholders only" idea came from

The clearest, most famous statement of the shareholder view came from economist **Milton Friedman**, in a 1970 *New York Times Magazine* essay with a title that says it all: **"The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits."**

Friedman's argument was pretty sharp: corporations aren't people, so they can't have "responsibilities" in a moral sense — only *people* can. A corporate executive is an employee hired by the shareholders. If that executive starts spending company money on social causes instead of maximizing returns, they're essentially spending *other people's money* on their own pet projects — without being elected, without a vote, without accountability. If society wants businesses to behave differently (less pollution, more diversity, whatever), Friedman said, the right tool is democracy and law — pass a regulation — not asking CEOs to freelance as moral philosophers.

This became known as **shareholder primacy**, and for about three decades (1970s–1990s) it was close to gospel in American business schools and boardrooms.

---

## Where the "stakeholders matter too" idea came from

Interestingly, the pushback started almost immediately in the corporate strategy world, not the activist world.

In 1984, a business professor named **R. Edward Freeman** published *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach*. His point wasn't really moral — it was practical. He argued that any group who can "affect or be affected by" a company (employees, suppliers, regulators, communities, competitors) has real power over whether that company succeeds. Ignore them, and they can sink you — through strikes, lawsuits, boycotts, bad press, or lost trust. Manage those relationships well, Freeman said, and you build a *more* successful, more durable company. Only 2,000 copies of the book were printed initially, and Freeman later joked that "no one cared" — but the idea slowly built momentum over the following decades and he's now often called the father of modern stakeholder theory.

Around the same time, on the other side of the Atlantic, a young engineering-professor-turned-organizer named **Klaus Schwab** was thinking along similar lines. In 1971 he wrote about a "stakeholder" model of management, and that same year he founded what became the **World Economic Forum** — the organization behind the annual Davos meeting. In 1973, Forum members signed the first "Davos Manifesto," an early statement that companies should serve more than just their shareholders. Schwab kept pushing this idea for fifty years, culminating in his 2021 book *Stakeholder Capitalism: A Global Economy that Works for Progress, People and Planet* and an updated 2020 Davos Manifesto.

So you've got two separate roots: an American management professor making a "this is smart business" case, and a European institution-builder making a "this is how we save capitalism from itself" case. Both converged on the same word: *stakeholder*.

---

## The 2019 moment everything changed

For decades, stakeholder capitalism was mostly an academic and Davos-conference idea. Then, in **August 2019**, the **Business Roundtable** — a group representing the CEOs of America's largest companies — released a new "Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation." It was a big deal because the Roundtable's *previous* statement, in place since 1997, had explicitly endorsed shareholder primacy. The new one, signed by 181 CEOs (including JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon and Johnson & Johnson's Alex Gorsky), pledged to deliver value to customers, employees, suppliers, and communities — with shareholders listed last.

It was treated as a historic pivot away from the Friedman era.

Not everyone bought it, though. Harvard Law professors **Lucian Bebchuk and Roberto Tallarita** later dug through the actual corporate paperwork — governance guidelines, bylaws, proxy filings — of the 128 companies that signed. Their finding, in a paper called *"The Illusory Promise of Stakeholder Governance,"* was blunt: almost none of the companies actually changed their internal governance rules to reflect the new promise. When they called company PR offices and asked who had approved signing the statement, most said it was just the CEO — not even the board. Their conclusion: for most signatories, it was closer to a PR move than a binding commitment.

This tension — big public promises about stakeholders vs. what companies actually do internally — comes up again and again in this topic, so keep it in your back pocket.

## Enter BlackRock and Larry Fink's letters

**BlackRock** is the world's largest asset manager, controlling trillions of dollars — much of it through **index funds**, which are funds that just buy a little of everything in a market index (like the S&P 500) rather than picking stocks. Because index funds own tiny slices of almost every major public company, BlackRock (along with Vanguard and State Street) ends up being one of the largest shareholders of nearly every big company in America. That gives it enormous voting power in **proxy votes** — the elections companies hold for shareholders to weigh in on board seats, executive pay, and shareholder proposals.

Since 2012, BlackRock CEO **Larry Fink** has written an annual public letter to CEOs. Starting around 2018, these letters increasingly pushed companies to articulate a "purpose" beyond profit and to pay attention to stakeholders — employees, climate risk, social issues. Because BlackRock could back up its opinions with actual votes on actual boards, these letters carried real weight, not just vibes.

But the backlash came fast and from multiple directions:
- **Conservatives** accused Fink of using other people's retirement money to push a political and environmental agenda they didn't sign up for.
- **Progressives and some financial analysts** pointed out that BlackRock doesn't actually divest from fossil fuels or misbehaving companies at any real scale — the "stakeholder" talk didn't change much about where the money actually went.

By 2022, Fink's letter opened defensively, insisting stakeholder capitalism "is not woke" and "is not a social or ideological agenda" — just good long-term business. By 2023, under mounting political pressure (including several states pulling billions in pension money from BlackRock over its ESG stance), Fink told reporters he'd stopped using the term "ESG" altogether because it had become "weaponized" by both the far left and far right.

## What is ESG, actually?

**ESG** stands for **Environmental, Social, and Governance** — it's a framework investors use to score companies on things beyond pure financial statements: carbon emissions, labor practices, board diversity, executive pay structure, anti-corruption policies, and so on.

It's easy to conflate ESG with stakeholder capitalism, but they're not quite the same thing:
- **Stakeholder capitalism** is a theory about what a company's *purpose* should be.
- **ESG** is a set of *metrics investors use* to evaluate companies — originally sold as a way to identify risks (a company polluting a river might get sued or regulated later) and opportunities, not necessarily as activism.

In practice, though, the two got tangled together, especially once ESG ratings started influencing which companies got included in "sustainable" investment funds, and once ESG scores started factoring in social issues like diversity.

This is also where **corporate social responsibility (CSR)** fits in — CSR is the older, more voluntary cousin of ESG: things like corporate philanthropy, sustainability reports, and community programs, generally run by a company's PR or communications team. ESG tried to make similar concerns more rigorous and investable, with actual scores and disclosure standards.

## The anti-ESG backlash

Starting around 2021–2022, a wave of conservative state governments began fighting back against ESG investing:

- **Texas** passed a law (2021) barring the state from doing business with financial firms deemed to be "boycotting" the fossil fuel industry, and later published a list of banned firms that included BlackRock.
- **Florida**, under Governor Ron DeSantis, pulled about $2 billion in state pension money from BlackRock in 2022 and passed a law in 2023 barring ESG factors from state investment decisions entirely, requiring investments to focus only on "pecuniary" (financial) factors.
- **West Virginia, Louisiana, Missouri**, and others pulled hundreds of millions more.

The core argument from these states: public pension money belongs to retirees, and fund managers have a legal duty to maximize *their* returns — not to use that money to pursue climate or social goals the retirees never agreed to.

Meanwhile, Democrats in Congress pushed back the other way — in 2023, President Biden issued his *first-ever veto* to block a Republican-led resolution that would have restricted retirement plan managers from considering ESG factors, arguing it would put people's savings at *more* financial risk, not less, by ignoring real-world risks like climate change.

Notice the interesting reversal: both sides accuse the other of putting politics ahead of pure financial returns.

## Vivek Ramaswamy's "Woke, Inc." — the sharpest popular critique

If you want the most widely-read, plain-spoken attack on stakeholder capitalism from the political right, it's Vivek Ramaswamy's 2021 bestseller **"Woke, Inc.: Inside Corporate America's Social Justice Scam."** Ramaswamy is a former biotech entrepreneur (Roivant Sciences) who later became a political figure, including a 2024 presidential candidate.

His argument, boiled down:

**1. It's mostly a performance, not a conviction.** Ramaswamy argues that most executives who talk about stakeholder capitalism, diversity, or environmental commitments don't actually believe in these causes deeply — they've realized that *appearing* virtuous is profitable. A company can slap a rainbow logo on its website for Pride Month while doing business in countries that criminalize homosexuality, or post a Black Lives Matter statement while manufacturing products with forced labor overseas. He calls this stuff "wokewashing" — cheap, symbolic gestures that let companies dodge harder questions about their actual practices.

**2. Unelected companies shouldn't be setting social policy.** His deeper worry isn't really about whether diversity or sustainability are good goals — it's about *who gets to decide*. CEOs are accountable to shareholders and (loosely) to customers, not to voters. When corporations start acting like moral or political authorities — deciding which laws to support, which causes to fund, which speech to allow on their platforms — Ramaswamy argues that important decisions about how society should work get made by people nobody elected and nobody can vote out.

**3. Capitalism and politics should be kept separate — like church and state.** This is Ramaswamy's central metaphor. He argues America runs on a set of institutions that work *because* they're kept apart: markets, democracy, science, and religion each do their own job. When capitalism starts doing democracy's job (setting social values) — or when government pressures private companies to do things the government itself isn't allowed to do directly, like restrict speech — you get a kind of soft authoritarianism that's harder to notice, and harder to vote against, than the regular kind. He personally stepped down as CEO of his own company partly to make this argument credibly — separating his role as a businessperson from his role as a political commentator.

**One useful note for the record:** Ramaswamy's book is a *polemic* — a persuasive argument, not a neutral history. He picks vivid examples (Google and Apple criticized for staying quiet on human rights abuses in China's Xinjiang region while promoting social causes domestically, for instance) to make his case forcefully. Readers across the spectrum have found parts of his critique persuasive even when they disagree with his proposed solution (basically: businesses should shut up and make money; let voters and elected governments handle everything else).

Interestingly, some reviewers have pointed out that Ramaswamy's core mechanism — "companies embrace social causes as a *distraction* from harder scrutiny" — is almost identical to a critique made by progressive writers too (more on that next). They just disagree about what should replace it.

## The critique from the political left

While the right worries stakeholder capitalism gives companies too much unaccountable *cultural* power, many on the left make almost the opposite complaint: that stakeholder capitalism is mostly **PR that lets companies dodge real accountability**, and it doesn't come close to fixing the actual problem — concentrated corporate and economic power.

The sharpest popular version of this came from journalist **Anand Giridharadas** in his 2018 book *Winners Take All: The Elite Charade of Changing the World*. His argument: wealthy elites love announcing they're "changing the world" through philanthropy, conscious capitalism, and stakeholder-friendly branding — because it lets them look generous while never threatening the systems (low taxes, weak regulation, concentrated wealth) that made them rich in the first place. His famous line: "the winners of our age...have amassed vast power over the levers of change" and would rather offer "scraps" from the top than support the "grueling democratic work" of building fairer public institutions.

Legal scholars on this side of the debate (echoing Bebchuk and Tallarita's research above) argue that stakeholder capitalism, as actually practiced, gives boards and executives more *discretion* — more excuses — without giving employees, communities, or the public any real new *power*, like board seats or legal enforcement rights. If a company says it cares about workers but workers still have no seat at the table and no legal claim if the company breaks its promise, what actually changed?

So: the right worries companies have taken on too much unaccountable moral authority; parts of the left worry it's the opposite — that "stakeholder" talk is a smokescreen that leaves the underlying power structure untouched, or even reinforces it. Both critiques, oddly, distrust the sincerity of the corporate promise — they just disagree about what the fix should be.

## Is any of this actually legal? The fiduciary duty question

Here's a wrinkle that surprises a lot of people: in the U.S., corporate law (most importantly **Delaware law**, since most big companies incorporate there) does **not** strictly require a "shareholders only" approach the way Friedman's essay implied.

The key legal concept is **fiduciary duty** — directors and officers owe duties of *care* and *loyalty* to "the corporation and its stockholders." Courts generally give boards wide latitude under something called the **business judgment rule**: as long as directors act in good faith, with due care, and without a personal conflict of interest, courts won't second-guess their decisions — even decisions that clearly benefit non-shareholder stakeholders (paying workers more, cutting emissions, sourcing more expensively but ethically), *as long as* the board can plausibly connect it to long-term value for the company.

There's one narrow legal doctrine that's stricter — the **Revlon duty** — which only kicks in when a company is actively being sold for cash to a new controlling owner. In that specific moment, the board does have to focus on getting the highest price for shareholders. Outside of that narrow scenario, U.S. law gives boards much more room than the "shareholders are the only legal priority" myth suggests.

So legally, a company *can* pursue stakeholder-friendly policies almost anytime it wants — the real constraints are usually *market pressure* (investors punishing "unprofitable" social spending) and *political pressure* (state laws, like Texas's and Florida's anti-ESG rules, that specifically restrict how public pension money can be invested), not some hard legal rule that companies must serve shareholders alone.

## The COVID-19 stress test

The pandemic became an unplanned real-world experiment for stakeholder capitalism. In 2020, plenty of CEOs who'd signed the Business Roundtable statement or talked about stakeholder values were suddenly forced to choose, fast: protect worker pay and jobs at a real cost to shareholders, or protect the balance sheet.

Results were genuinely mixed. Some companies extended paid leave, hazard pay, and kept workers on payroll at a real financial cost. Others — including some Business Roundtable signatories — laid off large numbers of workers or cut hours quickly, even while continuing dividend payments and stock buybacks that primarily benefited shareholders. Critics on the left cited this as more evidence that 2019's pledges were mostly words; defenders argued that companies were doing the best they could in an unprecedented, existential crisis, and that keeping the company alive *was* the best thing they could do for every stakeholder, workers included.

## DEI: the most visible battleground

**Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI)** programs — hiring goals, training, employee resource groups, supplier diversity targets — became the most visible, most politically charged piece of the stakeholder-capitalism package in corporate America, especially after 2020.

By 2024–2025, the pendulum swung hard the other way. After a change in federal administration and a wave of legal and political pressure — including a January 2025 executive order aimed at ending DEI programs tied to federal contracting — a long list of major companies scaled back or eliminated DEI initiatives: Meta, Google, Amazon, Boeing, McDonald's, Walmart, Accenture, and many banks among them. Some companies quietly stopped mentioning "diversity" in their annual filings altogether. A handful of companies — Costco and Cisco among the more visible ones — publicly defended keeping their programs, arguing there was real business value in them.

This whipsaw — big public commitments in 2020, quiet reversals by 2025 — is probably the single clearest real-world illustration of the whole stakeholder-capitalism debate: how much of it is durable belief, and how much is following whichever way the political and market winds are blowing?

## So... does stakeholder capitalism "work"?

Honestly, the honest answer is: it depends what you mean by "work," and there isn't strong consensus.

**The case for it:**
- Companies that treat employees and customers well often *do* perform better over the long run — this isn't just a feel-good claim, it's Freeman's original, pretty pragmatic argument.
- Ignoring risks like climate change, supply-chain labor abuses, or reputational blowups can create *real* financial losses down the road — so considering "stakeholders" can just be good risk management with a nicer name.
- It can act as a partial substitute for regulation in areas where government moves too slowly (though critics say this is exactly the problem — it lets unelected companies fill a role government should be filling through democratic processes).

**The case against it:**
- It's vague. "Balance shareholder and stakeholder interests" doesn't tell a manager what to actually do when those interests genuinely conflict (should we pay workers more this quarter or protect the dividend?). Vague standards are hard to enforce and easy to talk around.
- The empirical evidence that companies substantially change behavior after making stakeholder pledges is thin — the Bebchuk/Tallarita research is the clearest example.
- It can concentrate more, not less, unaccountable power in corporate hands — precisely because it lets executives claim broad social and moral authority without correspondingly broad accountability to the people affected.
- It's become a political football, which arguably makes it *harder*, not easier, for companies to make consistent long-term commitments to workers, communities or the environment — since policy support flips every time political power changes hands.

## The one-paragraph summary, if you only remember this

Stakeholder capitalism is the idea that companies owe something to more than just their shareholders. It has a genuinely serious intellectual pedigree (Freeman, Schwab) and a genuinely serious critique (Friedman, and later, from very different angles, both Ramaswamy and Giridharadas). Legally, U.S. companies have much more freedom to pursue it than people assume — the real battles are political and cultural, not really about what the law requires. And in practice, the gap between what companies *say* about stakeholders and what they actually *do* — whether during COVID, on DEI, or on ESG — is the thing worth watching most closely, because that gap is where the real story of this debate lives.
