# Zionism

> **Entry type:** Foundational conceptual/historical reference — the base entry for this knowledge base's Israel/Palestine cluster
> **Core subject:** Zionism as a modern political and intellectual movement — its origins, internal schools, key thinkers, relationship to Judaism and Jewish identity, and its contested relationship to nationalism, colonialism, and religion.
> **Related entries:** `clean-break-1996.md`, `pnac.md`, `greater-israel-project.md`. This entry is meant to sit *underneath* those three — they assume a working knowledge of Revisionist Zionism in particular, which is covered in real depth there and only summarized here to avoid duplication.
> **A note on scope:** Zionism is not one thing. This entry's central organizing claim, stated at the outset rather than left implicit, is that "Zionism" has named at least six analytically distinct — and frequently mutually hostile — movements since the 1880s, unified mainly by a shared commitment to some form of Jewish national revival connected to the Land of Israel, and disagreeing sharply on nearly everything else: what kind of polity to build, on what territory, through what means, on what religious or secular basis, and in what relationship to the Arab population already living there. Treating any one school's claims as representative of "Zionism" as a whole is one of the most common errors in both pro- and anti-Zionist popular writing, and this entry tries to avoid it throughout.

## 2. Before Herzl: The Movement's Deeper Roots

Zionism did not spring fully formed from Herzl's 1896 pamphlet, and treating him as sole originator — as some popular accounts do — obscures a longer prehistory that matters for understanding the movement's later internal splits.

**Religious longing versus political program.** The Jewish liturgical and devotional tradition had, for roughly two millennia before Herzl, incorporated prayers and observances oriented toward Zion/Jerusalem and a hoped-for return — "next year in Jerusalem," recited at the close of the Passover seder, being the best-known example. This devotional yearning was near-universal across traditional Jewish communities and predates any political movement by centuries; it is not itself Zionism in the modern sense (a political program for sovereign statehood), and conflating the two — treating ancient devotional longing as evidence that political Zionism is simply the natural, inevitable expression of Judaism as such — is a move some Zionist popularizers make and some critics of Zionism reject just as forcefully from the other direction, arguing (as anti-Zionist and non-Zionist religious Jews have, across a range of traditions from Reform to Haredi) that the devotional tradition never implied or required a modern sovereign nation-state and that treating it as though it did collapses a religious category into a political one.

**Proto-Zionist thinkers before Herzl.** Rabbi Yehuda Alkalai and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer, both writing in the mid-19th century, argued in explicitly religious terms for organized Jewish resettlement of Palestine as a precursor to messianic redemption — an early template for what would later become religious Zionism (§6). Moses Hess, a secular socialist associate of Karl Marx's, published *Rome and Jerusalem* in 1862, arguing — well before Herzl and from an explicitly secular-nationalist rather than religious framework — that Jews constituted a nation requiring territorial self-determination like other 19th-century national movements then asserting themselves across Europe; this text is frequently cited (including in material reviewed for this entry) as a direct intellectual forerunner of Labor Zionism's later synthesis of nationalism and socialism (§5).

**Hovevei Zion.** Beginning in the 1880s, following waves of pogroms in the Russian Empire (particularly after the 1881 assassination of Tsar Alexander II, which triggered a wave of anti-Jewish violence), small "Lovers of Zion" (Hovevei Zion) societies formed across Eastern Europe, organizing and funding early waves of Jewish agricultural settlement in Ottoman Palestine — what would later be retroactively termed the First Aliyah (roughly 1882–1903). These groups had no unified ideology, coherent political program, or diplomatic strategy; multiple sources reviewed for this entry describe pre-Herzl Zionist activity in exactly these terms — real, organized, but lacking the coherent modern ideology and program Herzl would later provide. This matters for understanding what was genuinely new about Herzl's contribution: not the idea of Jewish return to Palestine, which predated him by decades in both religious and early-nationalist forms, but the attempt to organize that aspiration into a coherent, internationally legible *political* project seeking sovereign statehood through diplomacy with the era's great powers.

## 3. Political Zionism: Herzl and the Basel Program

**Herzl's biography and the Dreyfus catalyst.** Theodor Herzl (1860–1904), born in Budapest to an assimilated, German-speaking Austro-Hungarian Jewish family, trained as a lawyer and worked as a Vienna-based journalist, serving from 1891 as the Paris correspondent for the *Neue Freie Presse*. Herzl's own account, and most subsequent biography, credits his coverage of the 1894–95 Dreyfus Affair — in which a Jewish French army officer, Alfred Dreyfus, was falsely convicted of treason amid an eruption of explicitly antisemitic public sentiment across supposedly liberal, post-Enlightenment France — as the direct catalyst for his conversion from a believer in Jewish assimilation into European society to an advocate of a fully separate Jewish state. Some historians (this entry has not independently verified the strength of the primary-source basis for this causal claim beyond its wide secondary repetition) note Herzl had already been engaging seriously with "the Jewish question" for over a decade before the Dreyfus trial, since at least his 1882 reading of Eugen Dühring's antisemitic tract *Die Judenfrage*, suggesting the Dreyfus Affair may have crystallized and publicly catalyzed, rather than originated, Herzl's thinking.

**The core argument of *Der Judenstaat* (1896).** Herzl's central claim was that antisemitism was not a correctable social prejudice, resolvable through Jewish assimilation, education, or liberal reform, but a persistent structural feature of majority-minority relations in Europe that no amount of Jewish civic integration could overcome — a position that put him at odds with the dominant 19th-century liberal-Jewish faith in emancipation and assimilation. His proposed solution was explicitly political and diplomatic rather than religious or gradualist: an internationally recognized, legally chartered Jewish state, to be secured through negotiation with the era's great powers (Herzl personally sought audiences with, among others, the Ottoman Sultan, the German Kaiser, and British colonial officials) rather than through unauthorized mass settlement. Herzl was notably agnostic, at least initially, about the specific territory — his 1896 pamphlet discussed both Palestine and Argentina as possibilities, and the 1903 "Uganda Plan" (actually a proposal for Jewish settlement in a portion of British East Africa, in present-day Kenya) that Herzl himself brought to the Sixth Zionist Congress as a temporary refuge proposal, provoked the movement's first major internal schism — ultimately rejected by the Congress, with a walkout by delegates (mostly from Russia, where the pogrom threat was most acute) who insisted Palestine, and only Palestine, could be the legitimate object of Zionist settlement.

**The Basel Program and organizational Zionism.** The First Zionist Congress, convened by Herzl in Basel in August 1897, adopted the Basel Program, declaring that "Zionism seeks to establish a home for the Jewish people in Palestine secured by public law" — carefully worded diplomatic language (avoiding, notably, the word "state" in favor of the vaguer "home," a deliberate softening intended not to alarm Ottoman authorities or European powers) that would remain the movement's formal platform for the following two decades. The Congress also established the World Zionist Organization as the movement's permanent institutional vehicle, with Herzl as its first president — the beginning of Zionism's transformation from a scattered set of local societies and individual thinkers into an organized international political movement with congresses, elected leadership, a press (Herzl founded the newspaper *Die Welt*), and a sustained diplomatic strategy.

**Herzl's death and the movement's fracturing.** Herzl died in 1904 at age 44, having achieved no binding great-power commitment to Jewish statehood and having already, in the Uganda controversy, revealed how contested even the movement's basic territorial premise was among his own followers. His successor as WZO leader, Chaim Weizmann, would go on to secure the 1917 Balfour Declaration — Britain's landmark statement of support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people" — but by the time of Herzl's death, the movement he founded had already begun splitting into the distinct schools this entry covers next, each claiming a different piece of his legacy while rejecting others.

## 4. Cultural Zionism: Ahad Ha'am's Dissent

**The core disagreement with Herzl.** Asher Ginzberg (1856–1927), writing under the pen name Ahad Ha'am ("one of the people"), was Herzl's most consequential intellectual critic from within the Zionist movement itself, and the founder of what came to be called Cultural (or Spiritual) Zionism. Where Herzl's political Zionism prioritized rapid, internationally chartered statehood as the primary and most urgent solution to antisemitism, Ahad Ha'am — writing from Odessa, deeply embedded in the flourishing Hebrew literary culture of the Russian Pale of Settlement — argued this got the priorities backward. His formulation, quoted across multiple sources reviewed for this entry, was that he sought "a Jewish state and not merely a state of Jews" — i.e., a polity whose *content*, not merely its formal existence, was authentically and substantively Jewish, which he believed required a prior, patient process of cultural, linguistic, and ethical renewal (centered on the revival of Hebrew as a living literary and eventually spoken language) rather than a rapid diplomatic sprint toward statehood that might, in his view, produce a state that was merely "of Jews" in a demographic sense without any coherent Jewish cultural or spiritual content.

**The "spiritual center" concept.** Ahad Ha'am's alternative vision, developed across essays from the 1890s through the 1900s, proposed Palestine not as a mass refuge for the entirety of world Jewry (a population movement he considered logistically and diplomatically unrealistic in the near term) but as a "spiritual center" — a concentrated site of Hebrew cultural and intellectual renewal, populated by a dedicated elite, that would then radiate renewed Jewish identity and meaning outward to the much larger diaspora communities who would, in his framework, continue to live outside Palestine indefinitely. This is a genuinely different theory of what Zionism was *for* than Herzl's — not chiefly a refuge from persecution, but a remedy for what Ahad Ha'am diagnosed as the deeper crisis of secularizing, assimilating Diaspora Jewish identity losing its coherent cultural content under the pressure of European modernity.

**Institutional legacy.** Cultural Zionism's influence proved considerable within the movement's practical institution-building even as it never became the dominant political faction: the strong Zionist institutional emphasis on Hebrew-language revival (as opposed to Yiddish, the actual vernacular of most early immigrants), Hebrew education, and cultural production in Palestine owes substantially to Ahad Ha'am's framework, and the WZO formally incorporated cultural-Zionist priorities as an explicit programmatic pillar following the Sixth Zionist Congress in 1903. The poet and essayist Chaim Nachman Bialik, a central figure in the Hebrew literary revival, worked closely within this tradition.

**Martin Buber and binationalism.** The philosopher Martin Buber (1878–1965) extended and, in important respects, radicalized the cultural-Zionist critique of political Zionism's statist and, in his view, insufficiently ethical orientation. Buber — later renowned for his philosophy of dialogic relation ("I and Thou") — became a leading advocate, particularly from the 1920s through the 1940s, of Jewish-Arab binationalism: the position that the Zionist project's moral legitimacy depended on building a shared, cooperative political structure with the Arab population of Palestine rather than pursuing an exclusively Jewish sovereign state, a position institutionally expressed through his involvement with the small but intellectually significant Brit Shalom ("Covenant of Peace") movement in the 1920s–30s. Buber's binationalist current was a minority position within the Zionist movement even at its peak of institutional influence and was decisively marginalized by the trajectory of events from the 1936–39 Arab Revolt through the 1947–49 war, but it represents a genuinely distinct, non-trivial current within Zionism's own internal debates that later critics of Zionism (including, from a very different vantage, Rashid Khalidi — see §11) sometimes overlook in treating the movement as monolithically statist and exclusivist from the outset, when a substantial and serious minority current within the movement explicitly rejected that framework on Zionist grounds.

## 5. Labor Zionism: Building the State Through Socialist Settlement

**The core synthesis.** Labor (or Socialist) Zionism combined Zionist nationalism with socialist or social-democratic economic and political theory, and became, through its dominance of the pre-state Yishuv's (the Jewish community in Mandate Palestine) major institutions — the Histadrut labor federation, the Haganah defense force, and eventually the Mapai party — the single most institutionally consequential school of Zionism in the movement's practical, state-building history, culminating in David Ben-Gurion's leadership of the state from 1948 through the mid-1960s (with an interruption) and Labor's essentially uninterrupted political dominance of Israel until the watershed 1977 election that brought Revisionist Zionism's Likud to power for the first time (see the Clean Break entry, §5, for that transition's connection to later events).

**Ber Borochov's Marxist Zionism.** Dov Ber Borochov (1881–1917), the movement's most rigorous theoretical figure, attempted a genuine synthesis of Marxist class analysis with Jewish nationalism — a combination many contemporary orthodox Marxists considered close to a contradiction in terms, since classical Marxism generally treated nationalism as a bourgeois phenomenon destined to dissolve under proletarian internationalism. Borochov's "inverted pyramid" analysis argued the Jewish Diaspora's economic structure was pathologically distorted — an excess of intellectuals, traders, and middlemen and a deficit of actual productive (especially agricultural and industrial) labor, itself a consequence of centuries of exclusion from land ownership and guild trades in the European societies where most Jews lived — and that this distortion could only be corrected through concentrated settlement in a single territory where Jewish workers could reconstruct a "normal," complete class structure, including a genuine Jewish proletariat and, eventually, a Jewish capitalist class, as the necessary precondition for authentic participation in the international class struggle. Borochov's specifically Marxist and economically deterministic framework had its greatest influence in Eastern Europe (via the Left Poale Zion movement) rather than in Palestine itself, where — per the sourced material reviewed for this entry — Jewish immigrants' practical struggle simply to establish an economic foothold made rigid doctrinal class analysis increasingly untenable by the 1910s, with even Borochov himself reportedly moving away from his earlier rigid formulations later in life.

**A.D. Gordon's labor mysticism.** Aaron David Gordon (1856–1922), a genuinely distinct figure from Borochov despite sharing the "Labor Zionist" label, arrived in Palestine in 1905 at the unusually advanced age of 47 (having spent most of his life as an estate manager in Russia rather than as a professional revolutionary or theorist) and developed an influential, explicitly non-Marxist philosophy in which physical agricultural labor itself carried near-mystical, redemptive significance — a form of personal and national healing for a Jewish people Gordon believed had been pathologically alienated from physical connection to land and nature across centuries of urban Diaspora existence. Gordon's Hapoel Hatzair ("The Young Worker") party explicitly rejected the vocabulary of "socialism" and class struggle in favor of this more spiritually inflected labor philosophy, and Gordon and his followers founded Deganya, the first kibbutz (collective agricultural settlement), in 1910 — an institution that would become one of the Zionist movement's most iconic and internationally mythologized institutional forms, though never housing more than a small fraction of the total Jewish population even at the kibbutz movement's peak.

**Ben-Gurion: from ideologue to statesman.** David Ben-Gurion (1886–1973), who arrived in Palestine in 1906 and would become Israel's founding prime minister, is frequently but per sourced material somewhat imprecisely remembered as Labor Zionism's chief theorist; several historians reviewed for this entry (including material specifically distinguishing Ben-Gurion's actual intellectual formation from his later public image) note he explicitly abandoned what he termed "proletarian Zionism" — rigid Borochovian class analysis — after 1919, in favor of what one source characterizes as a more "voluntaristic" nationalism closer in spirit to general European "organic" romantic nationalism than to doctrinal Marxism, shunning strikes and formal class-struggle rhetoric in a manner closer to Gordon's Hapoel Hatzair than to Borochov's Poale Zion. Ben-Gurion's genuine and lasting historical significance lay less in original theoretical contribution than in organizational and political execution: building the Histadrut into the Yishuv's dominant economic and social institution, consolidating the Haganah as the core of what would become the Israel Defense Forces, and ultimately leading the political coalition (Mapai, later Labor) that would govern the pre-state Yishuv and then the State of Israel for its first three decades. Avi Shlaim's *The Iron Wall* (2001) — a work generally associated with the "New Historians" current discussed in §11 — makes the specific and, per the sourced material reviewed, historiographically significant argument that by 1948 Ben-Gurion, despite his Labor Zionist and self-described socialist political identity, had in practice adopted a strategic posture toward the surrounding Arab world substantially closer to the "Iron Wall" framework Ze'ev Jabotinsky had articulated on the ideological right in the 1920s (see the Clean Break entry, §6.1, and the Greater Israel entry, §3, for Jabotinsky's own doctrine) than to any specifically Labor Zionist or socialist theory of Arab-Jewish relations — a claim that, if accepted, complicates any account treating Labor and Revisionist Zionism as simply opposite poles on questions of security strategy, whatever their real and substantial differences on economic policy, religion, and formal territorial maximalism.

## 6. Religious Zionism: From Marginal Curiosity to Governing Coalition Partner

**The prior orthodox consensus against Zionism.** It is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to lose in retrospect: the great majority of traditionally Orthodox rabbinic authority in the late 19th and early 20th centuries was hostile or at best deeply ambivalent toward political Zionism, for reasons that were themselves religious rather than merely traditionalist inertia — chiefly the concern that a human, political effort to "force the end" (hasty a-l ha-ketz) and reestablish Jewish sovereignty in the Land of Israel through secular political and military means, rather than awaiting divinely initiated messianic redemption, constituted a theologically dangerous usurpation of a process traditional Jewish eschatology reserved for God's own initiative. This opposition persists today among some Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) communities and movements, most prominently Satmar Hasidism and the explicitly anti-Zionist Neturei Karta, which continue to regard the State of Israel's very existence as a religious transgression rather than a fulfillment — a genuinely important and often-overlooked data point demonstrating that opposition to Zionism has never been confined to non-Jewish or secular-left critics; some of the most theologically rigorous and long-standing opposition to Zionism as such has come from within traditional Orthodox Judaism itself, on explicitly religious rather than political grounds.

**Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Reines and the Mizrachi movement.** Religious Zionism's first major organized institutional expression was the Mizrachi movement, founded in March 1902 in Vilna by Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Reines (1839–1915), operating as an explicitly religious faction within Herzl's World Zionist Organization under the motto "The Land of Israel for the people of Israel according to the Torah of Israel." Reines's approach was notably pragmatic rather than mystical or explicitly messianic in its public justification: he argued religious Jews should support the practical, political work of Jewish national revival in Palestine as a straightforwardly positive development for Jewish physical and communal welfare, largely bracketing (rather than resolving) the harder theological question of whether this constituted or hastened messianic redemption — a deliberately narrower and more defensible religious justification than the more expansive, redemption-centered theology that would later come to dominate religious-Zionist thought through the influence of Rav Kook and especially his son.

**Rav Abraham Isaac Kook: mystical theology of secular Zionism.** Rabbi Abraham Isaac HaKohen Kook (1865–1935), who arrived in Palestine in 1904 to serve as rabbi of Jaffa and its surrounding agricultural settlements and later became the first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandate Palestine, developed a considerably more theologically ambitious and religiously novel framework than Reines's pragmatic Mizrachi approach — one that, notably, some sources reviewed for this entry stress Kook developed largely independently of, and in some tension with, organized Mizrachi politics (Kook reportedly never formally joined Mizrachi and, according to at least one source reviewed here, was for years an outspoken critic of Herzl's specifically secular political Zionism even as he came to embrace the underlying project of Jewish national return on his own distinct theological terms). Kook's central and genuinely original theological move was to interpret the secular, often explicitly anti-religious pioneers of Labor Zionism — builders of kibbutzim, speakers of revived Hebrew, participants in a movement whose dominant ideological current was avowedly socialist and secular — not as obstacles to or betrayers of authentic Jewish religious destiny, but as unwitting instruments of divine providence, participants (whether they recognized it or not) in the beginning stage (*atchalta d'geula*, "the beginning of redemption") of the messianic process itself. This was a strikingly generous and theologically creative reading of a movement whose leadership Kook otherwise had real and substantial disagreements with, and it gave religious Zionism a considerably more messianic, redemption-oriented theological register than Reines's more cautious founding framework had originally supplied.

**Zvi Yehuda Kook and the post-1967 transformation.** The elder Rav Kook's son, Rabbi Zvi Yehuda Kook (1891–1982), who led the Merkaz HaRav yeshiva his father had founded in Jerusalem, is credited across the sourced material reviewed for this entry with a decisive further transformation of religious Zionism following Israel's rapid 1967 territorial acquisitions — reframing the conquest of the West Bank, Gaza, the Sinai, and the Golan Heights not as an ordinary military and political outcome but as a further, dramatic unfolding of the messianic process his father had theorized, providing the direct theological foundation for the religious settlement movement, most prominently Gush Emunim (founded 1974, and discussed further in the Greater Israel entry, §4), that has driven much of the sustained, religiously motivated Jewish settlement of the West Bank from the 1970s onward. This entry flags — because sourced material specifically raises the point — that this represents a genuine and significant discontinuity from the more institutionally cautious, pragmatic religious Zionism of Reines and even, in important respects, of the elder Kook himself; critics within religious Zionism's own more moderate, "Reinesian" tradition (a position articulated in material reviewed for this entry, including a 2025 *Times of Israel* essay explicitly framed as recovering this older, more pragmatic and politically moderate strand against what its author characterizes as its later "messianic" eclipse) have argued the movement's later trajectory toward uncompromising settlement maximalism and, in some expressions, explicit political messianism represents a real ideological drift from — not a simple continuation of — religious Zionism's own founding framework, a genuinely internal and unresolved argument within religious Zionism itself rather than simply an external critique imposed on it.

**Institutional consolidation.** Mizrachi and its labor-oriented counterpart Hapoel HaMizrachi (founded 1921 by Rabbi Meir Bar-Ilan) merged in 1956 to form the National Religious Party, which functioned for decades as a relatively moderate coalition partner to Labor-led governments before its base shifted decisively rightward from the late 1970s onward, eventually giving rise to the explicitly settlement-focused religious-nationalist parties (including, in the entry's more recent frame of reference, figures like Bezalel Smotrich, discussed in the Greater Israel entry) that occupy a considerably more maximalist position within contemporary Israeli governing coalitions than the mid-century National Religious Party ever did.

## 7. Revisionist Zionism (Summary — See Companion Entries for Full Treatment)

Revisionist Zionism — founded by Ze'ev Jabotinsky (1880–1940) in the 1920s as an explicit "revision" of what he considered Chaim Weizmann and Labor Zionism's excessively cautious, gradualist, diplomatically deferential approach to the British Mandate authorities — is treated at length in both the Clean Break entry (§6.1, on Jabotinsky's "Iron Wall" doctrine and its influence on later Israeli and American strategic thought via Richard Perle's intellectual formation) and, especially, the Greater Israel entry (§3, tracing Jabotinsky's founding role, the movement's territorial maximalism, the 1921 Transjordan split, the Irgun's founding and its documented "both banks of the Jordan" emblem, and the movement's direct organizational descent into the modern Likud party). Readers building a fuller picture of Revisionist Zionism should consult those entries rather than have that material duplicated here; the essential summary for this entry's purposes is that Revisionism represents the third of Zionism's three great pre-state political-ideological currents (alongside Labor and religious Zionism), defined by territorial maximalism, skepticism of gradualist diplomacy in favor of demonstrated military strength, and — as both companion entries document — a documented, traceable intellectual and organizational line running from Jabotinsky through Menachem Begin's Herut party and the Irgun, into modern Likud, and (via a more American-focused, secular strand of Revisionist-adjacent thought passing through Richard Perle, and separately through the American Jewish community's own internal Revisionist-sympathetic organizations like the Jewish Institute for National Security Affairs) into the specific strategic worldview articulated in *A Clean Break* and shared, in modified form, by segments of the American neoconservative movement covered in the PNAC entry.

## 8. Christian Zionism: A Movement Largely External to Judaism, With Major Political Consequences

**Definitional note.** Christian Zionism is analytically distinct from every school covered above in one crucial respect: it is not a school of Jewish thought or a current within the Jewish Zionist movement, but a theological and political current within (primarily Protestant, and within that primarily evangelical) Christianity that supports Jewish return to and sovereignty over the Land of Israel on the basis of Christian biblical interpretation and, in its dominant modern form, Christian eschatology — belief about the events preceding the end of history and the Second Coming of Jesus Christ — rather than on the basis of Jewish national self-determination as such. This entry treats it as a distinct and important current warranting inclusion precisely because of its disproportionate, well-documented influence on the actual diplomatic and political history of the Zionist project, from the Balfour Declaration through contemporary U.S. foreign policy.

**Restorationism before Darby.** Christian belief in a future restoration of the Jewish people to Palestine, as a matter of biblical prophecy, considerably predates the specific 19th-century dispensationalist theology most closely associated with the term "Christian Zionism" today — 17th-century English Puritan thought included various restorationist currents, and the sourced material reviewed for this entry (a Liberty University academic paper specifically) makes the argument, contested by some Christian Zionism scholars, that this restorationist tradition existed independently of, and considerably predates, the specifically dispensationalist theological framework John Nelson Darby would later develop, complicating any simple story in which Darby is treated as the single originating source of all Christian pro-Zionist sentiment.

**John Nelson Darby and dispensationalism.** Darby (1800–1882), an Anglo-Irish former Church of Ireland clergyman and founder of the Plymouth Brethren movement, developed dispensationalism — a systematic theological framework dividing sacred history into a series of distinct "dispensations" or eras of God's differing modes of relating to humanity, holding that biblical prophecy assigned a distinct, ongoing, and future covenantal role to the literal, ethnic nation of Israel (as opposed to a purely spiritualized or church-superseding reading of biblical Israel, the more traditional Christian supersessionist position dispensationalism explicitly rejected) — including, crucially, a future physical return of Jews to the land of Israel as a necessary precondition for a sequence of prophesied end-times events culminating in the Second Coming. This entry flags, per the sourced material, a genuine scholarly dispute about how directly and personally politically active Darby himself was in promoting Jewish restoration to Palestine as an actual policy matter (some sources characterize him and his immediate Brethren circle as theologically committed to future restoration in principle but essentially apolitical in practice, resisting the reading of critics who treat him as a direct architect of Zionist political outcomes) versus how influential his systematized theological framework became, once popularized and somewhat adapted by later figures, in shaping a much broader current of politically engaged American evangelical pro-Zionist sentiment across the following century and a half.

**C.I. Scofield and American popularization.** Cyrus Ingerson Scofield's 1909 *Scofield Reference Bible*, incorporating extensive dispensationalist interpretive notes drawing directly and explicitly on Darby's framework, became — per the sourced material reviewed here — the single most influential vehicle for popularizing dispensationalist theology, and with it a theologically motivated pro-Zionist orientation, among American Protestant evangelicals across the 20th century, a foundational text for what would become the dominant eschatological framework within much of American evangelicalism by the mid-20th century.

**William Blackstone and direct political influence.** William Eugene Blackstone, an American evangelical dispensationalist, organized the "Blackstone Memorial" — a petition explicitly calling for Jewish resettlement of Palestine — and presented it to U.S. President Benjamin Harrison in 1891, a documented instance of organized Christian Zionist political advocacy predating Herzl's own 1896 pamphlet by five years. The petition was reportedly re-presented to President Woodrow Wilson in 1917, shortly before Wilson's administration expressed support for the Balfour Declaration; sourced material reviewed for this entry also describes Blackstone personally sending Herzl a Bible with prophetic passages relating to the Land of Israel specifically marked — a genuinely striking, if easy to overstate, documented point of direct personal contact between an early American Christian Zionist and the founder of political Zionism himself, worth noting without overreading it as establishing that Blackstone or Christian Zionist theology was somehow the true origin of Herzl's own, independently and separately motivated political project.

**The Balfour Declaration's mixed motivations.** Historians of the 1917 Balfour Declaration — Britain's foreign secretary Arthur Balfour's letter to Lord Rothschild expressing British government support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people" — have long debated the relative weight of several distinct, non-mutually-exclusive British motivations: wartime strategic calculation (courting American and Russian Jewish opinion to support the Allied war effort, and securing British postwar strategic control over Palestine against French and other rival claims); the influence of Chaim Weizmann's sustained personal diplomacy and his wartime scientific contributions to British munitions production; and — the point most directly relevant to this section — the documented personal religious sympathy for Jewish restoration held by several key British officials, including Balfour himself and Prime Minister David Lloyd George, both of whom had been raised in explicitly biblically literate, restorationist-adjacent Protestant religious environments. This entry does not adjudicate the precise relative weight of these factors — serious historians weight them differently — but flags that treating the Balfour Declaration as explicable purely through secular wartime *Realpolitik*, with no role whatsoever for genuine religious conviction among key British decision-makers, appears to go beyond what the documented historical record actually supports, per the range of sourced material reviewed here.

**Contemporary American Christian Zionism.** From the late 1970s onward — Jerry Falwell's Moral Majority, founded 1979, is frequently cited (including in sourced material reviewed for this entry, though that specific source is a polemical anti-Christian-Zionist piece whose broader framing this entry does not endorse) as a key vehicle for this development — Christian Zionism became an increasingly organized, politically consequential current within the American Republican Party's electoral coalition, with sourced material describing evangelical Christians (estimated at roughly one-third of the contemporary Republican electoral coalition) as a major, sustained institutional source of American political and diplomatic support for Israel, operating through a theological logic — biblical prophecy and the belief that Christians who "bless" Israel will themselves be blessed, a reading frequently traced to Genesis 12:3 — that is substantively different from, and analytically ought to be kept distinct from, the American Jewish community's own, considerably more religiously and politically heterogeneous relationship to Zionism and Israel, discussed further in §10 below. Organizations such as Christians United for Israel (CUFI), founded by pastor John Hagee in 2006, represent the most institutionally organized contemporary expression of this current.

## 9. Post-Zionism, Non-Zionism, and Anti-Zionism: Three Distinct Adjacent Positions Worth Disentangling

Popular discourse frequently collapses these three positions into a single undifferentiated "critical of Israel" category; they are analytically distinct and worth stating precisely:

- **Non-Zionism** describes a position of simple non-participation or indifference toward the Zionist project without active opposition to its existence — historically associated with, among others, segments of the pre-state American Reform Jewish establishment (before Reform Judaism's own later, substantial pro-Zionist realignment) and with some Haredi communities that simply do not engage with Zionism as a political category one way or the other, considering the question outside their proper religious concern.
- **Anti-Zionism** describes active opposition to Zionism as a political project or to the legitimacy of a Jewish sovereign state in Palestine/Israel, articulated from a range of genuinely distinct starting positions that should not be treated as a single coherent camp: religious anti-Zionism from within Orthodox Judaism (Satmar, Neturei Karta — see §6 above, opposing Zionism as theologically premature and religiously illegitimate); Palestinian and pan-Arab nationalist anti-Zionism (opposing it as a colonial-settler displacement of the indigenous population, discussed further in §11); and a range of secular-left, Marxist, and (as documented in the Greater Israel entry, §8) some libertarian/anti-interventionist anti-Zionist positions, each resting on distinguishable — and not always mutually compatible — theoretical foundations.
- **Post-Zionism** describes a position, associated particularly with the New Historians and post-Zionist Israeli academics discussed in §11, that does not necessarily reject the historical legitimacy or continued existence of the State of Israel but argues Israel should move beyond an ethnically or religiously particularist "Zionist" self-conception — treating Zionism as, in effect, a completed or superseded 20th-century national-liberation project rather than an ongoing organizing ideology that should continue to shape the state's institutions, immigration law (specifically the Law of Return's preferential treatment of Jewish immigrants), and self-understanding indefinitely into the future.

## 10. Zionism and American Jewish Identity: A Genuinely Contested Relationship, Not a Simple Consensus

It is worth stating directly, because popular discourse on both the pro- and anti-Israel sides of contemporary debate frequently assumes otherwise: American Jewish opinion on Zionism has never been simple, uniform, or uncontested, and treating "the Jewish community" as a monolithic pro-Zionist bloc — a framing both some pro-Israel advocacy organizations and some of their harshest critics converge on for very different rhetorical purposes — obscures a genuinely long and substantial history of internal American Jewish debate. Classical Reform Judaism's 1885 Pittsburgh Platform explicitly rejected Jewish nationhood and the prospect of a return to Palestine, describing Jews as a religious community rather than a nation-in-exile — a position American Reform Judaism would substantially, though not without continued internal dissent, reverse across the following six decades, particularly after the Holocaust and Israel's 1948 founding. The American Council for Judaism, founded in 1942 specifically in opposition to the Reform movement's Zionist turn, has continued to maintain an explicitly non-Zionist and, in some of its members' expression, anti-Zionist position within organized American Jewish religious life into the present — the institutional home, per material reviewed for this entry (see the Greater Israel entry, §8), that libertarian writer Sheldon Richman has more recently gravitated toward. This internal Jewish debate — considerably older, more textured, and less settled than a simple binary "Jewish support for Israel" framing suggests — is itself an important primary datum for understanding Zionism's actual historical and contemporary reception, distinct from (though obviously related to) the separate question of gentile political support discussed in §8 and the separate question of realist/critical foreign-policy analysis discussed in the Clean Break and Greater Israel entries.

## 11. The Historiographical Battle: "New Historians," Their Critics, and the Colonialism Debate

**The New Historians.** Beginning with Benny Morris's 1988 *Tikkun* magazine essay "The New Historiography: Israel Confronts Its Past," a cluster of Israeli historians — Morris, Avi Shlaim, Ilan Pappé, and (from journalism rather than academia) Simha Flapan — argued, drawing on Israeli state archives declassified starting in 1982 under Israel's standard 30-year rule, that the traditional Israeli national narrative of 1948 (a small, righteous, existentially outnumbered Jewish community successfully repelling coordinated Arab aggression, with Palestinian refugees having fled voluntarily or at Arab leaders' own urging rather than through Israeli policy or action) required substantial revision in light of what the archives actually documented. Morris's own archival work, especially *The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem, 1947–1949* (1988), documented specific, numerous instances of Israeli forces' direct role in Palestinian expulsion during the 1948 war — a genuinely major historiographical intervention, grounded in primary Israeli government and military archival sources rather than Arab or third-party accounts, that even most of Morris's later critics do not dispute at the level of the specific documented incidents.

**Where the New Historians diverge from each other.** It is a significant and, per the sourced material reviewed here, often under-appreciated point that the "New Historians" were never a unified school with a single interpretation, and their internal divergence has, if anything, sharpened rather than resolved over time. Ilan Pappé's *The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine* (2006) argues the 1948 expulsions reflected a coordinated Israeli "master plan" (frequently identified with "Plan Dalet," a 1948 Haganah operational plan) for the systematic removal of the Arab population — a strong, intentionalist causal claim. Benny Morris, by contrast, has explicitly and repeatedly rejected Pappé's "ethnic cleansing" framing and, in his own later work (particularly *1948: A History of the First Arab-Israeli War*, 2008), has moved toward an account emphasizing what he characterizes as a broader "jihadi" religious dimension to Arab resistance to the Jewish state — a shift Avi Shlaim, writing in a 2019 *Prospect* magazine piece reviewed for this entry, describes with evident concern, framing the contemporary debate as running "not between old and new historians, but within the ranks of the original group of new historians" themselves, with Pappé blaming Israel for Palestinian dispossession and Morris, in Shlaim's characterization, moving closer than ever before to a framework blaming Palestinians and the broader Muslim world for their own losses. This is a genuinely important point for anyone approaching this literature for the first time: "the New Historians" is not a school with a settled shared conclusion, and citing "the New Historians say X" without specifying which of Morris, Shlaim, Pappé, or Flapan is being cited, and from which period of that historian's own evolving work, risks real misrepresentation.

**The strongest documented mainstream critique: Efraim Karsh.** Historian Efraim Karsh's *Fabricating Israeli History: The 'New Historians'* (1997) and subsequent Middle East Forum writing mount the most sustained and, per the material reviewed here, most methodologically detailed critique of the New Historians as a group, arguing — regarding the specific question of numerical parity between Jewish and Arab forces in 1948 — that facts the New Historians presented as new archival discoveries had in fact been documented in Israeli sources (including Ben-Gurion's own published war diaries) for decades before the New Historians' work, and separately alleging that Morris specifically engaged in selective or inaccurate use of archival evidence regarding Israeli leadership's contemporaneous discussion of Arab "transfer" — an allegation Morris, Pappé, and Shlaim each disputed in kind in their own published responses to Karsh, producing a genuinely unresolved scholarly dispute this entry does not adjudicate, beyond noting that Karsh represents a serious, archivally engaged critical position rather than a purely polemical dismissal, and that his own critics have raised comparably serious methodological objections to his work in return.

**The settler-colonialism framing: Rashid Khalidi and his critics.** A related but analytically distinct debate concerns not what specifically happened in 1948 but how to characterize the entire Zionist project's relationship to European colonialism as a broader historical category. Rashid Khalidi — the Edward Said Professor of Modern Arab Studies at Columbia University, and himself a descendant of Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi, the Jerusalem mayor whose prescient 1899 letter warning Herzl that Palestine was "inhabited by others" opens Khalidi's own most influential book — argues in *The Hundred Years' War on Palestine: A History of Settler Colonialism and Resistance, 1917–2017* (2020) that the conflict is best understood not as a clash between two symmetrical national movements with competing claims to the same territory, but explicitly as "a colonial war waged against the indigenous population, by a variety of parties, to force them to relinquish their homeland to another people against their will" — a framework that treats the Zionist movement's dependence on, and successive alignment with, the era's dominant imperial powers (first Britain, later the United States) as decisive to understanding its actual historical character, regardless of the movement's own simultaneous and, in Khalidi's own stated formulation, genuinely sincere self-understanding as a national liberation movement. Khalidi's own language is notably precise on this specific point, and worth quoting directly given how often the settler-colonialism framing is caricatured by both supporters and critics as necessarily denying Zionism's character as a nationalist movement at all: Khalidi states explicitly that "Zionism, therefore, could be and was both a national and a colonial settler movement at one and the same time" — i.e., his argument is not that Zionism was merely a colonial imposition with no genuine national character, but that it was authentically both simultaneously, a considerably more precise and arguably more defensible claim than either pure "national liberation movement" or pure "colonial project" framings alone. Benny Morris — himself, as discussed above, no uncomplicated defender of the traditional Zionist narrative — published a sharply critical review of Khalidi's book in the *Jewish Review of Books* (2020) titled "The War on History," representing the clearest documented direct confrontation between a leading New Historian and a leading Palestinian-American historian over the settler-colonialism framing specifically; this entry has not independently reviewed Morris's specific counter-arguments in that piece in sufficient depth to characterize them fairly and flags that gap for future research (see §12).

**A more traditionalist Zionist historiographical position.** Historians and political theorists including Shlomo Avineri (whose *The Making of Modern Zionism: The Intellectual Origins of the Jewish State*, 1981, remains a standard academic intellectual history of the movement's ideological development) and Walter Laqueur (*A History of Zionism*, 1972, long considered a standard comprehensive one-volume treatment) represent a scholarly tradition generally more sympathetic to reading Zionism primarily through the lens of 19th-century European nationalism's general historical logic — treating Jewish nationalism as one instance, however historically distinctive in its circumstances, of the broader wave of ethnic and linguistic national self-determination movements that reshaped Europe's political map across the 19th and early 20th centuries — rather than primarily through the lens of colonial settlement, without necessarily denying the colonial-era diplomatic and demographic realities (British Mandate sponsorship, the movement's dependence on Western great-power support) that Khalidi's framework foregrounds. This entry treats the "nationalism versus colonialism" framing debate as a genuinely live, unresolved, and analytically serious disagreement among credentialed historians working from the same broad body of archival evidence — not a dispute where one side can be dismissed as straightforwardly propagandistic — while also noting, following Khalidi's own formulation above, that the strongest recent scholarship on both sides increasingly treats "was Zionism nationalist or colonial" as something of a false binary, with the more analytically productive question being how these two dimensions interacted and reinforced each other across the movement's actual historical development rather than which single label displaces the other entirely.
