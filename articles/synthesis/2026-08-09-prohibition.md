# The Forgotten Fuel War: Alcohol, Gasoline, and the Prohibition Question

*How America's farms almost became its gas stations — and why they didn't*

In 1925, a reporter asked Henry Ford what would power the cars of the future. Ford didn't hesitate. The fuel of tomorrow, he said, would come from "fruit like that sumac out by the road, or from apples, weeds, sawdust — almost anything." He wasn't describing gasoline. He was describing ethyl alcohol — the same molecule in a glass of whiskey — distilled from crops instead of drilled from the ground.

It's easy to hear that quote today and assume Ford was indulging in a bit of futurist whimsy, the kind of thing rich men say to reporters. He wasn't. For the better part of three decades, alcohol was a serious contender to become America's primary automobile fuel — backed by chemists, farm economists, the U.S. Department of Agriculture, and the world's most famous industrialist. It lost. Gasoline won, and a few years later, leaded gasoline won even bigger, dominating American roads for the next sixty years.

Why alcohol lost is one of the more genuinely contested corners of American industrial history — genuinely contested not because historians disagree about what happened, but because they disagree about *why*. One version of the story, popularized by biofuel advocate David Blume in his book *Alcohol Can Be a Gas!*, holds that Prohibition itself was a weapon: that John D. Rockefeller and Standard Oil bankrolled the temperance movement specifically to kill the farmer's still and, with it, ethanol's future as a fuel. That claim is worth investigating, and we will. But it turns out to be the less interesting story. The far better documented, far more damning story happened a few years later, inside the research labs and boardrooms of General Motors, Standard Oil of New Jersey, and DuPont — where the men who best understood ethanol's merits chose a more dangerous, more profitable alternative instead, and where the paper trail is unusually good, right down to a unanimous Supreme Court ruling.

## When Alcohol Was a Perfectly Normal Thing to Put in Your Engine

Before there was a gas station on every corner, there wasn't really a "normal" automobile fuel at all. Early internal combustion engines ran on gasoline, kerosene, benzene, and alcohol more or less interchangeably. Nicolaus Otto tested alcohol in his early engines; Samuel Morey ran an alcohol-and-turpentine engine decades before that. At the 1902 Paris exposition of the Automobile Club de France, an entire hall was devoted to alcohol-powered stoves, lamps, and motors, mostly imported from Germany, where a government-subsidized alcohol-fuel industry was already thriving. European governments, worried about dependence on imported petroleum, actively promoted farm-distilled alcohol as fuel.

In the United States, alcohol had one enormous problem that had nothing to do with chemistry: tax law. Since the Civil War, distilled spirits had been taxed as if they were whiskey, whether you drank them or ran your tractor on them — a gallon of alcohol fuel could cost several times the price of gasoline once the beverage tax was added. In 1906, Congress fixed this: the Free Alcohol Act let alcohol be sold tax-free for fuel and industrial use, provided it was "denatured" with something foul-tasting so it couldn't be diverted into the beverage market. It's worth noting that fourteen years *before* Prohibition began, the federal government had already moved to make fuel alcohol cheaper, not more restricted.

## Henry Ford's Farm-Grown Fuel

Into this environment came the Model T, in 1908. The popular legend of a literal dashboard switch to toggle between gasoline and alcohol has no contemporary documentary support — the owner's manual describes the carburetor's adjustment as controlling air-fuel mixture, not fuel type. But the legend is plausible precisely because the T's engine was so crude: a low 4.5:1 compression ratio, cast-iron internals impervious to alcohol's mild corrosiveness, and a hand-adjustable carburetor and spark-advance lever that let a driver compensate for whatever fuel was in the tank. Owners routinely ran Model Ts on home-distilled alcohol, at a modest cost in power (perhaps 12-13 horsepower versus 20-22 on gasoline) and fuel economy, since alcohol carries roughly a third less energy per gallon.

Ford's public advocacy for farm-grown fuel, though, was not folklore. Through the 1920s he championed "chemurgy" — industrial use of farm crops — sponsoring conferences and telling the press that alcohol from crop waste or surplus grain could ease the farm depression while fueling the nation's cars. He wasn't alone: Alexander Graham Bell made similar arguments, and, as we'll see, General Motors' own top engineer spent the early 1920s convinced alcohol was the fuel of the automotive future — for reasons that had nothing to do with sentiment about farmers.

## How Practical Was It, Really?

Strip away both the boosterism and the automatic skepticism, and the honest technical answer is: alcohol fuel was real and workable, but not a frictionless, obviously superior alternative.

**In its favor:** ethanol has a very high octane rating — meaning strong resistance to engine knock — which made it valuable both as a standalone fuel in low-compression engines and as a blending agent to boost cheap gasoline's performance in higher-compression ones. It burns cleaner, with less toxic exhaust, than the leaded gasoline that would eventually dominate. And the raw agricultural capacity existed: a 1921 estimate cited by GM's own researchers found the country's farms could produce nearly twice the gasoline-equivalent volume the era's automobile fleet demanded, using corn, sugar crops, and other biomass, without displacing food, since leftover mash could still be fed to livestock.

**Against it:** ethanol holds less energy per gallon than gasoline, is hygroscopic (absorbing water in ways that caused corrosion and phase-separation problems in fuel systems not designed for it), and was, gallon for gallon, generally costlier to produce than petroleum-derived gasoline once oil supply expanded through the 1910s and 1920s. Farm-scale distillation was mechanically simple, but doing it at gasoline's price point, at a scale that mattered nationally, was a taller order than "any farmer with a still can make fuel" suggests. Alcohol fuel programs that did scale up in this era, like Germany's, generally required active government subsidy to compete with petroleum.

That tension — technically credible, economically underdog — is the real starting condition for everything that follows.

## Enter Prohibition

David Blume's claim, at its strongest, holds that Prohibition wasn't really about temperance at all, but a Rockefeller-funded scheme to eliminate the farmer's still as competition for Standard Oil's gasoline. It deserves a real look rather than a shrug in either direction.

**Who built the Prohibition movement?** Temperance activism in America predates the automobile by nearly a century. Its most effective political engine was the Anti-Saloon League, founded in Ohio in 1893 under the ruthless, single-issue leadership of Wayne Wheeler. The League fused evangelical Protestant moral reform, Progressive-era anxieties about urban saloon culture, and a good deal of straightforward industrial self-interest: factory owners nationwide, worried about drunkenness cutting into worker productivity, were significant backers of temperance independent of any fuel motive.

**Who financed it?** John D. Rockefeller Jr. did give heavily to the Anti-Saloon League — documented contributions were around $350,000 over roughly two decades, substantial but a fraction of the League's total funding, which came overwhelmingly from churches and a broad base of small donors. Here the story gets inconvenient for the simple "oil man kills the competition" version: Rockefeller was not alone among industrialists backing the League. So did Andrew Carnegie. So, by most historical accounts including PBS's *Prohibition* documentary project, did **Henry Ford himself** — the man later cast as ethanol's great champion and Rockefeller's supposed nemesis. If Prohibition were secretly an oil-industry plot against farm alcohol, it recruited an odd co-conspirator in the country's leading alcohol-fuel evangelist. The more mundane explanation fits better: wealthy industrialists of many stripes backed temperance because they believed a sober workforce was more productive, and Rockefeller in particular was a lifelong, devoutly religious teetotaler for reasons that predated the automobile entirely.

**What did the Volstead Act actually do to fuel and industrial alcohol?** This is the most checkable part of the claim, and the law's text doesn't cooperate with the strong version of the story. The National Prohibition Act of 1919 explicitly stated its purpose included insuring "an ample supply of alcohol and promote its use in scientific research and in the development of fuel, dye, and other lawful industries." Its Title III set up a permit system specifically to keep industrial and fuel alcohol legally flowing, denatured so it couldn't be diverted to drink — continuing, not reversing, the framework the 1906 Free Alcohol Act had already established. On paper, fuel alcohol was carved out and protected.

**The weaker, more defensible hypothesis holds up better.** Even with a legal exemption, the permit system created real friction: producing, denaturing, and distributing industrial alcohol meant federal paperwork, bonding, and constant scrutiny, since the government's central preoccupation was stopping redistillation of denatured alcohol back into (sometimes fatally poisonous) moonshine. A farm-cooperative still making legal fuel alcohol now operated under the same suspicious federal gaze as a bootlegger's operation. And the timing is suggestive even if the mechanism was indirect: Prohibition ran almost exactly from 1920 to 1933, precisely the years the American automobile fleet exploded from roughly 8 million registered vehicles to over 20 million and the country's gas-station and pipeline infrastructure was permanently taking shape. An alcohol-fuel industry that might have used that decade to scale up instead spent it under a regulatory cloud — a real, if secondary, headwind, not a ban, and considerably more defensible than the conspiracy version.

That's Prohibition's honest role: a plausible drag, not a plot. The far more consequential, far better documented decision was still to come.

## What Kettering and Midgley Actually Knew About Ethanol

By the early 1920s, gasoline had a serious engineering problem: knock. As engineers raised compression ratios to get more power and efficiency from an engine, the fuel-air mixture would sometimes detonate erratically instead of burning smoothly — a sharp metallic pinging that, over time, damaged the engine. Solving knock was the key to unlocking the more powerful engines the industry wanted to build next, and it was General Motors' problem to solve: Charles Kettering, the research chief already famous for inventing the electric starter, put his star chemist Thomas Midgley Jr. on it starting around 1916.

The documentary record — reconstructed largely by environmental historian Bill Kovarik from roughly 80 linear feet of Midgley's own unprocessed research files, released decades later to what is now Kettering University — shows this was not a case of engineers overlooking an obvious alternative. Ethanol was their leading candidate for years, studied more thoroughly and enthusiastically than any other option.

In an unpublished 1921 Society of Automotive Engineers paper, Midgley described a bleak nine-year horizon for U.S. oil reserves then being projected by the U.S. Geological Survey — "a rather gloomy picture," he wrote, for the industry's future — and identified alcohol, alongside benzene from coal tar and shale oil, as the serious contenders to replace it. He and Kettering's other assistant, T.A. Boyd, judged shale oil impractical and coal-derived benzene too resource-intensive; alcohol, they wrote, "appear[ed] to be very promising," describing it as the "most direct route" for turning solar energy, via crops, into usable fuel. That July, Boyd traveled to Yale to study ethanol production from cellulose. That October, Midgley personally drove a high-compression test car from Dayton to an SAE meeting in Indianapolis running on a 30-percent alcohol blend, specifically to demonstrate the concept to fellow engineers, telling the audience — per notes later found in company archives — that "alcohol has tremendous advantages and minor disadvantages," among the advantages clean combustion and freedom from the deposits gasoline left behind.

Then, in December 1921 — two months after that demonstration — Midgley's systematic search for a "low percentage" antiknock additive isolated tetraethyl lead.

What happened next is the detail most often left out of both the corporate memoir version of this story and the simplified conspiracy version alike: Kettering and Midgley's enthusiasm for ethanol did *not* end when they found lead. It continued, in writing, for years. In May 1922 — five months *after* discovering TEL — Midgley wrote a private memo to Kettering responding to a report on alcohol production from Mexico's fermentable "century plant": "Unquestionably alcohol is the fuel of the future," he wrote, noting it could be produced there for roughly seven and a half cents a gallon. Kovarik has found similar statements in roughly a dozen internal memos from this period, and in Midgley and Boyd's own published SAE paper from June 1922 — written *after* tetraethyl lead's discovery — describing alcohol blends and leaded gasoline as parallel tracks: leaded gasoline as a transitional efficiency booster, and ethyl alcohol as the durable long-term fuel that would keep the automobile industry running no matter what happened to domestic or world oil supply.

In other words, the men who built tetraethyl lead did not believe, in the years they were building it, that ethanol was technically inferior or impractical. Quite the opposite — internally, they called it "unquestionably" the fuel of the future while simultaneously racing lead into commercial production. The gap between that private conviction and the public position GM would take a few years later — that no real alternative to lead existed — is one of the most important facts in this entire history, and it is not speculation. It is Midgley's own handwriting.

There was one real objection the GM team raised about ethanol, and it's worth taking seriously rather than dismissing: supply. Midgley calculated that replacing gasoline outright with alcohol would require roughly 60 percent of the entire U.S. corn and sugar crop, and Kettering, in a speech around 1921, put current industrial alcohol production at under 4 percent of national fuel demand, warning that scaling it to replace gasoline entirely would consume "over half of the total farm area of the United States." That is a legitimate engineering-and-agronomy concern if you assume alcohol has to *replace* gasoline outright. But it's a considerably less alarming problem if alcohol is used the way Midgley and Boyd's own research proposed using it in 1921-22 — as a 15-to-20-percent blending agent, not a total substitute. At that share, by Boyd's own published figures, only around 9 percent of the nation's grain and sugar crops would have been needed, well within the surplus American farms were producing at the time. Kovarik's assessment, working from GM's own numbers, is that the company's public framing of the "supply problem" consistently assumed the more extreme, all-or-nothing scenario — a framing that made alcohol look far less feasible than Kettering and Midgley's own internal blending research actually supported.

## The Patents That Decided the Outcome

If ethanol wasn't rejected on engineering grounds, why was it rejected? The answer sits in patent filings, corporate ownership charts, and — eventually — a unanimous U.S. Supreme Court opinion, and it is about as close to a documented, non-speculative answer as this kind of question ever gets.

Ethanol is a naturally occurring compound. Anyone with a still, agricultural feedstock, and (after 1920) a federal fuel-alcohol permit could legally make it. No company could patent the molecule itself or meaningfully corner the market on producing it — the "farmer with a still" was, quite literally, a competitor GM could never buy out, license, or exclude. Tetraethyl lead was different in a way that mattered enormously to a corporation's balance sheet: it was a novel synthetic compound, and every part of bringing it to market could be, and was, patented.

The paper trail here is unusually solid. General Motors, through Midgley's work, secured what court records later describe as the "use patent" — covering tetraethyl lead's application as a gasoline antiknock additive and the resulting fuel mixture — with applications filed in 1922 and patents ultimately granted between 1926 and 1930 (U.S. Patents 1,573,846; 1,592,954; 1,668,022; and 1,787,419). But GM's own manufacturing process, developed with du Pont using bromine chemistry, was slow and struggled to keep pace with demand; internal correspondence has Kettering complaining in June 1924 that "the whole program is prejudiced" by production delays. Standard Oil of New Jersey's chemists, working independently under Charles Kraus, developed and patented a faster, cheaper manufacturing process using ethyl chloride instead of bromine, assigned to Standard Oil Development Company — meaning Standard held patents GM needed, and GM held patents Standard needed. Neither company could commercialize tetraethyl lead alone without the other's patent rights. That mutual dependency — not sentiment, not a handshake, but literally incompatible patent estates that only made sense combined — is why, in August 1924, General Motors and Standard Oil of New Jersey formed the Ethyl Gasoline Corporation as equal 50-50 partners, pooling their patents into a single vehicle neither could route around.

There was a third, quieter beneficiary. At the time Ethyl Gasoline Corporation was formed, DuPont owned roughly 38 percent of General Motors' stock — making the "two-company" joint venture, in the ownership sense that mattered to shareholders, closer to a three-way arrangement from the start. DuPont was also brought in directly as the manufacturer, since neither GM nor Standard Oil had chemical-plant operating experience; DuPont ran the actual tetraethyl lead production lines under contract, at least until its own manufacturing know-how let it produce independently after the mid-1920s.

The financial logic behind all this was not subtle, and GM did the math itself. In 1923, American gasoline sales ran around six billion gallons a year. If a 20-percent share of that market used a lead-treated blend — a plausible target given the additive's effectiveness at a few grams per gallon — and Ethyl earned even a modest three-cent margin per gallon of *fluid*, Kovarik's reconstruction of GM's own internal figures puts the annual take at roughly $36 million, an enormous sum for a company that had been cash-strapped just a few years earlier. Ethanol, freely producible by any distillery or farm cooperative, offered no comparable mechanism for capturing that value. Alcohol might have been, in Midgley's own words, the fuel of the future — but it was profit-free for General Motors, a description that, whatever else it says about the decision, is not seriously in dispute.

Ethyl Gasoline Corporation's actual business model, once formed, made the control angle explicit rather than incidental. The corporation charged no royalty at all on its patents to the oil refiners who wanted to sell "Ethyl" gasoline — its profit came entirely from selling refiners the tetraethyl lead fluid itself, at a fixed price, under a licensing system requiring nearly all of them to use the "Ethyl" trademark (Standard Oil, marketing under "Esso," was the sole exception), and to sell only to jobbers Ethyl itself had approved. By the late 1930s, roughly 11,000 of the country's approximately 12,000 gasoline jobbers held an Ethyl license, and the company could revoke that license — cutting off a jobber's supply entirely — at will. This was not a passive patent sitting in a drawer; it was an active mechanism for shaping who could sell gasoline in America and on what terms.

That mechanism eventually reached the U.S. Supreme Court. In *Ethyl Gasoline Corp. v. United States*, 309 U.S. 436 (1940), the government argued that Ethyl's licensing scheme — patents on the additive being used to dictate resale terms and control access to the entire national jobber market — exceeded what patent law permitted and violated federal antitrust law. The Court agreed, unanimously among the justices participating, finding that Ethyl had used its patents to "regiment" the industry: excluding disfavored jobbers from a "nationwide market for lead-treated motor fuel" and using one patent monopoly to prop up commercial control that reached well beyond what the patents themselves covered. This is not a historian's inference or a later reinterpretation. It is a final, binding legal finding, made by the nation's highest court, that the corporate structure built around tetraethyl lead functioned — in the Court's own words — as an instrument for controlling a national fuel market. Whatever else can be debated about motive back in 1921, there is no debating what the machine Kettering and Midgley's patents ultimately built was later proven, in court, to actually do.

## What the Public Was Told, Versus What the File Showed

The gap between GM's private research record and its public position widened further after October 1924, when a catastrophic malfunction at Standard Oil's Bayway, New Jersey refinery killed workers outright and caused hallucinations and insanity among dozens more within days — the toll from that plant's short operating history eventually reaching at least 17 deaths. Several states and New York City banned leaded gasoline in the panic that followed, and GM and Standard came close to abandoning the product.

They didn't. Facing a May 1925 U.S. Public Health Service conference convened specifically to weigh a national ban, Kettering and Midgley told regulators and the press that no practical alternative to lead existed. That claim runs directly against their own memos from three years earlier. It also ran against events happening in the very same window: GM was simultaneously negotiating with Germany's I.G. Farben over a completely different non-lead antiknock compound, and Kettering himself, in August 1925, publicly unveiled "Synthol" — an alcohol-and-benzene blend he claimed could double gas mileage — a demonstration Kovarik reads less as a genuine product launch than as leverage in an ongoing supply negotiation, since Synthol never reached the market. News reports ahead of the Public Health Service conference had said it would run several days specifically to weigh alternatives to TEL, a plan corroborated by the Surgeon General's own opening remarks — yet the conference concluded within a single day, and tetraethyl lead stayed on the market. A Commerce Department report on alternative antiknock additives, mostly ethyl alcohol blends already in routine use across two dozen other industrial nations, had been prepared just days earlier and was not released before the conference ended.

Tetraethyl lead remained standard in the overwhelming majority of American gasoline for the next six decades, phased out beginning only in the 1970s and not fully banned from on-road U.S. fuel until 1996 — after its role in widespread environmental lead contamination and childhood neurological harm had become undeniable.

## So: Cost, Engineering, Infrastructure, or Control?

Untangling how much of this outcome to attribute to each factor is possible, because the record on this particular decision is unusually complete.

**Engineering:** not the deciding factor, and the evidence runs the other way. GM's own chief engineers spent years researching, publicly demonstrating, and privately endorsing ethanol as a superior long-term fuel, in writing, both before and after discovering tetraethyl lead. The 1930s-era public claims from petroleum trade groups that alcohol blends were simply too "inferior" or "unstable" to be practical arrived years after GM's own engineers had demonstrated the opposite, and read, in context, like an industry defending a market position rather than reporting a neutral technical verdict.

**Cost and supply, as an honest constraint:** partially real. Alcohol was genuinely costlier per gallon of energy delivered than gasoline at 1920s prices, and full substitution for gasoline nationwide genuinely would have strained agricultural capacity, as Midgley calculated. But GM's own published blending research undercuts the severity of that objection — a 15-to-20-percent blend, the actual proposal on the table, required a small fraction of the crop acreage the "replace all gasoline" framing implied, and surplus grain supply at the time suggests the ceiling was higher than GM's public statements let on.

**Petroleum infrastructure:** a real, compounding advantage for gasoline, though a secondary one to the patent story. Standard Oil already owned the refineries, pipelines, and a growing national network of jobbers and stations; converting any of that to handle a corrosive, water-absorbing fuel would have taken capital investment gasoline itself never required.

**Patents and corporate control: the decisive factor, and the one with the clearest paper trail of all.** Ethanol could not be patented, licensed, or monopolized; tetraethyl lead could be, and was, methodically — through complementary "use" and "manufacturing" patents held by two different companies, a joint venture built specifically to pool them, a hidden third beneficiary in DuPont's GM stake, a licensing system that controlled 11,000 of the nation's 12,000 gasoline jobbers, and a business model built entirely around selling a proprietary fluid rather than collecting a royalty on a widely producible commodity. This is the one piece of the story that didn't stay a private corporate calculation — it became, sixteen years later, a unanimous Supreme Court finding that the whole structure functioned as market control.

## The Sharpened Conclusion

Set the two halves of this history side by side, and they don't carry equal evidentiary weight, and shouldn't be treated as if they did.

The claim that Prohibition was secretly engineered to destroy ethanol as a fuel is, on the documentary record, the weaker story. It relies on a donor list that inconveniently includes ethanol's most famous champion as a funder of the very movement supposedly built to destroy his cause, and on a law whose actual text protected fuel alcohol rather than banning it. What's left of the claim after that scrutiny is real but modest: Prohibition-era bureaucracy and suspicion around anything involving a still made scaling up small, farmer-run alcohol production harder during the exact years America's fuel infrastructure was being locked in. A headwind, not a conspiracy.

The tetraethyl lead decision is the opposite case: a story that survives, and even strengthens under, close scrutiny. General Motors' own engineers spent years telling each other, on paper, that ethanol was "unquestionably" the fuel of the future — both before and after they discovered a patentable alternative. They then built an entire corporate structure — two interlocking patent estates, a jointly owned holding company, a hidden third shareholder, a nationwide licensing system controlling access to fuel distribution itself — around the one option that let three of America's largest companies own a piece of every gallon sold, while publicly insisting no other option existed. And when the government finally examined that structure in open court, the United States Supreme Court did not need historians' speculation to reach a verdict: it ruled, unanimously, that this was exactly what it looked like.

Gasoline, and for six decades leaded gasoline specifically, won America's first automobile-fuel competition not because it was the better fuel — its own inventors said otherwise, in writing, at the time — but because it was the fuel three companies could own, license, and control, while ethanol, freely available to anyone with a still and a crop, never could be.
