# The Case for Prejudice

What if trying to get rid of all your prejudices actually makes you *less* open-minded?

That's the question Allan Bloom put at the center of *The Closing of the American Mind* in 1987, and it set off a national debate. I don't agree with everything in the book. But I keep coming back to it, and every time I do, I find something I missed the first time.

Bloom wasn't against openness. His complaint was that we'd confused it with something else: the idea that every way of life is equally fine as long as it doesn't hurt anyone, that truth is relative, and that calling one way of life better than another makes you dangerously intolerant. Somewhere along the way, he argued, we stopped talking about *good* and *bad* and started talking about *values*.

And here's the trouble. If good and bad are just personal values, why bother asking which way of life is actually better? There's nothing left to find out.

That's the paradox. The attitude that was supposed to open our minds ended up closing them. And Bloom's answer was, of all things, a case for prejudice.

---

## Two Kinds of Openness

Bloom spent his career teaching university students, and he noticed something about almost all of them. They believed — or said they believed — that truth is relative. They didn't argue for it. It was simply assumed.

Why? Because they'd been taught to fear one thing above all: intolerance. The true believer was the real danger. So openness became the single virtue their education was built to instill.

But Bloom thought there were two kinds of openness, and we'd chosen the wrong one.

**The first kind is really indifference.** It says every view is as good as any other, so there's nothing important to find. Once you accept that, all that's left is to be whatever you like. It can look humble, even tolerant, but it gives up before the search begins. And a mind that isn't searching for anything tends to drift toward whatever is popular or powerful at the moment.

**The second kind says:** there are many views out there, so let's examine them and find out which are true. This kind feeds the desire to know. The first kind stunts it.

This is why Bloom said we *need* prejudices. He isn't defending bigotry. He means something broader — a belief you hold before you've examined it. Strong prejudices, he writes, are visions about the way things are, *divinations of the order of the whole of things*. They're early guesses about how the world fits together.

They might well be wrong. But the road to knowledge runs through wrong opinions, because a mind with no prejudices at the outset is simply empty.

Notice what he *doesn't* say. He isn't claiming prejudices are true. He's saying you can only reach the truth by taking beliefs seriously enough to discover where they fail. You have to have the experience of really believing something before you can feel the thrill of being liberated from it.

---

## What Socrates Knew

Bloom puts it sharply: only Socrates, after a lifetime of unceasing labor, knew that he was ignorant. Now every high school student knows it. How did it become so easy?

That's the key distinction.

Socrates's ignorance was the *result* of a lifetime spent testing every answer he could find and watching each one fail. Along the way he learned an enormous amount — which answers didn't work, that some were better than others, how hard these questions really are, and where the limits of his own mind lay. When he said he was ignorant, he was reporting the end of a long search.

Now take the student who says he's ignorant without ever having known or believed anything. He *sounds* like Socrates, but he's saying something entirely different. His mind is empty because nothing ever got in.

Bloom's worry is that we've taught a generation to skip to the end. We've handed them Socrates's conclusion without Socrates's labor. And a conclusion you haven't earned is, ironically, a prejudice of its own — a belief held on authority because someone told you.

Which means the person who is sure they have no prejudices may be the most prejudiced of all.

---

## The Man Without Prejudices

Once you see the difference between earned ignorance and empty ignorance, Bloom's next worry comes into focus: what kind of person does this produce?

He points to a character from Flaubert's *Madame Bovary* — the pharmacist Homais — as the ultimate man without prejudices. Homais is the successful modern type, forever talking about rationality and progress, full of empty platitudes. He's a warning about what people become when they stop asking how to live.

If that sounds familiar, it may be because it echoes Nietzsche's *last man*. Bloom was deeply influenced by Nietzsche and returns to the last man often. He believed he was seeing more and more people like him.

And he thought it started with things that seem small — like how we stopped reading old books.

Nobody believes anymore that the old books contain the truth, he says, so at best they've become "culture." Tocqueville saw a version of this already: once tradition is recognized *as* tradition, it turns into mere information — something you pay lip service to in the hope of edifying the kids.

For Americans, Bloom says, the Bible used to be the common book that gave access to the seriousness of books. That's no longer the case. He isn't arguing that the Bible holds the ultimate truth. His point is that without a book of similar gravity to replace it, the mind stays unfurnished. There's nothing substantial in there.

He asks us to picture the typical student walking through an art museum. Without the stories of the Bible or of Greek and Roman antiquity, Raphael and Rembrandt have nothing to say to him. All he sees are colors and forms, like modern art. Like almost everything else in his spiritual life, the paintings are abstract.

What's lost isn't just tradition. When the voices of a civilization, elaborated over millennia, fall silent, something more basic disappears with them: the sense that the world has a depth worth searching for.

Without great literature, Bloom says, you also lose the fine art of comparison — the ability to recognize and finely distinguish between human types. Great characters sharpen our vision. It takes a complex set of experiences to look at someone and say, *he's a Scrooge type.* Without them, students have only pop psychology and mass media to tell them what people are like.

The result? People become more alike for want of knowing they could be otherwise. Bloom has a cutting line about this: what poor substitutes for real diversity are wild rainbows of dyed hair and other external differences that tell the observer nothing about what's inside.

---

## Narrower and Flatter Souls

The outcome, in Bloom's words, is souls that are narrower and flatter.

*Narrower*, because they lack any real basis for discontent with the present, or any awareness that there are alternatives to it. The longing for the beyond has faded.

*Flatter*, because the refinement of the mind's eye — the thing that lets you see delicate distinctions among people, the thing that constitutes real taste — is impossible without literature and the grand style. People struggle to tell the sublime from trash, or insight from propaganda.

That, for Bloom, is what education was *for*.

One of the most flattering things that ever happened to him as a teacher was a postcard from a former student on his first trip to Italy. The student wrote that Bloom wasn't a professor of political philosophy at all, but a travel agent. Bloom said that was exactly his intention. The student felt he'd been *prepared to see*.

But being prepared to see means knowing what to look for — what is high in human beings and what is low.

---

## Heroes and Evil

When Bloom asked his students two simple questions, he found they couldn't see either one.

**First, he asked who their heroes were.** Mostly he got silence. Why have heroes? You should just be yourself.

Bloom traces this distrust of heroes to a perversion of democracy — one that denies anyone is truly great, so no one has to suffer unpleasant comparisons. But liberation from the heroic leaves students with no defense against conforming to whatever the current role models happen to be. They no longer have any image of a perfect soul, so they don't long to have one. They don't even imagine such a thing exists.

**Second, he asked who was evil.** The answer came instantly: Hitler. And then nothing. They couldn't think of anyone else.

They have no idea of evil, Bloom says. They doubt it exists. Hitler has become an abstraction, an item filling an empty category. They live in a world where terrible things are done, where they see brutal crime in the streets, and yet they turn aside. There is no inferno in this comedy.

So the common student view lacks awareness of both the depths and the heights — and therefore lacks gravity.

---

## The Language of Values

Bloom thought you could hear this loss in the way we talk.

The respectable nobility of man, he says, is no longer found in the quest for the good life, but in creating your own *lifestyle*. A person with a lifestyle competes with no one and is inferior to no one. Once lifestyles are granted rights, defending them becomes a moral cause. But what actually happens inside a given lifestyle — whether it ennobles or debases — makes no difference.

Underneath this, Bloom sees a change in the soul itself. People no longer believe in a natural hierarchy of souls. Philosophy once tried to tame and perfect desire through virtue. Now it turns to discovering what your desire *is*, and living by it. Desire used to be the questionable, even dangerous, part of us. Now it's an oracle we consult. You can see it in our entertainment — all the shows about people going on wellness retreats to "find themselves," as though that's the key to living well.

Here's a way to make Bloom's point concrete. Next time you're about to say:

- **"values"** — try saying *"my opinion"*
- **"lifestyle"** — try *"living exactly as I please"*
- **"ideology"** — try *"my prejudices"*

Notice how much worse they sound.

That's the effect Bloom is pointing to. Words like *values* and *lifestyle* let you sound serious and high-minded while quietly giving up the idea that anything is actually true or better. Values still feel worth defending, even fighting over. *My opinion* doesn't. We need reasons for what we do, and this vocabulary supplies them without making us think.

Bloom turns the same lens outward, to how we treat other cultures, and finds another paradox. One technique for opening students up, he notes, is to require a course on a non-Western culture — the point being to make them recognize there are many ways of thinking and that Western ways aren't better. These requirements are part of an effort to build a world community and train its ideal member: the person without prejudice.

But, Bloom observes, a very great narrowness is not incompatible with the health of a people, whereas with great openness it's hard to avoid decomposition. Our openness is unusual. And it doesn't actually make us curious about others. If every way of life is just a value, there's nothing real to learn from anyone beyond the superficial. What we call learning from other cultures is often just condescension — what he calls the Peace Corps mentality, a secularized version of doing good.

---

## The University

Why should any of this matter if you haven't been to university recently, or ever?

Because what happens at universities doesn't stay there. The people who pass through them go on to work in newsrooms, companies, and governments, and they bring those ideas with them. Look at what happened to Bret Weinstein at Evergreen State College and how that insanity spilled into the rest of society.

Bloom saw early versions of this at Cornell, where he taught. In 1969, armed student protesters occupied a building. Afterward, faculty were pressured to change course content and standards to eliminate elitism, racism, and sexism *as perceived by students*.

What bothered Bloom most was how the university responded: it simply gave in. For Bloom, a university exists to protect the free pursuit of truth, even against popular pressure. When the faculty yielded under threat, it showed they'd stopped believing in that purpose.

Cornell also surrendered by dropping requirements deemed too hard or too boring. Bloom understood that some requirements bored students, but he still wouldn't give them up. To him they were a threadbare reminiscence of the unity of knowledge — an obstinate little hint that there are some things you must know to be educated.

He thought it was childish to call imposing a point of view on students "authoritarian." If that's the objection, why have a university at all?

---

## What's Left

So what does it all add up to?

Bloom makes some strong claims, and I don't agree with all of them. Maybe relativism isn't the main culprit. When people say "truth is relative" or "it's all relative," maybe they're just being polite, or avoiding conflict — not really staking out a philosophical position.

He also generalizes, and perhaps idealizes the past. He writes as though earlier generations searched seriously for how to live. Most people in any era probably didn't. So it's hard to say how much was really lost.

Still, his argument may hold up better when applied to certain elites — the people who are *supposed* to be educated to ask these questions.

Here's what I took away. Bloom is a cultural elitist, and I mean that in the best possible sense. He's against vulgarity, sophistry, and low taste. He's a champion of excellence in an age he saw as given over to cultural leveling and moral and intellectual relativism. He defends a hierarchy of quality in art, music, books, and thought. And he saw our culture democratizing in the wrong direction — not by lifting ordinary people up to high culture, but by making high culture seem irrelevant or repressive while elevating sludge.

There's a passage where Bloom admits that very few people will spend their lives trying to think for themselves about these things. But liberal education exists especially for those few. Without them, he says, no society can be called civilized, no matter how rich or technologically advanced.

That's why he called the book a meditation on the state of our souls. And it's why I value it so much. It asks us to think about what's worth wanting, admiring, and becoming — and whether we're even still asking.

So I'll leave you with the question at the heart of it:

**Is a mind with no prejudices more open — or does it just have less to work with?**
