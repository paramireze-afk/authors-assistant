# Thimerosal

Thimerosal is a mercury-based preservative formerly used in many childhood vaccines (largely phased out of the U.S. childhood schedule by 2001, though it remains in some multi-dose flu vaccine vials). It is the substance at the center of [[Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]]'s pivot from environmental litigation to public-health advocacy, and functions as the connective tissue between his mercury-pollution work (coal-plant emissions litigation) and his later vaccine-safety career.

## Kennedy's argument

- Mercury is a known neurotoxin (established, uncontested science); Kennedy argues that thimerosal exposure through the childhood vaccine schedule of the 1990s contributed to a rise in autism and other neurodevelopmental disorders.
- He argues that federal health agencies, aware of internal data suggestive of harm (notably discussions at the [[Simpsonwood Conference]], a 2000 CDC-convened meeting reviewing early [[Vaccine Safety Datalink]] findings), failed to adequately warn the public or acted to minimize the finding's visibility.
- This argument was first made publicly in "Deadly Immunity" (2005, *Rolling Stone*/*Salon*) and later expanded in the edited volume *[[Thimerosal: Let the Science Speak]]* (2014).

## Key documents and figures

- **[[Simpsonwood Conference]] transcript (2000)** — the central primary document Kennedy cites; a closed-door CDC meeting discussing early, statistically noisy findings from the [[Verstraeten Study]] on thimerosal and neurodevelopmental outcomes.
- **[[Thomas Verstraeten]]** — CDC epidemiologist whose early analysis showed a possible signal that did not hold up in later, larger analyses; Kennedy treats the initial signal as more significant than subsequent agency conclusions did.
- **[[Boyd Haley]]**, **[[Mark Geier]]**, **[[David Geier]]** — researchers Kennedy cites as providing supporting toxicological and epidemiological evidence.
- **[[Julie Gerberding]]** — CDC director during the period of Kennedy's most active thimerosal advocacy.

## Scientific consensus

Following the removal of thimerosal from the vast majority of childhood vaccines starting in 1999–2001, autism prevalence continued to rise in subsequent birth cohorts that had no thimerosal exposure — a finding cited by public-health researchers as strong evidence against a causal link. Multiple large epidemiological studies across several countries have not found a causal association between thimerosal exposure and autism. This is among the most extensively studied and most consistently rejected of Kennedy's causal claims.
