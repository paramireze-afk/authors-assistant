# The Project for the New American Century (PNAC)

> **Entry type:** Institutional/historical-political reference
> **Core subject:** A 1997–2006 Washington think tank that translated a specific strand of post-Cold War conservative foreign-policy thinking into public advocacy, staffed much of the George W. Bush administration's national-security apparatus, and became the most-cited case study of how a small advocacy shop can shape — or appear to shape — the policy of a superpower.
> **Related entry:** See `clean-break-1996.md` for the companion 1996 Israeli policy paper that shared several authors, arguments, and personnel with PNAC. This entry cross-references that one rather than repeating its content; read them together for the fuller picture of 1990s hawkish foreign-policy thought moving into the Bush administration.
> **Status:** Foundation entry — anchors future notes on the Wolfowitz Doctrine, the Iraq Liberation Act, the Bush Doctrine, the Office of Special Plans, and general questions about how think tanks convert ideas into government policy.

PNAC was never a large organization — accounts describe a small permanent staff working out of modest Washington offices — and its power, to the extent it had any, came from something other than institutional size: the density of its signatory lists and the number of those signatories who went on to hold senior national-security posts. Political scientist Inderjeet Parmar's count is often cited: PNAC's roughly three dozen key figures held some 115 distinct points of connection to the U.S. state apparatus — 27 to the Department of Defense, 13 to the State Department, 12 to the White House, 10 to the National Security Council, and 23 to Congress. That density, more than any budget line, is what later observers mean when they call PNAC influential.

## 2. Origins: From a *Foreign Affairs* Article to a Think Tank

PNAC's intellectual starting point predates its 1997 founding by a year. In mid-1996, William Kristol and Robert Kagan co-authored "Toward a Neo-Reaganite Foreign Policy" in *Foreign Affairs*, arguing that American conservatives had gone "adrift" on foreign policy after the Cold War, lacking a "compelling vision," and proposing instead a stance of what they explicitly called "benevolent global hegemony." Kristol — son of Irving Kristol, one of neoconservatism's founding intellectuals, and former chief of staff to Vice President Dan Quayle — and Kagan, a historian and former State Department official, believed the Republican Party needed a sharper alternative to what they saw as Bill Clinton's foreign-policy drift, and also needed to marginalize a competing current within their own coalition: post-Cold War isolationism and traditional realism, both of which had real constituencies on the American right in the mid-1990s (Patrick Buchanan's 1992 and 1996 primary campaigns being the most visible expression).

In June 1997, Kristol and Kagan formalized this project as PNAC, releasing a "Statement of Principles" that echoed the *Foreign Affairs* article nearly line for line. The statement diagnosed a specific problem — that conservatives "have not confidently advanced a strategic vision of America's role in the world," had "allowed differences over tactics to obscure potential agreement on strategic objectives," and had failed to "fight for a defense budget that would maintain American security." Its remedy rested on four pillars: substantially increased defense spending; strengthened ties to democratic allies combined with a willingness to "challenge regimes hostile to our interests and values"; the promotion of "political and economic freedom abroad"; and acceptance of "responsibility for America's unique role in preserving and extending an international order friendly to our security, our prosperity, and our principles." It closed by invoking Ronald Reagan explicitly, calling for a "Reaganite policy of military strength and moral clarity," and warned — in a phrase later authors would return to repeatedly — that "the history of the 20th century should have taught us that it is important to shape circumstances before crises emerge, and to meet threats before they become dire."

**On the Statement of Principles' 25 signatories.** The list is worth naming in full because so much of PNAC's later reputation rests on who was on it: Elliott Abrams, Gary Bauer, William J. Bennett, Jeb Bush, Dick Cheney, Eliot A. Cohen, Midge Decter, Paula Dobriansky, Steve Forbes, Aaron Friedberg, Francis Fukuyama, Frank Gaffney, Fred Iklé, Donald Kagan, Zalmay Khalilzad, I. Lewis "Scooter" Libby, Norman Podhoretz, Dan Quayle, Peter W. Rodman, Donald Rumsfeld, Stephen P. Rosen, Henry S. Rowen, Vin Weber, George Weigel, and Paul Wolfowitz — alongside Kristol and Kagan themselves. Ten of the 25 went on to serve in the George W. Bush administration, including Cheney as vice president, Rumsfeld as secretary of defense, and Wolfowitz as deputy secretary of defense. The list mixed genuine neoconservative intellectuals (Podhoretz, Decter, Fukuyama, Cohen) with religious-right figures (Bauer, Weigel), mainstream Republican establishment figures (Forbes, Jeb Bush), and hawkish nationalists with no particular neoconservative pedigree (Rumsfeld, Cheney, Rodman) — a coalition-building breadth that historian Maria Ryan has argued was itself part of PNAC's institutional strategy: not to represent a narrow faction, but to build the broadest possible tent for an assertive, primacy-oriented foreign policy.

**Notably absent from the founding statement:** Richard Perle, Douglas Feith, and David Wurmser — the three figures most central to *A Clean Break* the year before — did not sign PNAC's original Statement of Principles, though Perle became deeply involved in PNAC's later work (he is listed as a *Rebuilding America's Defenses* project participant) and all three moved in the same institutional orbit as PNAC's leadership throughout the late 1990s and 2000s. This is a genuinely important distinction for anyone tracing the two documents' relationship: PNAC and Clean Break were parallel, overlapping, mutually reinforcing projects with shared personnel and shared assumptions, not the same project under two names. See the Clean Break entry for the fuller story of that document's authorship.

## 3. The Deeper Lineage: The 1992 Defense Planning Guidance

PNAC did not invent the ideas in its Statement of Principles from scratch. Five years earlier, in the final months of the George H.W. Bush administration, then–Under Secretary of Defense for Policy Paul Wolfowitz — working for Secretary of Defense Dick Cheney, with I. Lewis Libby and Zalmay Khalilzad as principal co-drafters — produced a classified planning document known as the Defense Planning Guidance (DPG) for fiscal years 1994–99. Leaked to the *New York Times* in March 1992, the draft argued the United States' central post-Cold War objective should be preventing "the re-emergence of a new rival" anywhere in the world — not merely deterring the Soviet Union's successor states, but foreclosing the emergence of *any* peer competitor, friendly or hostile, and asserted the U.S. should be prepared to act preemptively and, if necessary, unilaterally to protect vital interests, explicitly including "access to vital raw materials, primarily Persian Gulf oil."

The leaked draft caused an immediate public and diplomatic backlash — European allies read it as an assertion of permanent American tutelage, and even within the elder Bush's own administration, the State Department under James Baker pushed back hard. The White House ordered a substantially softened rewrite before the document was finalized, and the episode contributed to a general sense (accurate, per most historians) that unrestrained "primacy" doctrine was politically unsellable in 1992.

What matters for tracing PNAC's lineage is what happened to the *idea*, not the sanitized final document: the original draft's core argument — permanent, unchallenged American military primacy, maintained if necessary through preemption — did not disappear. It migrated into private life with its authors (Wolfowitz, Libby, Khalilzad all went on to other posts, then out of government during the Clinton years, then back in under Bush II) and resurfaced, essentially unmodified in substance though softened in packaging, in PNAC's 1997 Statement of Principles and far more explicitly in *Rebuilding America's Defenses* three years later. This is the single clearest documented case of an idea moving from a classified Pentagon planning document, through a public backlash that killed its official form, into a private-sector think tank, and back into government a decade later with many of the same named authors — the exact "think tank as holding pattern for policy ideas between administrations" dynamic this entry is partly designed to illustrate. **See §7 for how this pattern repeats with the "new Pearl Harbor" line and again, differently, with the Clean Break/Office of Special Plans arc.**

## 4. The Iraq Campaign: 1998

PNAC's most consequential and most-cited public act, prior to *Rebuilding America's Defenses*, was a sustained campaign for regime change in Iraq conducted throughout 1998, three years before Bush took office and five years before the invasion.

**The January 26, 1998 open letter to President Clinton.** Signed by Elliott Abrams, Richard Perle, Paul Wolfowitz, R. James Woolsey (Clinton's own former CIA director), Donald Rumsfeld, Robert Zoellick, John Bolton, and others, the letter argued the existing UN-sanctions-and-inspections containment regime was failing, that the U.S. could "no longer depend on our partners in the Gulf War to continue to uphold the sanctions or to punish Saddam when he blocks or evades UN inspections," and that American policy "cannot continue to be crippled by a misguided insistence on unanimity in the UN Security Council." The letter asserted the U.S. already possessed sufficient legal authority under existing UN resolutions "to take the necessary steps, including military steps, to protect our vital interests in the Gulf" — i.e., it called for unilateral military action to remove Saddam Hussein, in January 1998, nearly six years before it happened.

**Newspaper advocacy.** In parallel, Kristol and Kagan published op-eds pressing the same argument in mainstream outlets — "Bombing Iraq Isn't Enough" in the *New York Times* (January 30, 1998) and "A 'Great Victory' for Iraq" in the *Washington Post* (February 26, 1998) — arguing that limited airstrikes (what would become Operation Desert Fox that December) fell far short of what the situation required.

**The May 1998 letter to Gingrich and Lott.** A follow-up letter to the Republican congressional leadership pressed the same argument directly at the legislative branch, urging Congress to act where the letter's authors judged the executive branch too cautious.

**The Iraq Liberation Act of 1998.** Congress responded that October, passing the Iraq Liberation Act (H.R. 4655) with broad bipartisan support; Clinton signed it into law on October 31, 1998. The Act made regime change in Iraq the stated, official (though non-binding, and unfunded for military action) policy of the United States — a milestone frequently cited by later critics as proof that "regime change was already U.S. law" well before the Bush administration or the September 11 attacks, though it is worth being precise that the Act itself authorized non-military assistance to Iraqi opposition groups, not an invasion, and its passage under a Democratic president working with a Republican Congress reflected a genuinely broad bipartisan consensus at the time — not solely the product of PNAC's advocacy, even if PNAC had pushed hard for exactly this outcome.

**January 1999: a critical memo on containment's failure.** PNAC circulated an internal memo criticizing December 1998's Operation Desert Fox bombing campaign as ineffective, questioning whether the Iraqi opposition the U.S. was now backing under the Iraq Liberation Act could actually accomplish anything, and dismissing "containment" as an illusion — signaling PNAC's leadership already regarded regime change, not containment, as the only serious option nearly three years before 9/11 and Bush's inauguration made the question live at the highest levels of government.

## 5. *Rebuilding America's Defenses* (September 2000)

PNAC's single most-cited and most-controversial document is the 90-page report *Rebuilding America's Defenses: Strategies, Forces, and Resources For a New Century*, released in September 2000 — before the contested 2000 election, before 9/11, and before anyone knew who the next president would be. Its primary author was Thomas Donnelly (who later transitioned and now publishes as Giselle Donnelly); Donald Kagan (Robert Kagan's father, a Yale classicist) and Gary Schmitt served as project chairmen. The report lists 27 additional participants who contributed papers or attended project meetings, of whom six subsequently held senior Bush-administration defense and foreign-policy posts — a number smaller than sometimes implied by the "PNAC wrote the Bush foreign policy" framing, but not nothing.

**Core argument.** The report claimed the preceding post-Cold War decade had provided "the geopolitical framework for widespread economic growth" and the global spread of "American principles of liberty and democracy," but warned this "global Pax Americana will not preserve itself." Current defense spending, it argued, was inadequate to the task of maintaining it, forcing policymakers into a series of "false economies" — shortchanging future readiness to fund present operations, choosing between force presence in Europe or Asia rather than affording both, and so on.

**Four core missions proposed for U.S. forces:** defense of the American homeland; the capacity to fight and win "multiple, simultaneous major theater wars"; performance of "constabulary" duties to shape the security environment in key regions on an ongoing basis (i.e., a large permanent global military footprint even absent active war); and transformation of the force to exploit what the report called the "revolution in military affairs" (precision weapons, networked command, space and cyber capability).

**Concrete recommendations** included maintaining unambiguous U.S. nuclear superiority, growing active-duty personnel from roughly 1.4 to 1.6 million, redeploying forces into Southeast Europe and Asia, developing global missile defense, and creating a dedicated military space-control capability — funded by raising defense spending to 3.5–3.8% of GDP, an increase of roughly $15–20 billion annually in 2000 dollars.

**The "new Pearl Harbor" passage.** In a section titled "Creating Tomorrow's Dominant Force," the report observed that the kind of sweeping military transformation it called for would likely take a very long time to achieve politically, "absent some catastrophic and catalyzing event — like a new Pearl Harbor." This single sentence, written a year before September 11, 2001, became — and remains — the most quoted line in PNAC's entire output, for two very different reasons that should be kept firmly separate.

- **The documented, defensible reading:** the sentence is an observation about the *political economy of defense procurement* — that absent a dramatic external shock, democracies historically struggle to sustain the sacrifice and expenditure that large-scale military transformation requires. This is a genuinely common argument in defense-policy writing across the ideological spectrum, and mainstream academic critics of PNAC (see §7) generally read the passage this way: prescient in a grim, coincidental sense, but not evidence of foreknowledge or design.
- **The conspiratorial reading**, associated above all with the "9/11 Truth" movement and popularized by David Ray Griffin's 2004 book *The New Pearl Harbor* (a title taken directly from this passage, and a book that was reportedly found among material in Osama bin Laden's Abbottabad compound and was also included, entirely coincidentally to that fact, among reference materials made available to the official 9/11 Commission), holds that this sentence shows PNAC's authors anticipated, wanted, or even had foreknowledge of an attack like 9/11 to serve as the "catalyzing event" their broader project required. **This entry does not endorse this reading.** No documentary or testimonial evidence has ever substantiated the claim that any PNAC-linked official had foreknowledge of the September 11 attacks, and the "false flag" interpretation of this sentence rests entirely on retrospective pattern-matching between a widely circulated 2000 policy phrase and an unrelated 2001 terrorist attack carried out by al-Qaeda, a non-state actor with no documented institutional connection to PNAC or its authors. Readers should treat any source asserting PNAC "engineered" or foreknew 9/11 with the same skepticism this knowledge base applies to other undocumented grand-conspiracy claims (compare the Yinon Plan sourcing caveat in the Clean Break entry, §6.5 there).

**Post-9/11 reception.** Independent of the conspiratorial reading, serious critics — British journalist John Pilger, Labour MP Michael Meacher, and the German magazine *Der Spiegel*'s Jochen Bölsche among the most prominent — argued in 2002–03 that *Rebuilding America's Defenses* demonstrated the Bush administration's post-9/11 foreign policy (the 2002 National Security Strategy's preemption doctrine, the pursuit of a large sustained Gulf military presence, and eventually the Iraq invasion) had been substantially drafted *before* 9/11 and merely used the attacks as a politically convenient occasion to implement a preexisting agenda, rather than genuinely responding to a new threat. This is a real and defensible reading of the documentary record — the report's own text makes explicit that Gulf force presence "transcends the issue of the regime of Saddam Hussein" — distinct from, and much better evidenced than, the conspiratorial reading above.

## 6. After September 11: PNAC and the Path to War

**September 20, 2001 letter to President Bush.** Nine days after the attacks, PNAC sent Bush a letter — signed by Kristol and a long list of the usual affiliated figures — arguing that "any strategy aiming at the eradication of terrorism and its sponsors must include a determined effort to remove Saddam Hussein from power in Iraq," explicitly stating this should proceed *even if no evidence directly links Iraq to the attack itself*, and warning that leaving Hussein in power would represent "an early and perhaps decisive surrender in the war on international terrorism." This is among the most-cited PNAC documents precisely because of its timing and its explicit acknowledgment, in writing, that the case for invading Iraq did not depend on Iraqi responsibility for 9/11 — a fact later relevant to assessing how the actual public case for war (built substantially around alleged, and ultimately non-existent, Iraqi weapons of mass destruction and overstated terrorism links) related to the private strategic reasoning of some of its earliest advocates.

**Sustained public advocacy, 2001–2003.** Through the run-up to the invasion, PNAC and its affiliated writers continued a public campaign: Kristol's "Liberate Iraq" in *The Weekly Standard* (May 2001, i.e., before 9/11); Gary Schmitt memos to "opinion leaders" in 2000 and 2002 pressing alleged Iraq–al-Qaeda links; and repeated framing, across PNAC output and allied outlets, that anything short of regime change amounted to "surrender to terrorism." PNAC senior fellow Reuel Marc Gerecht offered one of the era's more nakedly stated rationales: that only a war against Saddam Hussein could "decisively restore the awe that protects American interests abroad and citizens at home" — a formulation about restoring deterrent *reputation* rather than addressing a specific documented threat, which critics have repeatedly pointed to as evidence the actual strategic logic driving PNAC's Iraq advocacy differed substantially from the public WMD-centered case the Bush administration ultimately made to Congress and the UN.

**Institutional afterlife inside government.** As detailed at greater length in the Clean Break entry (§8 there), several PNAC-adjacent figures moved into Bush-administration posts where Iraq policy was actually made: Wolfowitz as deputy secretary of defense; Perle chairing the Defense Policy Board; Douglas Feith (not a PNAC Statement signatory, but a Clean Break co-author moving in the identical circle) as under secretary of defense for policy, where he established the Office of Special Plans; Zalmay Khalilzad on the National Security Council and later as ambassador to Afghanistan, Iraq, and the UN; Elliott Abrams eventually as a Bush-era national security advisor; Scooter Libby as Cheney's chief of staff; and John Bolton — a PNAC director, though (per Scott Horton's characterization) more a hardline nationalist than a doctrinal neoconservative — as under secretary of state for arms control before becoming UN ambassador.

## 7. Did PNAC Actually Drive the Iraq War? A Real, Unresolved Academic Argument

This is the single most contested question in PNAC's historiography, and — as with the parallel question about Clean Break — serious, well-credentialed people land in genuinely different places. This entry tries to represent the actual shape of that disagreement rather than resolve it.

**The "PNAC as architect" position.** Journalists and commentators including the BBC's Paul Reynolds, Germany's Jochen Bölsche (writing in *Der Spiegel*), British MP Michael Meacher, and American academic Peter Dale Scott have argued PNAC's documents functioned as something close to an actual blueprint — that *Rebuilding America's Defenses* in particular "had been developed by PNAC for Rumsfeld, Cheney, Wolfowitz, and Libby" (Bölsche's phrasing) and represented a coherent, pre-drafted program for global American hegemony that the Bush administration then implemented "under cover of," in Philip Hammond's summary of this position, the War on Terror. Reynolds went further, suggesting Bush's post-9/11 "dominant" foreign-policy posture was at least partly inspired by, and possibly derivative of, PNAC's prior published thinking.

**The "influence overstated" position.** A cluster of academic political scientists and international-relations scholars — Donald E. Abelson (Western University), Philip Hammond (London South Bank University), and Inderjeet Parmar (City, University of London) prominent among them — have pushed back, arguing this reading overstates what can actually be demonstrated. Abelson's formulation is representative: acknowledging that PNAC signatories "received high level positions in the Bush administration" is, in his words, "a far cry from making the claim that the institute was the architect of Bush's foreign policy," and he notes scholars "know very little about the inner workings of this think tank and whether it has lived up to its billing." Hammond similarly argues *Rebuilding America's Defenses* was, on inspection, "unexceptional" — that its actual recommendations were "exactly what one would generally expect neoconservatives to say," publicly available since 2000, and that treating a think tank publication as a secret blueprint mistakes the ordinary, open functioning of Washington policy advocacy for something more conspiratorial than the evidence supports. Foreign-policy commentator Max Boot, himself broadly sympathetic to the neoconservative foreign-policy tradition, made a related point from inside that tradition: PNAC's contribution was real and significant, but the claim that it represented "some secret effort to overthrow traditional American foreign policy is not true" — his framing being that PNAC operated in full public view, publishing openly, precisely because its authors believed they were right and wanted to win the argument, not because they were running a covert operation.

**What is not seriously disputed:** the personnel overlap (§6 above); the documented timeline showing PNAC's Iraq advocacy predated both the Bush administration and 9/11 by several years; and the fact that language and priorities from *Rebuilding America's Defenses* and the broader PNAC corpus are recognizably present in the Bush administration's September 2002 National Security Strategy, which formalized a doctrine of military preemption. What remains genuinely disputed is the *causal* weight to assign this continuity — whether PNAC *caused* the shift in U.S. policy, merely *anticipated and articulated* views that its signatories (many of whom, like Cheney and Rumsfeld, had long records of hawkish foreign-policy positions predating PNAC's founding) already held and would likely have pursued through some other institutional vehicle if PNAC hadn't existed, or functioned as something in between — a coordinating and legitimizing platform that made a preexisting set of individual convictions look, and function, more like a coherent movement with a paper trail. Scholars in the second and third camps do not deny PNAC mattered; they deny it should be treated as a singular causal engine in a story that also includes 9/11's psychological and political shock, the Iraq Liberation Act's prior bipartisan momentum, the CIA's independently flawed WMD assessments, Ahmed Chalabi's Iraqi National Congress's parallel lobbying (a channel largely separate from PNAC proper), and Cheney and Rumsfeld's own long-standing institutional preferences dating back to their service under Nixon, Ford, and the elder Bush.

## 8. Scott Horton's Synthesis and the Antiwar/Libertarian Reading

Scott Horton — director of the Libertarian Institute, editorial director of Antiwar.com, and host of *The Scott Horton Show* — offers one of the most granular popular accounts connecting PNAC to the actual machinery of the Iraq War, serialized from his 2021 book *Enough Already: Time to End the War on Terrorism*. Horton's account, unlike the academic "influence overstated" position surveyed in §7, treats PNAC's role as central but embeds it in a wider argument about *three distinct, overlapping forces* — the neoconservative movement (of which PNAC was one institutional expression, alongside JINSA, AEI, WINEP, the Center for Security Policy, Heritage, and the Hudson Institute), the broader Israel lobby (a related but analytically separate phenomenon — see the Mearsheimer-Walt discussion in the Clean Break entry, §9 there), and the ordinary bureaucratic and financial interests of the military-industrial complex. Horton's framing explicitly resists collapsing these three into a single unified conspiracy, while still arguing all three pushed, for different internal reasons, toward the same outcome in 2002–03.

Horton traces the Wolfowitz Doctrine (1992 DPG) directly into PNAC's 1997 founding, describing Kristol and Kagan's "benevolent global hegemony" language as a public-facing repackaging of what Wolfowitz's team had tried and failed to make official Pentagon doctrine five years earlier. He documents Dick Cheney's own continuity across this period — noting Cheney's Gulf War-era private assurances to Saudi Arabia that U.S. forces would leave after Iraq's expulsion from Kuwait, and reading Cheney's later enthusiasm for a "doable" (Wolfowitz's word) Iraq invasion as consistent with a decade-old institutional preference for demonstrating, in Horton's phrase, that any state testing America's post-Cold War primacy "would find out the hard way." Horton is also careful to distinguish sincere strategic miscalculation from conscious deception: his account holds that figures like Wolfowitz and Woolsey were subject to serious "confirmation bias" — Horton specifically cites Iraqi National Congress-linked claims (including discredited assertions tying Iraq to the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing) that a rigorous, unbiased read of CIA and FBI assessments had already dismissed — rather than treating every PNAC-linked figure as knowingly lying in every case.

**Where Horton's account differs most sharply from the mainstream-academic "overstated influence" position (§7):** Horton does not treat the personnel overlap as merely suggestive; he treats it, combined with the documentary timeline (1992 DPG → 1996 Clean Break and Kristol/Kagan's *Foreign Affairs* article → 1997 PNAC founding → 1998 Iraq letters and the Iraq Liberation Act → 2000 *Rebuilding America's Defenses* → 2001 post-9/11 letter → 2002–03 Office of Special Plans and the actual invasion), as establishing a continuous, traceable intellectual and institutional through-line — not proof of a single unified plot, but evidence that a specific, identifiable, numerically small network of people (Horton repeats the "never more than 100" estimate) pursued a consistent strategic vision across three administrations and eventually got the chance to implement it. Readers should recognize this as a real point of method-level disagreement with academic critics like Abelson and Hammond, not merely a difference in political sympathy: Horton's case rests on treating documented personnel continuity and documentary timeline as strong evidence of causation, while Abelson and Hammond argue that personnel continuity is compatible with several less dramatic explanations and caution against inferring causation from correlation absent more direct evidence of PNAC documents functioning as literal instructions followed by decision-makers.

## 9. Institutional Network and Overlap with Clean Break

PNAC did not operate in isolation; it was one node — arguably the most publicly visible one — in a cluster of Washington institutions that shared personnel, funding relationships, and strategic outlook through the late 1990s and 2000s:

- **American Enterprise Institute (AEI):** Institutional home to Perle and (later) David Wurmser; published Wurmser's *Tyranny's Ally* (1999); many PNAC signatories and *Rebuilding America's Defenses* participants held concurrent AEI fellowships.
- **Jewish Institute for National Security Affairs (JINSA):** Represented on the original Clean Break study group by James Colbert; supplied several later Office of Special Plans staff, including Michael Makovsky.
- **Washington Institute for Near East Policy (WINEP):** An AIPAC-linked research spin-off; represented on the Clean Break study group by Jonathan Torop; later supplied OSP staffer David Schenker.
- **Institute for Advanced Strategic and Political Studies (IASPS):** The Jerusalem/Washington think tank that produced Clean Break itself; see the dedicated entry.
- **Center for Security Policy, Heritage Foundation, Hudson Institute:** Additional hawkish Washington think tanks in the same broad network, per Horton's account.
- **Foreign Policy Initiative (2009–2017):** PNAC's explicit, direct successor, co-founded by Kristol and Kagan once the Iraq War's unpopularity had made continuing to operate under the "PNAC" name a liability; scholars including Stephen Walt and Donald Abelson have characterized it as essentially "PNAC 2.0" pursuing the same broad primacy-oriented agenda (notably including sustained advocacy for intervention in Syria and Libya, and later hawkish positions on Russia and Ukraine) under a rebranded name. It dissolved in 2017.

**The relationship to Clean Break, precisely stated:** PNAC and Clean Break were separate documents from separate institutions (PNAC/New Citizenship Project vs. IASPS), produced a year apart, for different audiences (an American domestic foreign-policy coalition vs. an incoming Israeli prime minister), with only partial personnel overlap at the level of formal authorship (Perle, and later Wolfowitz and Khalilzad through the DPG lineage, are the clearest connective figures; Feith and the Wurmsers were not PNAC Statement signatories, though they moved in identical circles and ended up in overlapping government roles). What genuinely unites them is not shared authorship but a shared strategic logic — that negotiated accommodation with hostile or illiberal regional powers (Ba'athist Iraq and Syria in both documents' cases) was a strategic dead end, that American and Israeli interests in confronting those powers were substantially convergent, and that a demonstrated willingness to use preemptive force was the more reliable long-term guarantor of security than diplomacy or containment. Treating the two documents as literally the same project, or treating their authors as an undifferentiated single group, overstates the documentary record; treating them as wholly unrelated understates it just as badly. The honest description is two parallel, mutually aware, personnel-adjacent projects that arrived at closely related conclusions from related but not identical starting premises (Clean Break's was explicitly Israel-centered; PNAC's was explicitly America-centered, with Israel's regional position treated as one interest among several rather than the central organizing concern).

## 10. Decline, Dissolution, and What "Doing Its Job" Meant

By the end of 2006, a BBC correspondent found PNAC reduced to "a voice-mail box and a ghostly website," with a single remaining employee winding down operations. Gary Schmitt, PNAC's former executive director, told the BBC that year that the organization had never been intended to "go on forever" and had "already done its job" — a striking, unusually candid admission from a departing insider that PNAC understood itself as a temporary, purpose-built vehicle for a specific set of policy outcomes rather than a permanent institution, and that (in Schmitt's own assessment) it had achieved what it set out to do. Readers can weigh that self-assessment against the §7 debate above: Schmitt's claim that "our view has been adopted" is itself evidence for the "PNAC mattered" position, though it is worth noting it comes from a former PNAC official with an obvious interest in claiming credit for consequential real-world outcomes, rather than from a neutral source.

By 2006, of course, the Iraq War had also become a documented catastrophe by most measures — an insurgency, a sectarian civil war, no WMD ever found, and a U.S. Senate Intelligence Committee report (July 2004) concluding prewar intelligence claims had been "either overstated or were not supported by the underlying intelligence reporting." PNAC's dissolution in this context reads differently depending on the interpretive lens: as a movement declaring premature victory before its central project's failure became undeniable (an unflattering reading), as an organization genuinely built around narrow tactical objectives (regime change in Iraq, a preemption-centered National Security Strategy) that had, whatever their catastrophic consequences, technically been achieved (Schmitt's own reading), or simply as an ordinary Washington think tank reaching the natural end of its funding and organizing energy once its founders' attention moved elsewhere (the more mundane, institutionally unremarkable reading favored by Abelson and Hammond).

## 11. Open Questions for Further Research

1. **The causal-weight question (§7) remains the central unresolved issue** and deserves more than this entry's summary treatment — a deeper dive would trace primary internal Bush-administration deliberative records (to the extent declassified) rather than relying on PNAC's own public output and outside academic inference.
2. **How much of PNAC's post-2001 advocacy was genuinely new versus recycled from its 1998 Iraq campaign?** A close textual comparison of the January 1998 Clinton letter and the September 2001 Bush letter would sharpen this considerably; the surface argument is nearly identical three years and one catastrophic terrorist attack apart, which is itself worth interrogating directly rather than merely noting.
3. **The Foreign Policy Initiative's record (2009–2017)** is comparatively under-examined relative to PNAC itself; a dedicated entry tracing its advocacy on Libya, Syria, and Ukraine against PNAC's earlier Iraq advocacy would test whether the "same network, same doctrine, different decade" pattern holds up under equally close scrutiny for a case study playing out in real time as this knowledge base is being written.
4. **The Iraq Liberation Act's bipartisan support** deserves a more careful treatment than PNAC-centered narratives typically give it — separating out how much of its passage reflected the influence of PNAC-adjacent advocacy specifically versus a broader, genuinely bipartisan mid-1990s Washington consensus against Saddam Hussein that would very plausibly have found some other legislative vehicle regardless.
5. **A rigorous accounting of the "new Pearl Harbor" line's afterlife** in 9/11 conspiracy literature — tracing exactly how and when it moved from an obscure defense-policy report into Griffin's bestselling book and from there into wider popular culture — would be a genuinely interesting case study in how a real, verifiable primary-source quotation becomes raw material for an unrelated and unsupported conspiracy narrative, distinct from (and worth analytically separating from) the well-documented, non-conspiratorial critiques of the same report discussed in §5 and §7.

# The Project for the New American Century (PNAC)

> **Entry type:** Institutional/historical-political reference
> **Core subject:** A 1997–2006 Washington think tank that translated a specific strand of post-Cold War conservative foreign-policy thinking into public advocacy, staffed much of the George W. PNAC was never a large organization — accounts describe a small permanent staff working out of modest Washington offices — and its power, to the extent it had any, came from something other than institutional size: the density of its signatory lists and the number of those signatories who went on to hold senior national-security posts. That density, more than any budget line, is what later observers mean when they call PNAC influential.

## 2. Origins: From a *Foreign Affairs* Article to a Think Tank

PNAC's intellectual starting point predates its 1997 founding by a year. In mid-1996, William Kristol and Robert Kagan co-authored "Toward a Neo-Reaganite Foreign Policy" in *Foreign Affairs*, arguing that American conservatives had gone "adrift" on foreign policy after the Cold War, lacking a "compelling vision," and proposing instead a stance of what they explicitly called "benevolent global hegemony." Kristol — son of Irving Kristol, one of neoconservatism's founding intellectuals, and former chief of staff to Vice President Dan Quayle — and Kagan, a historian and former State Department official, believed the Republican Party needed a sharper alternative to what they saw as Bill Clinton's foreign-policy drift, and also needed to marginalize a competing current within their own coalition: post-Cold War isolationism and traditional realism, both of which had real constituencies on the American right in the mid-1990s (Patrick Buchanan's 1992 and 1996 primary campaigns being the most visible expression).

In June 1997, Kristol and Kagan formalized this project as PNAC, releasing a "Statement of Principles" that echoed the *Foreign Affairs* article nearly line for line. The statement diagnosed a specific problem — that conservatives "have not confidently advanced a strategic vision of America's role in the world," had "allowed differences over tactics to obscure potential agreement on strategic objectives," and had failed to "fight for a defense budget that would maintain American security." Its remedy rested on four pillars: substantially increased defense spending; strengthened ties to democratic allies combined with a willingness to "challenge regimes hostile to our interests and values"; the promotion of "political and economic freedom abroad"; and acceptance of "responsibility for America's unique role in preserving and extending an international order friendly to our security, our prosperity, and our principles." It closed by invoking Ronald Reagan explicitly, calling for a "Reaganite policy of military strength and moral clarity," and warned — in a phrase later authors would return to repeatedly — that "the history of the 20th century should have taught us that it is important to shape circumstances before crises emerge, and to meet threats before they become dire."

**On the Statement of Principles' 25 signatories.** The list is worth naming in full because so much of PNAC's later reputation rests on who was on it: Elliott Abrams, Gary Bauer, William J. Bennett, Jeb Bush, Dick Cheney, Eliot A. Cohen, Midge Decter, Paula Dobriansky, Steve Forbes, Aaron Friedberg, Francis Fukuyama, Frank Gaffney, Fred Iklé, Donald Kagan, Zalmay Khalilzad, I. Lewis "Scooter" Libby, Norman Podhoretz, Dan Quayle, Peter W. Rodman, Donald Rumsfeld, Stephen P. Rosen, Henry S. Rowen, Vin Weber, George Weigel, and Paul Wolfowitz — alongside Kristol and Kagan themselves. Ten of the 25 went on to serve in the George W. Bush administration, including Cheney as vice president, Rumsfeld as secretary of defense, and Wolfowitz as deputy secretary of defense. The list mixed genuine neoconservative intellectuals (Podhoretz, Decter, Fukuyama, Cohen) with religious-right figures (Bauer, Weigel), mainstream Republican establishment figures (Forbes, Jeb Bush), and hawkish nationalists with no particular neoconservative pedigree (Rumsfeld, Cheney, Rodman) — a coalition-building breadth that historian Maria Ryan has argued was itself part of PNAC's institutional strategy: not to represent a narrow faction, but to build the broadest possible tent for an assertive, primacy-oriented foreign policy.

**Notably absent from the founding statement:** Richard Perle, Douglas Feith, and David Wurmser — the three figures most central to *A Clean Break* the year before — did not sign PNAC's original Statement of Principles, though Perle became deeply involved in PNAC's later work (he is listed as a *Rebuilding America's Defenses* project participant) and all three moved in the same institutional orbit as PNAC's leadership throughout the late 1990s and 2000s. This is a genuinely important distinction for anyone tracing the two documents' relationship: PNAC and Clean Break were parallel, overlapping, mutually reinforcing projects with shared personnel and shared assumptions, not the same project under two names. See the Clean Break entry for the fuller story of that document's authorship.

## 3. The Deeper Lineage: The 1992 Defense Planning Guidance

PNAC did not invent the ideas in its Statement of Principles from scratch. Five years earlier, in the final months of the George H.W. Bush administration, then–Under Secretary of Defense for Policy Paul Wolfowitz — working for Secretary of Defense Dick Cheney, with I. Lewis Libby and Zalmay Khalilzad as principal co-drafters — produced a classified planning document known as the Defense Planning Guidance (DPG) for fiscal years 1994–99. Leaked to the *New York Times* in March 1992, the draft argued the United States' central post-Cold War objective should be preventing "the re-emergence of a new rival" anywhere in the world — not merely deterring the Soviet Union's successor states, but foreclosing the emergence of *any* peer competitor, friendly or hostile, and asserted the U.S. should be prepared to act preemptively and, if necessary, unilaterally to protect vital interests, explicitly including "access to vital raw materials, primarily Persian Gulf oil."

The leaked draft caused an immediate public and diplomatic backlash — European allies read it as an assertion of permanent American tutelage, and even within the elder Bush's own administration, the State Department under James Baker pushed back hard. The White House ordered a substantially softened rewrite before the document was finalized, and the episode contributed to a general sense (accurate, per most historians) that unrestrained "primacy" doctrine was politically unsellable in 1992.

What matters for tracing PNAC's lineage is what happened to the *idea*, not the sanitized final document: the original draft's core argument — permanent, unchallenged American military primacy, maintained if necessary through preemption — did not disappear. It migrated into private life with its authors (Wolfowitz, Libby, Khalilzad all went on to other posts, then out of government during the Clinton years, then back in under Bush II) and resurfaced, essentially unmodified in substance though softened in packaging, in PNAC's 1997 Statement of Principles and far more explicitly in *Rebuilding America's Defenses* three years later. This is the single clearest documented case of an idea moving from a classified Pentagon planning document, through a public backlash that killed its official form, into a private-sector think tank, and back into government a decade later with many of the same named authors — the exact "think tank as holding pattern for policy ideas between administrations" dynamic this entry is partly designed to illustrate. **See §7 for how this pattern repeats with the "new Pearl Harbor" line and again, differently, with the Clean Break/Office of Special Plans arc.**

## 4. The Iraq Campaign: 1998

PNAC's most consequential and most-cited public act, prior to *Rebuilding America's Defenses*, was a sustained campaign for regime change in Iraq conducted throughout 1998, three years before Bush took office and five years before the invasion.

**The January 26, 1998 open letter to President Clinton.** Signed by Elliott Abrams, Richard Perle, Paul Wolfowitz, R. James Woolsey (Clinton's own former CIA director), Donald Rumsfeld, Robert Zoellick, John Bolton, and others, the letter argued the existing UN-sanctions-and-inspections containment regime was failing, that the U.S. could "no longer depend on our partners in the Gulf War to continue to uphold the sanctions or to punish Saddam when he blocks or evades UN inspections," and that American policy "cannot continue to be crippled by a misguided insistence on unanimity in the UN Security Council." The letter asserted the U.S. already possessed sufficient legal authority under existing UN resolutions "to take the necessary steps, including military steps, to protect our vital interests in the Gulf" — i.e., it called for unilateral military action to remove Saddam Hussein, in January 1998, nearly six years before it happened.

**Newspaper advocacy.** In parallel, Kristol and Kagan published op-eds pressing the same argument in mainstream outlets — "Bombing Iraq Isn't Enough" in the *New York Times* (January 30, 1998) and "A 'Great Victory' for Iraq" in the *Washington Post* (February 26, 1998) — arguing that limited airstrikes (what would become Operation Desert Fox that December) fell far short of what the situation required.

**The May 1998 letter to Gingrich and Lott.** A follow-up letter to the Republican congressional leadership pressed the same argument directly at the legislative branch, urging Congress to act where the letter's authors judged the executive branch too cautious.

**The Iraq Liberation Act of 1998.** Congress responded that October, passing the Iraq Liberation Act (H.R. 4655) with broad bipartisan support; Clinton signed it into law on October 31, 1998. The Act made regime change in Iraq the stated, official (though non-binding, and unfunded for military action) policy of the United States — a milestone frequently cited by later critics as proof that "regime change was already U.S. law" well before the Bush administration or the September 11 attacks, though it is worth being precise that the Act itself authorized non-military assistance to Iraqi opposition groups, not an invasion, and its passage under a Democratic president working with a Republican Congress reflected a genuinely broad bipartisan consensus at the time — not solely the product of PNAC's advocacy, even if PNAC had pushed hard for exactly this outcome.

**January 1999: a critical memo on containment's failure.** PNAC circulated an internal memo criticizing December 1998's Operation Desert Fox bombing campaign as ineffective, questioning whether the Iraqi opposition the U.S. was now backing under the Iraq Liberation Act could actually accomplish anything, and dismissing "containment" as an illusion — signaling PNAC's leadership already regarded regime change, not containment, as the only serious option nearly three years before 9/11 and Bush's inauguration made the question live at the highest levels of government.

## 5. *Rebuilding America's Defenses* (September 2000)

PNAC's single most-cited and most-controversial document is the 90-page report *Rebuilding America's Defenses: Strategies, Forces, and Resources For a New Century*, released in September 2000 — before the contested 2000 election, before 9/11, and before anyone knew who the next president would be. Its primary author was Thomas Donnelly (who later transitioned and now publishes as Giselle Donnelly); Donald Kagan (Robert Kagan's father, a Yale classicist) and Gary Schmitt served as project chairmen. The report lists 27 additional participants who contributed papers or attended project meetings, of whom six subsequently held senior Bush-administration defense and foreign-policy posts — a number smaller than sometimes implied by the "PNAC wrote the Bush foreign policy" framing, but not nothing.

**Core argument.** The report claimed the preceding post-Cold War decade had provided "the geopolitical framework for widespread economic growth" and the global spread of "American principles of liberty and democracy," but warned this "global Pax Americana will not preserve itself." Current defense spending, it argued, was inadequate to the task of maintaining it, forcing policymakers into a series of "false economies" — shortchanging future readiness to fund present operations, choosing between force presence in Europe or Asia rather than affording both, and so on.

**Four core missions proposed for U.S. forces:** defense of the American homeland; the capacity to fight and win "multiple, simultaneous major theater wars"; performance of "constabulary" duties to shape the security environment in key regions on an ongoing basis (i.e., a large permanent global military footprint even absent active war); and transformation of the force to exploit what the report called the "revolution in military affairs" (precision weapons, networked command, space and cyber capability).

**Concrete recommendations** included maintaining unambiguous U.S. nuclear superiority, growing active-duty personnel from roughly 1.4 to 1.6 million, redeploying forces into Southeast Europe and Asia, developing global missile defense, and creating a dedicated military space-control capability — funded by raising defense spending to 3.5–3.8% of GDP, an increase of roughly $15–20 billion annually in 2000 dollars.

**The "new Pearl Harbor" passage.** In a section titled "Creating Tomorrow's Dominant Force," the report observed that the kind of sweeping military transformation it called for would likely take a very long time to achieve politically, "absent some catastrophic and catalyzing event — like a new Pearl Harbor." This single sentence, written a year before September 11, 2001, became — and remains — the most quoted line in PNAC's entire output, for two very different reasons that should be kept firmly separate.

- **The documented, defensible reading:** the sentence is an observation about the *political economy of defense procurement* — that absent a dramatic external shock, democracies historically struggle to sustain the sacrifice and expenditure that large-scale military transformation requires. This is a genuinely common argument in defense-policy writing across the ideological spectrum, and mainstream academic critics of PNAC (see §7) generally read the passage this way: prescient in a grim, coincidental sense, but not evidence of foreknowledge or design.
- **The conspiratorial reading**, associated above all with the "9/11 Truth" movement and popularized by David Ray Griffin's 2004 book *The New Pearl Harbor* (a title taken directly from this passage, and a book that was reportedly found among material in Osama bin Laden's Abbottabad compound and was also included, entirely coincidentally to that fact, among reference materials made available to the official 9/11 Commission), holds that this sentence shows PNAC's authors anticipated, wanted, or even had foreknowledge of an attack like 9/11 to serve as the "catalyzing event" their broader project required. **This entry does not endorse this reading.** No documentary or testimonial evidence has ever substantiated the claim that any PNAC-linked official had foreknowledge of the September 11 attacks, and the "false flag" interpretation of this sentence rests entirely on retrospective pattern-matching between a widely circulated 2000 policy phrase and an unrelated 2001 terrorist attack carried out by al-Qaeda, a non-state actor with no documented institutional connection to PNAC or its authors. Readers should treat any source asserting PNAC "engineered" or foreknew 9/11 with the same skepticism this knowledge base applies to other undocumented grand-conspiracy claims (compare the Yinon Plan sourcing caveat in the Clean Break entry, §6.5 there).

**Post-9/11 reception.** Independent of the conspiratorial reading, serious critics — British journalist John Pilger, Labour MP Michael Meacher, and the German magazine *Der Spiegel*'s Jochen Bölsche among the most prominent — argued in 2002–03 that *Rebuilding America's Defenses* demonstrated the Bush administration's post-9/11 foreign policy (the 2002 National Security Strategy's preemption doctrine, the pursuit of a large sustained Gulf military presence, and eventually the Iraq invasion) had been substantially drafted *before* 9/11 and merely used the attacks as a politically convenient occasion to implement a preexisting agenda, rather than genuinely responding to a new threat. This is a real and defensible reading of the documentary record — the report's own text makes explicit that Gulf force presence "transcends the issue of the regime of Saddam Hussein" — distinct from, and much better evidenced than, the conspiratorial reading above.

## 6. After September 11: PNAC and the Path to War

**September 20, 2001 letter to President Bush.** Nine days after the attacks, PNAC sent Bush a letter — signed by Kristol and a long list of the usual affiliated figures — arguing that "any strategy aiming at the eradication of terrorism and its sponsors must include a determined effort to remove Saddam Hussein from power in Iraq," explicitly stating this should proceed *even if no evidence directly links Iraq to the attack itself*, and warning that leaving Hussein in power would represent "an early and perhaps decisive surrender in the war on international terrorism." This is among the most-cited PNAC documents precisely because of its timing and its explicit acknowledgment, in writing, that the case for invading Iraq did not depend on Iraqi responsibility for 9/11 — a fact later relevant to assessing how the actual public case for war (built substantially around alleged, and ultimately non-existent, Iraqi weapons of mass destruction and overstated terrorism links) related to the private strategic reasoning of some of its earliest advocates.

**Sustained public advocacy, 2001–2003.** Through the run-up to the invasion, PNAC and its affiliated writers continued a public campaign: Kristol's "Liberate Iraq" in *The Weekly Standard* (May 2001, i.e., before 9/11); Gary Schmitt memos to "opinion leaders" in 2000 and 2002 pressing alleged Iraq–al-Qaeda links; and repeated framing, across PNAC output and allied outlets, that anything short of regime change amounted to "surrender to terrorism." PNAC senior fellow Reuel Marc Gerecht offered one of the era's more nakedly stated rationales: that only a war against Saddam Hussein could "decisively restore the awe that protects American interests abroad and citizens at home" — a formulation about restoring deterrent *reputation* rather than addressing a specific documented threat, which critics have repeatedly pointed to as evidence the actual strategic logic driving PNAC's Iraq advocacy differed substantially from the public WMD-centered case the Bush administration ultimately made to Congress and the UN.

**Institutional afterlife inside government.** As detailed at greater length in the Clean Break entry (§8 there), several PNAC-adjacent figures moved into Bush-administration posts where Iraq policy was actually made: Wolfowitz as deputy secretary of defense; Perle chairing the Defense Policy Board; Douglas Feith (not a PNAC Statement signatory, but a Clean Break co-author moving in the identical circle) as under secretary of defense for policy, where he established the Office of Special Plans; Zalmay Khalilzad on the National Security Council and later as ambassador to Afghanistan, Iraq, and the UN; Elliott Abrams eventually as a Bush-era national security advisor; Scooter Libby as Cheney's chief of staff; and John Bolton — a PNAC director, though (per Scott Horton's characterization) more a hardline nationalist than a doctrinal neoconservative — as under secretary of state for arms control before becoming UN ambassador.

## 7. Did PNAC Actually Drive the Iraq War? A Real, Unresolved Academic Argument

This is the single most contested question in PNAC's historiography, and — as with the parallel question about Clean Break — serious, well-credentialed people land in genuinely different places. This entry tries to represent the actual shape of that disagreement rather than resolve it.

**The "PNAC as architect" position.** Journalists and commentators including the BBC's Paul Reynolds, Germany's Jochen Bölsche (writing in *Der Spiegel*), British MP Michael Meacher, and American academic Peter Dale Scott have argued PNAC's documents functioned as something close to an actual blueprint — that *Rebuilding America's Defenses* in particular "had been developed by PNAC for Rumsfeld, Cheney, Wolfowitz, and Libby" (Bölsche's phrasing) and represented a coherent, pre-drafted program for global American hegemony that the Bush administration then implemented "under cover of," in Philip Hammond's summary of this position, the War on Terror. Reynolds went further, suggesting Bush's post-9/11 "dominant" foreign-policy posture was at least partly inspired by, and possibly derivative of, PNAC's prior published thinking.

**The "influence overstated" position.** A cluster of academic political scientists and international-relations scholars — Donald E. Abelson (Western University), Philip Hammond (London South Bank University), and Inderjeet Parmar (City, University of London) prominent among them — have pushed back, arguing this reading overstates what can actually be demonstrated. Abelson's formulation is representative: acknowledging that PNAC signatories "received high level positions in the Bush administration" is, in his words, "a far cry from making the claim that the institute was the architect of Bush's foreign policy," and he notes scholars "know very little about the inner workings of this think tank and whether it has lived up to its billing." Hammond similarly argues *Rebuilding America's Defenses* was, on inspection, "unexceptional" — that its actual recommendations were "exactly what one would generally expect neoconservatives to say," publicly available since 2000, and that treating a think tank publication as a secret blueprint mistakes the ordinary, open functioning of Washington policy advocacy for something more conspiratorial than the evidence supports. Foreign-policy commentator Max Boot, himself broadly sympathetic to the neoconservative foreign-policy tradition, made a related point from inside that tradition: PNAC's contribution was real and significant, but the claim that it represented "some secret effort to overthrow traditional American foreign policy is not true" — his framing being that PNAC operated in full public view, publishing openly, precisely because its authors believed they were right and wanted to win the argument, not because they were running a covert operation.

**What is not seriously disputed:** the personnel overlap (§6 above); the documented timeline showing PNAC's Iraq advocacy predated both the Bush administration and 9/11 by several years; and the fact that language and priorities from *Rebuilding America's Defenses* and the broader PNAC corpus are recognizably present in the Bush administration's September 2002 National Security Strategy, which formalized a doctrine of military preemption. What remains genuinely disputed is the *causal* weight to assign this continuity — whether PNAC *caused* the shift in U.S. policy, merely *anticipated and articulated* views that its signatories (many of whom, like Cheney and Rumsfeld, had long records of hawkish foreign-policy positions predating PNAC's founding) already held and would likely have pursued through some other institutional vehicle if PNAC hadn't existed, or functioned as something in between — a coordinating and legitimizing platform that made a preexisting set of individual convictions look, and function, more like a coherent movement with a paper trail. Scholars in the second and third camps do not deny PNAC mattered; they deny it should be treated as a singular causal engine in a story that also includes 9/11's psychological and political shock, the Iraq Liberation Act's prior bipartisan momentum, the CIA's independently flawed WMD assessments, Ahmed Chalabi's Iraqi National Congress's parallel lobbying (a channel largely separate from PNAC proper), and Cheney and Rumsfeld's own long-standing institutional preferences dating back to their service under Nixon, Ford, and the elder Bush.

## 8. Scott Horton's Synthesis and the Antiwar/Libertarian Reading

Scott Horton — director of the Libertarian Institute, editorial director of Antiwar.com, and host of *The Scott Horton Show* — offers one of the most granular popular accounts connecting PNAC to the actual machinery of the Iraq War, serialized from his 2021 book *Enough Already: Time to End the War on Terrorism*. Horton's account, unlike the academic "influence overstated" position surveyed in §7, treats PNAC's role as central but embeds it in a wider argument about *three distinct, overlapping forces* — the neoconservative movement (of which PNAC was one institutional expression, alongside JINSA, AEI, WINEP, the Center for Security Policy, Heritage, and the Hudson Institute), the broader Israel lobby (a related but analytically separate phenomenon — see the Mearsheimer-Walt discussion in the Clean Break entry, §9 there), and the ordinary bureaucratic and financial interests of the military-industrial complex. Horton's framing explicitly resists collapsing these three into a single unified conspiracy, while still arguing all three pushed, for different internal reasons, toward the same outcome in 2002–03.

Horton traces the Wolfowitz Doctrine (1992 DPG) directly into PNAC's 1997 founding, describing Kristol and Kagan's "benevolent global hegemony" language as a public-facing repackaging of what Wolfowitz's team had tried and failed to make official Pentagon doctrine five years earlier. He documents Dick Cheney's own continuity across this period — noting Cheney's Gulf War-era private assurances to Saudi Arabia that U.S. forces would leave after Iraq's expulsion from Kuwait, and reading Cheney's later enthusiasm for a "doable" (Wolfowitz's word) Iraq invasion as consistent with a decade-old institutional preference for demonstrating, in Horton's phrase, that any state testing America's post-Cold War primacy "would find out the hard way." Horton is also careful to distinguish sincere strategic miscalculation from conscious deception: his account holds that figures like Wolfowitz and Woolsey were subject to serious "confirmation bias" — Horton specifically cites Iraqi National Congress-linked claims (including discredited assertions tying Iraq to the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing) that a rigorous, unbiased read of CIA and FBI assessments had already dismissed — rather than treating every PNAC-linked figure as knowingly lying in every case.

**Where Horton's account differs most sharply from the mainstream-academic "overstated influence" position (§7):** Horton does not treat the personnel overlap as merely suggestive; he treats it, combined with the documentary timeline (1992 DPG → 1996 Clean Break and Kristol/Kagan's *Foreign Affairs* article → 1997 PNAC founding → 1998 Iraq letters and the Iraq Liberation Act → 2000 *Rebuilding America's Defenses* → 2001 post-9/11 letter → 2002–03 Office of Special Plans and the actual invasion), as establishing a continuous, traceable intellectual and institutional through-line — not proof of a single unified plot, but evidence that a specific, identifiable, numerically small network of people (Horton repeats the "never more than 100" estimate) pursued a consistent strategic vision across three administrations and eventually got the chance to implement it. Readers should recognize this as a real point of method-level disagreement with academic critics like Abelson and Hammond, not merely a difference in political sympathy: Horton's case rests on treating documented personnel continuity and documentary timeline as strong evidence of causation, while Abelson and Hammond argue that personnel continuity is compatible with several less dramatic explanations and caution against inferring causation from correlation absent more direct evidence of PNAC documents functioning as literal instructions followed by decision-makers.

## 9. Institutional Network and Overlap with Clean Break

PNAC did not operate in isolation; it was one node — arguably the most publicly visible one — in a cluster of Washington institutions that shared personnel, funding relationships, and strategic outlook through the late 1990s and 2000s:

- **American Enterprise Institute (AEI):** Institutional home to Perle and (later) David Wurmser; published Wurmser's *Tyranny's Ally* (1999); many PNAC signatories and *Rebuilding America's Defenses* participants held concurrent AEI fellowships.
- **Jewish Institute for National Security Affairs (JINSA):** Represented on the original Clean Break study group by James Colbert; supplied several later Office of Special Plans staff, including Michael Makovsky.
- **Washington Institute for Near East Policy (WINEP):** An AIPAC-linked research spin-off; represented on the Clean Break study group by Jonathan Torop; later supplied OSP staffer David Schenker.
- **Institute for Advanced Strategic and Political Studies (IASPS):** The Jerusalem/Washington think tank that produced Clean Break itself; see the dedicated entry.
- **Center for Security Policy, Heritage Foundation, Hudson Institute:** Additional hawkish Washington think tanks in the same broad network, per Horton's account.
- **Foreign Policy Initiative (2009–2017):** PNAC's explicit, direct successor, co-founded by Kristol and Kagan once the Iraq War's unpopularity had made continuing to operate under the "PNAC" name a liability; scholars including Stephen Walt and Donald Abelson have characterized it as essentially "PNAC 2.0" pursuing the same broad primacy-oriented agenda (notably including sustained advocacy for intervention in Syria and Libya, and later hawkish positions on Russia and Ukraine) under a rebranded name. It dissolved in 2017.

**The relationship to Clean Break, precisely stated:** PNAC and Clean Break were separate documents from separate institutions (PNAC/New Citizenship Project vs. IASPS), produced a year apart, for different audiences (an American domestic foreign-policy coalition vs. an incoming Israeli prime minister), with only partial personnel overlap at the level of formal authorship (Perle, and later Wolfowitz and Khalilzad through the DPG lineage, are the clearest connective figures; Feith and the Wurmsers were not PNAC Statement signatories, though they moved in identical circles and ended up in overlapping government roles). What genuinely unites them is not shared authorship but a shared strategic logic — that negotiated accommodation with hostile or illiberal regional powers (Ba'athist Iraq and Syria in both documents' cases) was a strategic dead end, that American and Israeli interests in confronting those powers were substantially convergent, and that a demonstrated willingness to use preemptive force was the more reliable long-term guarantor of security than diplomacy or containment. Treating the two documents as literally the same project, or treating their authors as an undifferentiated single group, overstates the documentary record; treating them as wholly unrelated understates it just as badly. The honest description is two parallel, mutually aware, personnel-adjacent projects that arrived at closely related conclusions from related but not identical starting premises (Clean Break's was explicitly Israel-centered; PNAC's was explicitly America-centered, with Israel's regional position treated as one interest among several rather than the central organizing concern).

## 10. Decline, Dissolution, and What "Doing Its Job" Meant

By the end of 2006, a BBC correspondent found PNAC reduced to "a voice-mail box and a ghostly website," with a single remaining employee winding down operations. Gary Schmitt, PNAC's former executive director, told the BBC that year that the organization had never been intended to "go on forever" and had "already done its job" — a striking, unusually candid admission from a departing insider that PNAC understood itself as a temporary, purpose-built vehicle for a specific set of policy outcomes rather than a permanent institution, and that (in Schmitt's own assessment) it had achieved what it set out to do. Readers can weigh that self-assessment against the §7 debate above: Schmitt's claim that "our view has been adopted" is itself evidence for the "PNAC mattered" position, though it is worth noting it comes from a former PNAC official with an obvious interest in claiming credit for consequential real-world outcomes, rather than from a neutral source.

By 2006, of course, the Iraq War had also become a documented catastrophe by most measures — an insurgency, a sectarian civil war, no WMD ever found, and a U.S. Senate Intelligence Committee report (July 2004) concluding prewar intelligence claims had been "either overstated or were not supported by the underlying intelligence reporting." PNAC's dissolution in this context reads differently depending on the interpretive lens: as a movement declaring premature victory before its central project's failure became undeniable (an unflattering reading), as an organization genuinely built around narrow tactical objectives (regime change in Iraq, a preemption-centered National Security Strategy) that had, whatever their catastrophic consequences, technically been achieved (Schmitt's own reading), or simply as an ordinary Washington think tank reaching the natural end of its funding and organizing energy once its founders' attention moved elsewhere (the more mundane, institutionally unremarkable reading favored by Abelson and Hammond).

## 11. Open Questions for Further Research

1. **The causal-weight question (§7) remains the central unresolved issue** and deserves more than this entry's summary treatment — a deeper dive would trace primary internal Bush-administration deliberative records (to the extent declassified) rather than relying on PNAC's own public output and outside academic inference.
2. **How much of PNAC's post-2001 advocacy was genuinely new versus recycled from its 1998 Iraq campaign?** A close textual comparison of the January 1998 Clinton letter and the September 2001 Bush letter would sharpen this considerably; the surface argument is nearly identical three years and one catastrophic terrorist attack apart, which is itself worth interrogating directly rather than merely noting.
3. **The Foreign Policy Initiative's record (2009–2017)** is comparatively under-examined relative to PNAC itself; a dedicated entry tracing its advocacy on Libya, Syria, and Ukraine against PNAC's earlier Iraq advocacy would test whether the "same network, same doctrine, different decade" pattern holds up under equally close scrutiny for a case study playing out in real time as this knowledge base is being written.
4. **The Iraq Liberation Act's bipartisan support** deserves a more careful treatment than PNAC-centered narratives typically give it — separating out how much of its passage reflected the influence of PNAC-adjacent advocacy specifically versus a broader, genuinely bipartisan mid-1990s Washington consensus against Saddam Hussein that would very plausibly have found some other legislative vehicle regardless.
5. **A rigorous accounting of the "new Pearl Harbor" line's afterlife** in 9/11 conspiracy literature — tracing exactly how and when it moved from an obscure defense-policy report into Griffin's bestselling book and from there into wider popular culture — would be a genuinely interesting case study in how a real, verifiable primary-source quotation becomes raw material for an unrelated and unsupported conspiracy narrative, distinct from (and worth analytically separating from) the well-documented, non-conspiratorial critiques of the same report discussed in §5 and §7.
