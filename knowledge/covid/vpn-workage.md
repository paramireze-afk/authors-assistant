# Can Anyone Get the VPN to Work?

*How a public health emergency quietly reinvented the office*

In March 2020, “working from home” still meant something had gone wrong. There were no hybrid schedules, no remote-work agreements, and no choosing which days to come in.

And in March 2020, something had gone very wrong.

## “I'll do what I can”

On March 17, 2020, I got an email telling me that, because of the COVID-19 pandemic, I had been selected to telecommute, effective the day before. My reply was wonderfully understated: I had read the message and would do what I could to help with daily operations from home.

There was no grand realization that my working life was about to change. Just: okay, I'll work from home.

A week later, I was apparently sold. On March 24, I wrote: “This is my first time working remotely and definitely the world I want to live in.”

The next day, I passed along a prediction we'd heard from the UW Health infrastructure side: after all the work it took to move the workforce home, maybe there would be no going back. Two days after that, I was wondering whether remote schooling might change for good, too.

Those emails are funny now because we're reading them with the ending already spoiled. At the time, nobody knew.

## Moving the office into everyone's house

Working from home sounds simple until an entire organization tries it. The office was never just a room. It was computers, networks, printers, shared drives, phones, websites, and countless invisible connections that everyone took for granted until they had to work from a kitchen table.

A May 2020 email thread captures the period perfectly. Someone was struggling to get between a VPN-connected desktop and a Radiology website that didn't work over the VPN. The conversation bounced among the VPN, the remote office computer, the home computer, and whether files could be moved from one to another. Then, almost immediately, a follow-up arrived: never mind, it's something else.

Welcome to IT during COVID.

Today, that's an annoying support ticket. In the spring of 2020, enough tickets like it decided whether a department scattered across hundreds of improvised home offices could function at all.

## A farewell to the printer

Of all the technology COVID disrupted, the office printer may deserve the most affectionate send-off.

In June 2020, I was helping someone pull together reports for an annual review, and she couldn't print from home. My solution was pure 2020: save it as a PDF. It would stay nicely formatted and could be printed later if anyone still needed paper.

It's not the stuff of pandemic documentaries, and that's exactly why I like it. History remembers lockdowns, vaccines, and overwhelmed hospitals. An IT inbox remembers that someone couldn't print a report. For many of us, those small problems were what working through COVID actually looked like.

We were building the plane while flying it, except sometimes the plane couldn't connect to the VPN.

## Not everyone went home

There was another side to all of this. In December 2020, a department holiday message thanked everyone for their flexibility as most of the group moved to remote work, and it specifically recognized the people still working onsite for the extra effort that took.

That's worth remembering. “Remote work” can make it sound as if COVID sent everyone home with a laptop. Healthcare doesn't work that way. Some jobs could move to bedrooms and kitchens; others still needed someone physically there. IT worked across that line, keeping the technology running for both.

## When temporary became permanent

Eventually, the emergency stopped feeling temporary, and you can watch it happen in the inbox.

Even in 2023, COVID lingered in the language of everyday work: a colleague's email signature still explained that she was working both onsite and remotely “due to COVID-19.”

That same year, my own remote-work agreement formally recorded 100 percent remote and 0 percent on campus for July 2023 through June 2024. It covered things nobody mentioned in March 2020: where I would physically work, insurance, business visitors, onsite expectations, university-owned equipment, VPN access, data security, and privacy.

The contrast says it all:

- **2020:** Go home. There's a pandemic. We'll figure it out.
- **2023:** Please document the precise terms under which your home is now a workplace.

And naturally, there was paperwork about the paperwork. In July 2024, an email titled “Potential Missing Remote Work Agreements” sorted out two versions of my agreement in the approval queue. One was approved and the earlier one was sent back, with corrections to dates, expectations for coming onsite for meetings or critical work, and whether personal hardware was being used to access or transfer data.

Four years earlier, the emergency was whether I could reach a website over the VPN. Now we were fine-tuning the governance of remote work. The technology hadn't just helped us survive a lockdown. It had created a new kind of workplace, complete with its own bureaucracy.

## The office phone quietly retires

Then there are the small casualties.

In September 2024, I was reviewing training documentation for a Radiology program and noticed it listed my office phone number. I asked to have it changed, because I didn't use an office phone anymore. I was “full time at home now.” I suggested the team's shared email address instead, so messages would reach the developers even when I wasn't available.

That may be my favorite post-COVID artifact. There was no farewell for the office phone and no announcement that an era had ended. It simply became irrelevant enough that I asked someone to delete it.

## Solving Tuesday

That's the funny thing about a technology crisis. At first, everyone is focused on getting through tomorrow: get the VPN working, get people into their remote computers, figure out how to move files, turn the thing someone usually prints into a PDF, keep the department running.

Nobody staring at a VPN problem in May 2020 needed to solve the future of work. They needed to solve Tuesday.

But solve enough Tuesdays, and eventually you've built the future.

By 2024, I wasn't connecting to an office because of an emergency. I was simply home, full time. The workaround had become the arrangement.

And I had predicted it almost as soon as it started: “This is my first time working remotely and definitely the world I want to live in.”

Apparently, I meant it.
