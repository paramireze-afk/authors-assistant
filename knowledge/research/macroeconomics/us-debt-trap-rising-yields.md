# Notes: The US Debt Trap, Rising Yields, and the 1970s Parallel

*Source: Finance podcast conversation between Chris and Dave Russell*
*Topic: Bond yields, inflation, the dollar, and fiscal sustainability*

---

## 1. The Core Problem: Borrowing to Pay Interest

- The 30-year Treasury yield hit **5.2%**, a ~19-year high (last comparable levels: early 2000s, briefly in 2007).
- On its own, 5.2% isn't a dangerous rate. It becomes dangerous when **debt is large relative to income** and that income can't cover the borrowing cost.
- The US carries roughly **$40 trillion in on-balance-sheet debt** (not counting unfunded liabilities like Social Security/Medicare).
- Historically, low rates masked the risk of high debt loads — cheap borrowing meant the consequences of piling on more debt were minor. As rates rise, that math flips.
- Key metric: the **return generated per dollar borrowed**. When that return was consistently below $1, the debt stopped paying for itself, and more borrowing became necessary just to keep up — a self-reinforcing spiral.
- The analogy used: a bank won't lend to someone who can only repay a loan's interest by taking out *more* debt. That's what loan sharks do — and loan sharks charge higher rates. The implication: the market is starting to treat US debt the way a loan shark treats a bad borrower.

## 2. The Real Deficit Number

- The officially projected deficit for the year: **~$1.9–2 trillion**.
- But looking at the actual increase in total US debt (via the Treasury's "Debt to the Penny" data) over the last 12 months shows a **$3.5 trillion** increase — suggesting the "real" fiscal gap is much larger than headline figures imply.
- Rising defense spending (a proposed 56% increase, toward $1.5 trillion) is flagged as especially inflationary, because military spending largely produces goods with **no downstream revenue-generating use** — unlike infrastructure or productive investment. GDP growth driven by "building things to destroy them" is framed as illusory growth, potentially even negative once inflation is factored in.

## 3. Why Is Nobody Buying US Debt Anymore?

Several structural reasons were given for **falling demand for US Treasuries**:

1. **Weaponization of the dollar/SWIFT system** — after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the freezing/confiscation of Russian dollar assets sent a signal to *every* large holder of US debt (China, Japan, Gulf states, etc.): holding US debt is a geopolitical liability, not just a financial asset.
2. **Falling trust in US institutional/political stability** — bonds trade fundamentally on trust in the sovereign issuer; that trust is eroding.
3. **Central banks pivoting to gold** — gold has reportedly overtaken US Treasuries as the primary reserve asset held by central banks globally, as countries (notably China) have reduced Treasury holdings.
4. **No new buyer pool exists** — with traditional large holders stepping back, there's no obvious new source of demand, meaning yields have to rise to attract *any* buyers.

## 4. A Warning Signal: Yields Up, Dollar Down at the Same Time

- Normally, rising yields attract foreign capital, which should **strengthen** a currency.
- The chart discussed showed the opposite: **30-year yields rising while the dollar simultaneously fell** — described as a mirror-image move.
- This pattern is a classic sign of **fiscal/currency stress typically associated with emerging markets**, not a reserve-currency economy. If you removed the label from the chart, most economists would guess it belonged to an emerging-market currency in trouble.
- Interpretation: this looks like large holders (sovereign or institutional) selling Treasuries and converting proceeds into other assets (gold, other currencies) rather than reinvesting in dollars.

## 5. The 1970s Parallel: Two Waves of Inflation

- The 1973–74 oil embargo triggered the **first wave** of 1970s inflation.
- A **second, worse wave** followed, eventually requiring Fed Chair Paul Volcker to hike short-term rates to ~21% and long rates to ~14–15% to break it.
- The argument: we may be repeating this pattern with different triggers — an **energy price shock** (today, tied to war/geopolitics) plus a **government spending/money-printing problem** (Vietnam War then; a broader "forever war" dynamic now).
- Mechanism described (the wage-price spiral):
  1. Energy costs rise → costs ripple through the whole supply chain.
  2. Real wages fall (prices outpace pay).
  3. Workers demand higher wages to keep up.
  4. Higher wages feed back into higher prices.
  5. This repeats — "the genie out of the bottle" — and becomes very hard to reverse once entrenched.
- Current framing in official narratives ("this inflation is temporary, it'll resolve when the conflict ends") is compared to similar messaging in the early 1970s — before the second, more damaging wave hit.

## 6. What a Second Inflation Wave Would Do to Bonds

- In the early 1980s, long-duration bonds bought at ~5% coupons, while inflation ran at ~13%, were nicknamed **"certificates of confiscation"** — their market value collapsed (a large "haircut") because fixed low yields become worthless in real terms during high inflation.
- The concern raised: today's long-duration bondholders could face a similar repricing if a second inflation wave materializes.

## 7. The Oil–Yield Correlation

- A **sustained** (not day-to-day) rise in oil prices correlates strongly with rising bond yields, because oil is a foundational input across the entire economy — fuel for transport, raw material for goods, and an input at every stage of the supply chain.
- Even though headline oil futures don't look extremely high, refined products (diesel, jet fuel, petrol, bunker fuel) have been trading as if crude were $130–140/barrel for several months — implying real tightness in energy markets beneath the surface.
- Sustained oil inflation is **cost-push inflation**, which is largely immune to interest rate policy: cutting rates doesn't fix a supply-side energy shortage, and cutting rates while supply is tight risks stimulating demand for oil products the market can't currently absorb — worsening the imbalance.

## 8. The Fed's (and Treasury's) Trap

Options for the US government/Fed to manage the rising-yield/inflation problem, and why each is limited:

| Option | Problem |
|---|---|
| **Increase demand for bonds** | No new major buyer pool exists; former large buyers (China, others) are pulling back for geopolitical/trust reasons. |
| **Cut spending / reduce deficits** | Politically unpopular — cutting spending doesn't win re-election, so there's little political appetite. |
| **Cut interest rates** | Doesn't address supply-side (cost-push) inflation from energy; may even worsen it by stimulating demand. |
| **Print money / monetize debt (buy your own debt, yield curve control)** | "Solves" the immediate financing problem but devalues the currency — not a fix, just an accelerant toward the same endpoint. |

The conclusion drawn: policymakers (the Fed) are "caught between a rock and a hard place and another hard place" — there isn't a clean policy lever left that solves the debt/inflation/yield problem simultaneously.

## 9. Big Picture Takeaway

- 5.2% isn't inherently a crisis rate — it becomes dangerous only when debt levels are already extreme and can't be serviced without additional borrowing.
- Years of cheap money allowed debt to accumulate to a scale where even modest rate increases now have outsized consequences.
- Structural demand for US debt is shrinking (geopolitical trust erosion, shift to gold as a reserve asset).
- A second, wage-driven inflation wave (echoing the late 1970s) is considered a real risk, which would be especially punishing for long-duration bondholders.
- The practical toolkit for addressing all of this simultaneously is very limited — most available levers make one problem better while making another worse.

## Glossary (Quick Reference)

- **Yield vs. Price**: Bond prices and yields move inversely. Rising yields = falling bond prices.
- **Cost-push inflation**: Inflation driven by rising input/production costs (e.g., energy), as opposed to demand-pull inflation (too much money chasing goods). Monetary policy (rate changes) is less effective against it.
- **Debt servicing**: The cost of paying interest (and sometimes principal) on existing debt.
- **Reserve asset**: An asset central banks hold as a store of value/foreign exchange backing — traditionally US Treasuries, increasingly gold per this discussion.
- **Yield curve control**: A central bank policy of buying bonds to cap yields at a target level, effectively printing money to suppress borrowing costs.
- **"Certificates of confiscation"**: 1980s nickname for long-duration bonds whose fixed low yields were wiped out in real terms by high inflation.
