# The Island of Mist: A History Lover's Guide to the Isle of Skye

If your cousin came home from Skye with photos of jagged black mountains, sheep on single-track roads, and cliffs dropping straight into the sea, they were standing in one of the most layered places in Europe. Almost every rock on this island has a story, and some of those stories are 170 million years old.

You don't need a plane ticket to get to know it. Here's the island, era by era.

---

## First, Where Is It?

Skye sits just off the northwest coast of mainland Scotland. It's the largest island of the **Inner Hebrides**, a chain of islands scattered along Scotland's Atlantic edge. It's roughly 50 miles long, but its coastline is so ragged with sea lochs and peninsulas that no spot on the island is more than about five miles from the sea.

About 10,000 people live there today. The main town, **Portree**, is famous for its row of brightly painted harbor houses, which very likely appear somewhere in your cousin's photos.

The island has two Gaelic names, and both tell you something:

- **An t-Eilean Sgitheanach**, its formal name, whose meaning is still debated (possibly "the winged island," for its shape)
- **Eilean a' Cheò**, "the Island of Mist," which anyone who has been there will tell you is simply accurate

---

## Chapter 1: When Dinosaurs Walked the Beaches (~170 million years ago)

Long before anyone named it anything, Skye was part of a warm, shallow coastal landscape of lagoons and mudflats. Dinosaurs walked across that mud, and some of their footprints hardened and survived.

Today you can see them on beaches like **An Corran near Staffin**, where at low tide large three-toed prints appear in the rock, left by meat- and plant-eating dinosaurs of the Middle Jurassic period. Fossils from this period are rare worldwide, which makes Skye unusually important to paleontologists. The island has earned the nickname "Dinosaur Isle," and new trackways are still being found by researchers.

> **Picture this:** You're standing on a gray Scottish beach in a raincoat, and at your feet is a footprint bigger than a dinner plate, made by an animal that lived before flowers existed.

---

## Chapter 2: Fire and Ice Make the Mountains (~60 million years ago)

The famous **Cuillin** mountains came much later, from volcanic activity as the Atlantic Ocean was opening up. There are two very different ranges side by side:

- **The Black Cuillin:** dark, jagged, made of a rough rock called gabbro. These are considered the most challenging mountains in Britain, and one peak, the **Inaccessible Pinnacle**, is a blade of rock that requires real rock-climbing to summit.
- **The Red Cuillin:** smoother, rounder, rosy-colored granite hills.

Ice Age glaciers then carved everything into its current dramatic shape. In the north, on the **Trotternish** peninsula, massive ancient landslides left behind the bizarre landscape of the **Old Man of Storr** (a tall pinnacle of rock standing alone on a hillside) and the **Quiraing**, a tangle of cliffs and hidden plateaus that looks like the set of a fantasy film. (It has actually been one, several times.)

---

## Chapter 3: The First People and the Stone Towers (~8,000 to 2,000 years ago)

People arrived on Skye shortly after the ice retreated. A rock shelter at **An Corran**, the same area as the dinosaur prints, holds some of the earliest evidence of human life in Scotland: hunter-gatherers who lived on shellfish, fish, and deer around 8,000 years ago.

Much later, in the Iron Age, islanders built **brochs**: round, hollow, double-walled stone towers found only in Scotland. Nobody is entirely sure whether they were fortresses, status symbols, or fortified farmhouses. **Dun Beag**, near the village of Struan, is one of the best-preserved on Skye, and you can still walk inside its circle of drystone walls, built without any mortar.

---

## Chapter 4: The Vikings Arrive (~800 to 1266 AD)

Around the year 800, Norse raiders began sailing into the Hebrides. They didn't just raid; they stayed, farmed, intermarried, and ruled. For roughly 400 years, Skye was effectively part of the Norse world, governed under the Kingdom of the Isles with ties to the King of Norway.

The Vikings left their mark mostly in **place names**. If a Skye place name ends in *-bost* (farm), *-dale* (valley), or *-ay* (island), it's probably Norse. Carbost, Glendale, and Ensay all carry this Viking DNA.

Norse rule ended after King **Haakon IV of Norway** brought a fleet to the area in 1263 and fought the Scots at the indecisive Battle of Largs. According to tradition, the village of **Kyleakin** ("Haakon's Strait") is named after the narrow channel where his fleet sheltered. In 1266, the Treaty of Perth formally handed the Hebrides to the Scottish crown.

## Chapter 5: The Age of the Clans (1300s to 1700s)

With the Vikings gone, Skye was dominated by two rival clans, both with Norse-Gaelic roots:

### The MacLeods
Their seat is **Dunvegan Castle**, which has been home to the chiefs of Clan MacLeod for around 800 years, making it one of the longest continuously inhabited castles in Scotland. Inside is the castle's most mysterious treasure: the **Fairy Flag**, a faded, fragile piece of ancient silk. Legend says it was a gift from the fairies and that, when waved in battle, it would save the clan from defeat. (Its real origin is still debated; one theory says it came from the Middle East centuries ago.)

### The MacDonalds
The MacDonalds held much of the north and south of the island, with strongholds like the now-ruined **Duntulm Castle**, perched dramatically on a cliff in Trotternish.

### The Feud
The two clans fought viciously for centuries. The darkest episode came in 1578 at **Trumpan Church**, when MacDonald raiders reportedly set fire to the church while MacLeod families were inside worshiping. The MacLeods retaliated, by tradition with the Fairy Flag in hand, and the slaughtered raiders' bodies were heaped against a stone dyke and buried by toppling it over them, a fight remembered as the "Battle of the Spoiling of the Dyke." The ruins of Trumpan Church still stand on a lonely hill overlooking the sea.

The last major clan battle on Skye was fought in 1601, at Coire na Creiche in the shadow of the Cuillin.

## Chapter 6: The Prince in a Dress (1746)

This is Skye's most famous story, and it's a great one.

In 1745, **Charles Edward Stuart**, better known as **Bonnie Prince Charlie**, led a rebellion (the Jacobite Rising) to put his family back on the British throne. It ended in crushing defeat at the **Battle of Culloden** in April 1746, and Charles became the most wanted man in Britain, with a £30,000 bounty on his head (an astronomical sum at the time).

He was hiding in the Outer Hebrides when a young woman named **Flora MacDonald** agreed to help him escape. She obtained travel papers for herself, a few companions, and an "Irish maid" named **Betty Burke**. Betty Burke was the prince, wearing a dress. Contemporary accounts say he made an awkward, unconvincing woman: too tall, with an alarmingly long stride.

They sailed through rough weather "over the sea to Skye," landed on the Trotternish coast, and made their way to Portree, where Charles said goodbye to Flora and slipped away. He eventually escaped to France and never returned to Scotland.

Flora was arrested and briefly imprisoned in the Tower of London, then released, becoming a celebrity. She later married, lived for a time in North Carolina, and eventually returned to Skye. Her grave is in the cemetery at **Kilmuir**, marked by a tall stone cross. Over a century later, the journey inspired the **"Skye Boat Song,"** which is still sung today and was used as the theme for the TV series *Outlander*.

> **Fun fact:** In 1773, the famous writer **Samuel Johnson** and his biographer **James Boswell** toured the Hebrides and actually met Flora MacDonald on Skye. Johnson wrote glowingly about her, and his words were later used on her memorial.

## Chapter 7: The Clearances and the Crofters Who Fought Back (1700s to 1880s)

After Culloden, the British government set out to break the clan system for good. Chiefs gradually stopped acting as protectors of their people and became landlords looking for profit. Across the Highlands and Islands, families who had worked the land for generations were evicted to make room for more profitable sheep farms. This period is called the **Highland Clearances**.

Thousands of Skye people left, some by force, some driven out by poverty and famine (the potato famine of the 1840s hit hard here, too). They scattered to Canada, Australia, the United States, and New Zealand. If you know anyone with roots in Nova Scotia or Cape Breton, there's a decent chance some of their ancestors sailed from places like Skye.

Those who stayed lived as **crofters**: tenants farming small plots of land with no security, at the mercy of their landlords.

### The Battle of the Braes (1882)
In 1882, crofters in **the Braes**, a district just south of Portree, refused to pay rent after losing their traditional grazing rights. When officials came to serve eviction notices, the locals, including many women, burned the papers. A force of around 50 policemen was brought in from Glasgow, and they were met by crofters armed with sticks and stones in a pitched fight.

It sounds small, but the "Battle of the Braes" made headlines across Britain. Unrest spread to other parts of Skye, and the government sent a gunboat and marines. Public sympathy swung toward the crofters, a government inquiry (the Napier Commission) investigated their conditions, and in 1886 Parliament passed the **Crofters' Holdings Act**, finally giving crofters secure tenure on their land. A small stone cairn near the Braes marks the spot today.

To see how ordinary islanders lived in this era, the **Skye Museum of Island Life** at Kilmuir preserves a cluster of traditional thatched cottages.

## Chapter 8: Skye Today

- **The bridge:** Skye was reachable only by ferry until the **Skye Bridge** opened in 1995. Its tolls were among the highest in Europe, which sparked years of protest (and arrests of locals who refused to pay) until they were abolished in 2004.
- **The language:** Scottish Gaelic is still spoken on Skye. **Sabhal Mòr Ostaig**, a Gaelic-language college in the south of the island, has been a center of the language's revival since the 1970s.
- **The whisky:** The **Talisker** distillery in Carbost has been making smoky, peppery single malt since 1830.
- **The crowds:** Skye's beauty has made it famous worldwide, and in summer its narrow roads and small villages can be overwhelmed by visitors, a modern challenge for a community that has weathered many older ones.

## Questions to Ask Your Cousin

Now that you know the backstory, here are a few things you could ask about their trip:

1. Did you see the **Old Man of Storr** or the **Quiraing**?
2. Did you visit **Dunvegan Castle** and see the Fairy Flag?
3. Did you spot any **dinosaur footprints** (or try to)?
4. Did you hear anyone speaking **Gaelic**?
5. Did you come across Flora MacDonald's grave at Kilmuir?
6. Was it actually misty the whole time?

---

*Skye is a reminder that "remote" doesn't mean "quiet." This small island has been a dinosaur playground, a volcanic battleground, a Viking outpost, a clan war zone, a royal escape route, and the birthplace of a land-rights movement. Not bad for a place you can drive across in an afternoon.*
