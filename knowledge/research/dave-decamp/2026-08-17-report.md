# The Machinery of Endless War: Inside Antiwar.com's August 17 Briefing

*A synthesis of Dave DeCamp's Antiwar News episode for Monday, August 17, 2026*

Every night, Dave DeCamp of Antiwar.com runs through the day's dispatches from Gaza, Lebanon, Iran, Yemen, Korea, and half a dozen other flashpoints. Taken individually, these stories can look like unrelated regional conflicts. Taken together — which is how DeCamp presents them — they form a single picture: a U.S. foreign policy apparatus that talks constantly about peace while consistently enabling, funding, and profiting from war. This episode is a compact illustration of that argument, moving from Gaza to Lebanon to Iran to Yemen to Korea to the defense industry to Somalia, with the same skeptical thread running through all of it — don't trust the announced deal, watch what actually happens on the ground.

## Gaza: A "Ceasefire" That Keeps Killing

The episode opens with a report from *Haaretz* that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Jared Kushner — President Trump's son-in-law and a longtime Netanyahu family friend — met and came away agreeing that Israel will continue what both sides call "targeted strikes" in Gaza. DeCamp's framing here sets the tone for the whole segment: he stresses that this isn't a new war, it's a continuation of one that supposedly ended. By his accounting, in the roughly ten months since the Gaza ceasefire deal was signed, those "targeted strikes" have killed over 1,200 people, including at least 300 children — with the health ministry reporting two more killed and fifteen wounded just in the 48 hours before this broadcast. For DeCamp, the term "ceasefire" is doing very little descriptive work.

The meeting followed Kushner's separate sit-down with Hamas officials in Egypt, where Hamas reportedly reaffirmed commitment to the U.S.-brokered framework put forward by the so-called **Board of Peace** — an oversight body tied to the Trump administration's Gaza plan. Israel, by contrast, is the party publicly rejecting elements of that framework. According to Israeli media reports of the Kushner-Netanyahu meeting (which ran three to four hours), Israeli officials insist there will be no troop withdrawal until Hamas is *fully* disarmed — even though the original U.S.-announced deal called for withdrawal and disarmament to happen **simultaneously**. DeCamp notes that the Board of Peace has already walked back that simultaneity after meeting with Netanyahu, and that Netanyahu is rejecting the revised version too. As DeCamp puts it, "it's not a good sign."

He then plays a clip of Kushner's post-meeting Fox News interview, in which Kushner ties reconstruction funding to demilitarization, promises Israel won't be restricted from acting on "imminent threats," and lays out a timeline — weapons handovers within 30 days, tunnel destruction within 60–90. DeCamp's reading of Kushner's key line is the crux of his argument: Kushner frames the arrangement as a "win-win" for Israel because *either* Hamas disarms (eliminating a security threat) *or* it doesn't, in which case Israel gets American backing to "finish the job." DeCamp finds this framing alarming on its face — a plan is structured so that failure and success both justify continued Israeli military action — and draws an explicit parallel to Kushner's earlier role in the run-up to the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran, where, in DeCamp's view, negotiations served largely as public-relations cover: an effort to demonstrate that "we tried everything" before resorting to force. He suggests the same script may now be running on Gaza.

He's also careful to push back on the idea that Israel's strikes are defensive responses to active threats. Since the ceasefire, DeCamp says, Hamas has launched no attacks on Israel, and Israeli casualties in Gaza during that period stem from friendly fire and clashes involving trapped IDF units, not organized attacks — five Israeli soldiers dead altogether, versus the Palestinian toll above. He views the "imminent threat" framing used to justify ongoing strikes as largely a media and political fiction.

On the personnel present at the Kushner-Netanyahu meeting, DeCamp is blunt: alongside Kushner and Netanyahu were Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's envoy for Gaza, former UK Prime Minister Tony Blair, and U.S. Ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee — a lineup he calls "very bleak" for anyone hoping for a resolution favorable to Palestinians. One concrete outcome did emerge: agreement to allow sanitation infrastructure work in Gaza to prevent disease outbreaks, while broader reconstruction remains blocked. A preliminary plan was also floated in which Hamas would hand weapons to an international stabilization force commanded by U.S. Army Major General Jasper Jeffers as Israeli troops withdraw — but DeCamp is skeptical this will materialize, citing Israeli officials' own skepticism about disarmament and reports of "no breakthrough."

## Lebanon: The Escalation Moves Next Door

DeCamp connects Gaza directly to a second front: southern Lebanon, where Israeli forces pushed further into Lebanese territory on Monday, striking a location in Wadi al-Hujair and advancing ground vehicles into the Ali al-Taher area — notably outside the zone Israel had previously occupied. Israeli Channel 12 reported troops were told to prepare for several days of combat, anticipating resistance. The push follows a weekend of heavy strikes that killed at least 11 people, including in the bombing of a house full of civilians.

DeCamp treats this less as a separate story than as evidence of a rotating pattern: Israel scales back operations in Gaza somewhat while intensifying them in Lebanon, and vice versa — the two fronts trading off rather than either one actually ending. He notes that, according to reporting, the U.S. has given Israel a "green light" to invade and seize the town in question, which he juxtaposes against Trump's public statements about wanting the broader war to end. His conclusion: "I just don't believe it."

## The West Bank: Settler Violence as Routine, Not Exception

DeCamp highlights a report from *Middle East Eye* on an Israeli settler shooting a Palestinian nurse, Muhammad Mutur, in the back near Hebron over the weekend, severing his spinal cord. Another man was shot in the shoulder. Mutur's uncle told the outlet that settlers had spread through the area firing live ammunition and blocked ambulances from reaching the wounded. DeCamp cites the family's account that settlers "aimed to kill" — attributing that characterization to the family, not confirming it independently — and notes that shooting people in the back, when they pose no threat, is a recurring pattern in these incidents.

His broader point is about media framing: with international attention concentrated on other Israeli actions, he argues settler violence across the West Bank gets treated as anomalous, isolated incidents — a framing he attributes partly to figures like Huckabee — when in his view it's a constant, systemic feature of the occupation rather than an exception to it.

## Iran: Threats, Sanctions, and a Warning Sign From Tehran

The Iran segment opens with Trump repeating a threat — first made in May — to bomb Oman "the s*** out of them" if Muscat "gets in the way" of U.S. objectives, this time in the context of Omani-mediated talks between Oman and Iran. DeCamp flags the irony: senior U.S. officials, including Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, have simultaneously expressed hope that those very talks succeed. Oman has long played the role of neutral broker in the region — DeCamp argues that's precisely the kind of relationship threats like this jeopardize.

Trump also told Fox News reporter Trey Yingst that Iran should raise "the white flag of surrender," and that he's in "no hurry" and has "no time schedule," explicitly denying that the upcoming midterms factor into his approach. DeCamp reads this as a signal that the administration intends to let sanctions and a naval blockade grind down Iran's economy over time. But he pairs that with a countervailing signal from Tehran: Iranian officials and state media, in his account, are indicating they won't simply absorb the pressure indefinitely and may move to some kind of offensive response. He says he "wouldn't be surprised" to see Iran act.

A related story: Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi denied Tehran was behind a drone attack on the office of Masrour Barzani, prime minister of Iraq's Kurdistan Regional Government, calling the incident a possible "false flag" meant to "sow discord" between Iran and its Kurdish neighbors. DeCamp notes Iran has attacked Kurdish groups in Iraq before — including Iranian Kurdish opposition groups the U.S. has reportedly considered arming — but rarely, to his knowledge, targeted the KRG government itself, with which Tehran has kept relatively cordial relations. He also flags a separate, unconfirmed *Axios* report that Masrour Barzani's cousin, KRG President Nechirvan Barzani, helped facilitate a back-channel message from Iran's IRGC expressing support for Iranian negotiators — a claim Trump seemed to confirm publicly and that an IRGC official then denied as "delusional." DeCamp treats this as murky and unresolved, guessing that if any such channel existed, it was a one-off message rather than sustained contact.

## Yemen: A Second War Reignites, With Consequences for Global Oil

DeCamp describes the Yemen conflict as having clearly resumed in earnest. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree announced strikes on a Saudi-linked landing ship and four escort vessels in the Red Sea near Mocha, plus a strike on what he called an arms depot in Marib province. Saudi Arabia has not confirmed the ship attack; Saudi-backed Yemeni government officials told Reuters the vessel had been out of service for maintenance and was likely unmanned. DeCamp treats reporting from both sides with caution, noting that with minimal international media coverage, much of what's known comes from the belligerents' own competing claims. Renewed fighting is also reported in Taiz and Marib, provinces that saw heavy combat before a 2022 ceasefire.

He traces this escalation back to a Saudi strike on Sana'a International Airport the previous month, after which hostilities have steadily built. Notably, he says, sustained heavy Saudi airstrikes on Yemen — the kind that characterized the brutal Saudi-led war of the 2010s — haven't yet returned, and he flags that such a campaign would require renewed U.S. support, which would pull Washington directly back into the conflict.

The knock-on effect lands in the oil markets: citing a Bloomberg report, DeCamp explains that Saudi Arabia's Aramco has directed Asian refiners to collect crude from the Red Sea port of Yanbu — itself a target of recent Houthi strikes — and that at least two Asian refiners have instead requested pickup next month from Egypt's Sidi Kerir port instead, avoiding the Red Sea route entirely. For DeCamp, this is a concrete data point showing the Yemeni blockade is inflicting real economic costs on Saudi Arabia and reshaping global oil logistics, not just a regional military story.

## North Korea: A Rare Opening, Viewed Skeptically

In a lower-key segment, DeCamp addresses Trump's announcement of scaled-back joint military exercises with South Korea (the "Ulchi Freedom Shield" drills) and the South Korean government's response — a statement from President Lee Jae-myung's office expressing hope that Trump's rapport with Kim Jong-un could lead to "meaningful dialogue" between Washington and Pyongyang.

DeCamp pushes back on Western commentary framing this as a rupture with Seoul, noting that most alarmed reactions he saw were sourced to Western analysts rather than the South Korean government itself, which put out a hopeful statement. He connects the move to context largely missing from that commentary: President Lee had proposed talks with the North the day before Trump's announcement, and pursuing diplomacy with Pyongyang has been a stated priority of Lee's administration since his inauguration. DeCamp admits his own initial read overstated the connection — he first assumed Trump was scaling back *all* joint exercises rather than just the current round — but maintains the underlying point: this isn't as sudden or unilateral as it's been portrayed, and it echoes the dynamic during Trump's first term, when a South Korean president likewise seeking talks with the North helped set the stage for Trump's own outreach to Kim. Trump separately revived rhetoric about South Korea paying more for the U.S. military presence there, reversing arrangements DeCamp says the Biden administration had dropped.

DeCamp remains guarded about Trump's motives throughout — "I don't trust his motives, you never know what any of this means" — while still treating renewed U.S.–North Korea contact as, on balance, a potential opening rather than a threat.

## The Business of War: Raytheon's $22.9 Billion Signal

If there's a thesis statement buried in this episode, it comes in the Raytheon segment. The company has secured a $22.9 billion Pentagon contract to scale Tomahawk missile production from roughly 60 units per year to 1,000 per year — a response to the roughly 1,000 Tomahawks (nearly a third of the pre-war U.S. inventory, per the Center for Strategic and International Studies) fired into Iran during the recent U.S.-Israeli war, compressed into just a couple of months.

DeCamp uses this to make a point he returns to often: stockpile depletion is a real strategic problem for U.S. planners, but it is simultaneously a windfall for defense contractors — "one man's stockpile depletion is another man's payday." He connects this to a broader critique of the revolving door between military leadership and the arms industry, where generals who oversee these campaigns often go on to board seats or think-tank positions funded by the same weapons makers — a dynamic he argues normalizes a fairly blatant conflict of interest that rarely gets named directly in mainstream coverage.

He grounds the human cost of this production directly in the story of the strike on the Shahid Rajaei Elementary School in Manbij (referred to in the transcript by its Persian name), which killed at least 156 people, including 120 children, during the first day of the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran. Evidence indicates the school was hit — three times — with Tomahawk missiles, the same weapon system now getting the $22.9 billion production boost. DeCamp treats this juxtaposition as central rather than incidental: the same missile responsible for one of the war's worst civilian casualty events is the one now driving a massive new defense contract.

## Somalia: The Quiet Cost of a US-Backed Government

The episode closes with a story that, on its face, seems disconnected from the rest: clashes in Somalia's Southwest State between federal troops and forces loyal to an ousted regional leader in the state capital, Baidoa. Somalia's federal military claims al-Shabaab was behind a recent assault that killed more than ten people and wounded twenty, per the *Somali Guardian* — a claim DeCamp says he can't verify and that doesn't appear to be echoed by the ousted leader's own side.

DeCamp's interest is in the government itself. Somalia's federal government — the one the U.S., EU, and African Union effectively installed after backing Ethiopia's 2006 ouster of the Islamic Courts Union (a coalition of Muslim groups that briefly held power in Mogadishu after driving out CIA-backed warlords) — has since clashed repeatedly with regional administrations, including Jubaland and Puntland, not just Baidoa. He traces al-Shabaab's origins to the radical offshoot that emerged from the Islamic Courts Union's collapse, framing the current unrest as a long-tail consequence of that original U.S.-backed intervention: two decades later, Washington is still tied to a government whose legitimacy is contested by its own regions, and which current President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud's push for new election laws has only inflamed further.

## The Throughline

Strip away the geography and a consistent argument emerges across all nine stories: publicly announced diplomacy (Gaza reconstruction talks, Iran-Oman mediation, Korea outreach) keeps running up against military facts on the ground (continued Gaza strikes, the Lebanon offensive, threats against Oman, unresolved Yemen escalation) that undercut or contradict it. Where DeCamp sees a pattern is in *why* that gap persists — not simply confusion or bad luck, but a set of incentives: allied governments (Israel, Saudi-backed Yemeni factions) that benefit from continued conflict, an arms industry that profits directly from it (Raytheon's contract, purchased with the same missiles that killed children in Iran), and U.S.-backed governments (Somalia) that remain dependent on continued military and political support regardless of local legitimacy. Diplomacy, in his telling, functions as often as a public-relations backdrop for continued military action as it does a genuine off-ramp — a claim he draws most explicitly around Kushner's dual role in the Iran and Gaza negotiations.

DeCamp closes the episode pointing listeners to Antiwar.com's opinion section — pieces from Ron Paul, Jose Niño, and others — and to the outlet's fundraiser, noting he'll be off starting Thursday for a week's break before returning to the next day's news.
