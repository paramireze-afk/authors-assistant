# East of Eden: The Novel That Refuses to Be Small

John Steinbeck's *East of Eden* is the kind of book a lot of writers today would be a little embarrassed to write. It goes straight at the biggest questions there are (good, evil, choice, original sin) and it does it with total earnestness. No irony, no shrug, no wink at the reader.

That's what makes it feel out of step. A lot of contemporary art leans ironic, nihilistic, or quietly assumes morality is relative. *East of Eden* assumes none of that.

And yet it's not a difficult book. It's a page-turner. So much happens plot-wise that some critics have called it melodramatic or even lowbrow. That combination, huge philosophical ambition plus pulpy readability, is rare in prestige literary fiction. It's also why the book splits people. Some call it the Great American Novel and Steinbeck's true masterpiece, ahead of *The Grapes of Wrath*. Others call it clumsy, preachy, or pretentious.

The interesting question isn't just who's right. It's what that split says about the kind of fiction we reward now.

---

## The Story: Cain and Abel, Twice

On the surface, the novel follows two families in California's Salinas Valley across several generations. Its skeleton is the story of Cain and Abel.

- **First generation:** brothers Adam and Charles. One seems good, the other dark.
- **Second generation:** Adam's sons, Cal and Aron. Again, one brother is loved and the other feels rejected.

Cal is the Cain figure, wrestling with jealousy and his own darker impulses. Hanging over the whole book is one question: **does the pattern have to repeat?** In Genesis, Cain kills Abel. Are people locked into that script, or can they become something else?

Described like that, it sounds like a dry allegory with characters as chess pieces. It isn't. The characters are vivid. Cal especially is fully human: dark in a lot of ways, but also desperately not wanting to be. Steinbeck describes him as packed full of every good and every bad thing, and that seems to be his view of people in general. Everyone carries the potential for both.

## A Book With Two Natures

*East of Eden* runs on two tracks at once:

1. **Realism.** The characters feel like real, complicated people (with one possible exception, more on her below).
2. **Allegory.** The characters are also part of a bigger mythic pattern where the past is very much alive, and the book is not subtle about it.

That second track is where the novel gets almost aggressively unfashionable. The narrator regularly stops the plot to editorialize. At one point he declares that humanity really has only one story: the endless struggle between good and evil inside each of us. Modern writing workshops drill "show, don't tell" into everyone. Steinbeck tells, loudly, and often.

Characters also break into long philosophical conversations in a way that recalls Dostoevsky.

## Timshel: "Thou Mayest"

The most famous of those conversations is the heart of the book.

Lee, Adam's household servant and one of the novel's great characters, gets curious about the passage in Genesis where God speaks to Cain about sin. He compares translations:

- One version reads like a **promise**: you *will* rule over sin.
- Another reads like a **command**: you *must* rule over sin.

Then Lee digs into the Hebrew word, *timshel*, which he reads as **"thou mayest."** Not a promise, not an order, but a *choice*. The way is open, and the responsibility lands squarely on the person.

(There's an ongoing debate about whether Steinbeck got the Hebrew translation or spelling right. That's beside the point here. What matters is what the idea does inside the novel.)

That idea is the core of the book: **we aren't doomed to inherit sin. We get to choose, and what we choose matters.**

## A Book That Doesn't Make You Feel Small

This is maybe the most striking thing about *East of Eden*. A lot of modern thinking is reductionist: we're just particles, tiny blips in an indifferent universe. This book goes the other way. It insists that what an ordinary person does can carry cosmic weight.

Lee says as much. The idea of *timshel* gives a person stature, real dignity, because even someone weak, compromised, or guilty of terrible things still holds that great choice. It echoes St. Augustine's picture of humans as something in between beasts and angels.

So why are books like this so rare now? It's not only that the novel draws unironically on the Bible, which isn't exactly in vogue. It's that it takes for granted that:

- good and evil are real,
- they matter, and
- an ordinary person's life can be as big as Genesis.

And it gets there not through a relativistic "whatever you feel about yourself is what counts" philosophy, but through a sincere acceptance of good and evil and where humans stand between them.

People noticed. The novelist William T. Vollmann singled it out among Steinbeck's books for its sincerity. Steinbeck himself called it his favorite. He said a writer really has only one book in him, and for him this was it. Everything before was practice.

## In Defense of Being Loud

Yes, the narrator interjects. Yes, it's not subtle. That doesn't have to be a flaw.

Subtlety is a real aesthetic virtue, but it can also be a convenient hiding place. A writer can stay vague and avoid committing to anything. *East of Eden* doesn't hide. It's loud and ambitious, and if it tips into excess while chasing something this weighty, that's a risk worth taking.

Steinbeck even framed the book less as a novel than as a *history*. He said it was tightly built but meant to feel as shapeless as history does, while noting that history only looks formless until you step back far enough to see the pattern.

## The Fair Criticisms

It's not all praise. There are legitimate reasons someone might bounce off this book:

- **Slow stretches.** Most of it moves fast, with chapters that end on cliffhangers. But the narrator's essay-like chapters and long scenery descriptions can drag, depending on your taste. Some of it does drag.
- **A huge cast.** Beyond Adam's family there's a whole second family, the Hamiltons, with chapters for nearly every member. They add texture and counterpoint, but you won't care about all of them equally.
- **Cathy.** The big one, covered next.

## Cathy, and the Problem of Evil

Earlier I said nearly every character feels real. The possible exception is Cathy, Adam's wife and the mother of his sons. She's diabolical, likely a psychopath, and the narrator essentially calls her a monster. Steinbeck said she was meant to represent Satan. The narrator even admits readers may not believe in her, but insists she exists.

A lot of readers find her one-dimensional. Today we know a lot about psychology and why people do harmful things, so we tend to bristle at any character who's simply *evil*. That's an understandable reaction.

But it's worth holding up against Allan Bloom's *The Closing of the American Mind* (1987), a long, cranky argument that Western culture and universities were slipping into a relativism that passed itself off as tolerance. Bloom describes asking his students to name someone evil. They instantly said Hitler. After that? Almost nothing. A few used to name Nixon, then stopped.

Bloom's diagnosis: his students didn't really believe evil existed. They lived in a world full of terrible acts but looked away from them. They seemed to assume bad deeds come from people who just needed the right therapy, that there are evil *acts* but no evil *people*. And without a sense of how low people can go, Bloom argued, you also lose a sense of how high they can rise. Life loses its gravity.

The honest takeaway here isn't that Bloom is right. It's a question. Many of us grew up in a relativistic milieu where the word "evil" feels uncomfortable. Is that a gain, or a loss? Has our world gotten flatter because we stopped taking good and evil seriously? Are we less aware of both the depths and the heights?

Seen that way, Cathy isn't just a weak character. She's a challenge to a modern reflex.

## Why It Matters Now

Steinbeck said he wrote *East of Eden* for his two young sons. He wanted to hand them what he considered maybe the greatest story of all: the story of good and evil, strength and weakness, love and hate, beauty and ugliness.

It's hard to think of anything weightier. And very few novels even try to take that on directly, let alone with this much sincerity. At minimum, the book deserves credit for attempting what most authors won't.

Add in genuinely good, old-fashioned storytelling, and you get a book that a lot of readers (the reviewer behind this piece included) put in their all-time top five.

Being out of step with the modern world isn't a strike against *East of Eden*. It's the whole reason to read it now.

## Questions to Sit With

1. Does the narrator's "telling" weaken the novel, or is it part of what makes it work?
2. Is Cathy a failure of characterization, or a deliberate refusal to explain evil away?
3. Has a relativistic outlook made our moral lives richer or flatter?
4. Is *timshel* (real freedom to choose) believable, or a comforting idea?
