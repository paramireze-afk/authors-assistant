# Cannon Fodder and Custodial Parents

### Michael Yon on combat, memory, and the coming draft

---

## Executive Summary

This transcript is a single, unbroken monologue from Michael Yon — a combat correspondent and former U.S. Army Special Forces soldier known for years of embedded reporting in Iraq and Afghanistan. It opens with a Facebook memorial notification marking the anniversary of a friend's death, and moves from there into a chain of firsthand memory, prediction, and political argument.

The throughline is this: Yon has spent years in close physical proximity to young Americans dying in combat, and he treats that proximity as the basis for a warning — that a draft is coming, that it will fall on people he calls "our tribe," and that the institutions asking for sacrifice (churches, the presidency, the financial system) are not acting in the interest of the people they send. He argues that the correct response is not individual draft evasion but collective, coordinated withdrawal of support — money, attendance, and loyalty — from any institution pushing toward war.

The material below is organized to keep his combat testimony, his predictions, and his political interpretations distinguishable, while preserving the argument as he makes it.

---

## Part I — The Firsthand Testimony (Combat Memory)

This is the anchor of the transcript: direct, sensory recollection from a correspondent who says he did "a tremendous amount of combat."

- **Bryce Scott**, killed in Afghanistan at 22, shot in the face while on a rooftop. Yon was present. Another soldier nearby was hit in the ribs in the same engagement — the SAPI side plate stopped the round, but Yon recalls the visible bruising and the soldier's shock afterward.
- **Chazray Clark**, Yon's tent-mate, stepped on a bomb at night during a CH-47 helicopter insertion into a Taliban-heavy village in Kandahar. He lost both legs and an arm. Yon says he was filming when the blast occurred and that debris was landing around them in the dark. He describes the medevac response as "very late."
- He connects Chazray's death to **Red Air**, the term for delayed or denied medevac response in Afghanistan, which he says he reported on extensively — not primarily as a story about Chazray specifically, but as part of a broader pattern he documented with off-the-record help from Air Force pararescue ("Pedro") and Army medevac personnel. He credits that reporting with contributing to more medevac helicopters being sent to theater.
- He recalls a separate incident in Iraq in which a soldier was shot and killed through the same side-plate area that saved the soldier's life in the Chazray episode.
- He describes routinely helping carry the wounded and the dead: "I would often help the soldiers when these things would happen, carrying casualties. I'm not going to watch our guys die."
- He draws a hard line between "war correspondents," whom he says are common, and "combat correspondents" — journalists with actual photographic or video documentation of firefights — whom he says are extremely rare. He says he deliberately limited his time embedded with special forces units specifically because so much of what happens there can't be reported, and there was little point being shot at without being able to write about it.

## Part II — "Infantry Means Infant"

Yon builds an argument around the word itself: that "infantry" shares a root with "infant," and that foot soldiers were historically the ones judged too young or too low-status to be trusted with a horse. From this he draws the phrase that anchors the whole piece — infantry as, in his words, "basically servants, war slaves."

He supports this with a secondhand quotation he attributes to General James Mattis, describing officers and NCOs as "custodial parents" to their infantry — using a real military figure's language to argue that the soldiers doing the actual fighting are, structurally, still children being cared for and sent forward by others.

This framing is what carries the transcript from personal grief into political warning: Bryce was 22, Chazray was 24, and Yon treats their deaths as evidence of a system built to consume the young.

---

## Part III — The Draft: Prediction and Argument

**Prediction:** Yon states, without qualification, that a draft is coming — "like a freight train." He does not cite legislation or policy in the transcript; this is his own forecast based on where he sees the broader trajectory heading.

**Supporting reasoning:**
- He points to the gap between draft age and full civic adulthood — draftable at 17–18 historically, but unable to vote, drink, or in many states buy a handgun until 21 — as evidence that the young are treated as expendable before they're treated as full citizens.
- He cites Scott Helvenston, the youngest known U.S. Navy SEAL, whom he says he personally knew growing up in Winter Haven, Florida, as proof that teenagers are capable of extraordinary things — not to undercut his "child soldier" framing, but to show that youth and capability aren't opposites in his account; the same young people he says are exploited are also, in his telling, exceptional.
- He argues that resistance to a draft is a moral stance, not cowardice, and says that if he'd faced Vietnam-era conscription into a war he didn't believe in, he would have chosen either special forces or Canada — never service he thought was wrong.

**Speculation on Ukraine:** Yon claims Ukrainian men are being forcibly taken off the streets for military service, and raises the possibility — without offering evidence in the transcript — that some may not survive to reach the front at all. He frames this as a preview of what conscription can become once a state decides it needs bodies.

**Prediction on who fills a future draft:** He predicts that any American draft would eventually be filled out with foreign nationals who don't speak English or understand the Constitution, "trained to come kill people... come kill Americans." This is presented as where he believes the current trajectory leads, not as something that has happened.

## Part IV — Institutions, Money, and the Case for Collective Withdrawal

This is the moral center of the transcript, and the part Yon spends the most energy on: not just naming what's wrong, but arguing for a specific, collective response.

**The core argument:** don't just dodge the draft individually — withdraw support, as a group, from every institution that pushes toward it. He frames this explicitly as a "we," not an "I": *"we have to work through this together because the young people are part of our tribe... they're our people and so they are our future. So we collectively have to stand up against the draft."*

**Churches:** Yon calls out congregations and pastors who preach unconditional financial and political support for Israel, and argues that money should stop flowing to them. His instruction is direct — tell the preacher plainly that the money isn't coming, and stop attending. He frames these congregations as "war churches," arguing that a church steering its members toward funding wars abroad has stepped outside its purpose. This is a Christian Zionism critique made from inside a self-identified Christian, patriotic frame — he isn't rejecting faith, he's rejecting what he sees as its political capture.

**The presidency:** He argues that Donald Trump is functioning in the interest of Israel rather than the United States, and states this as settled in his own mind rather than something that changed with the current term — he says he sees no real difference between Trump now and Trump at first election. He also raises, as his own speculation rather than a claim of fact, the possibility that Trump may not be fully in control of himself at this point.

**Digital money:** Yon connects the draft warning to the shift toward digital currency, suggesting that once money is fully digital, the leverage available to a state — and the difficulty of opting out of its demands — changes fundamentally. He doesn't develop this into a full argument in the transcript, but treats it as part of the same coercive apparatus as conscription: control over money and control over bodies as two sides of the same system.

**Financial cost of speaking:** He states plainly that this argument will cost him money and supporters — that people have already cancelled subscriptions to his work over less — and says he accepts that cost: "I will sleep."

## Part V — Widening War, Famine, and the "Process War"

Yon extends his argument geographically and temporally. On the geographic side, he describes a war he sees expanding outward from Gaza into Lebanon and beyond, and predicts that the expansion will produce "massive famines" — a humanitarian consequence he treats as effectively locked in given the trajectory he describes, rather than a remote possibility.

On the temporal side, he introduces the idea of a **"process war"** — his term for a conflict that is not tied to any single administration or leader, but continues underneath whoever happens to be in office. His argument is that Trump's eventual departure won't change the trajectory, because people will simply transfer their hope to "some new guy" and the same pattern will repeat. In his framing, the war is structural and ongoing rather than a policy choice any single president is making or could unmake.

**On Israel, Gaza, Lebanon, Iran, and Armenia:** Yon makes a series of specific claims in this section — that Israel is centrally responsible for pushing the draft in order to fill American ranks with foreign conscripts; that Israel has armed groups responsible for killing Armenian Christians while also striking churches in Gaza; that strikes on schools in Gaza and Iran were deliberate rather than accidental, based on his own reading of the pattern and his combat experience with how militaries operate; and that the October 7 attack near Gaza may have produced a higher death toll than initially reported, which he frames as intentional. He also references the 1967 USS Liberty incident in passing, without elaboration, as part of the same pattern he's describing. These are his claims and interpretations, made with the same certainty he brings to his combat memories, though without the same firsthand access — worth noting for anyone using this document to look further into the sourcing behind each one.

**On vaccines:** Yon repeatedly refers to COVID-19 vaccination as "the jab," argues that people who took it were manipulated the same way people are manipulated into supporting a draft, and states that people are dying from it. This is his stated belief, delivered with the same rhetorical structure as his draft warning — an institution asking for sacrifice, dressed up as care.

**On propaganda:** He references the book *Rape of the Mind* (a study of coercive persuasion) as a framework for understanding how populations are talked into supporting things against their own interest — whether that's a vaccine, a war, or a draft. He treats propaganda as the connective tissue between all of his separate warnings: the same mechanism that gets people to take a shot, he argues, is the mechanism that will get people to hand over their kids.

---

## Part VI — "Our Tribe": The Collective Framing

Yon is explicit that his argument is not about individual self-preservation. He rejects the idea that young people facing a hypothetical draft should simply "figure it out" on their own: *"it's not like we got okay you figure it out... No, they're our people."* The obligation he describes runs in the other direction — those who are older, who already served, who already carry the memory of Bryce and Chazray, owe something to the generation coming up behind them.

This is why his proposed response to the draft is institutional and collective rather than individual: withdraw money from churches, withdraw support from politicians, refuse the framing that resistance is cowardice. The moral weight of the argument depends on this tribal framing — the young aren't an abstraction to be pitied, they're kin whose defense is a shared responsibility.

## Tensions Worth Sitting With

Rather than treating these as contradictions to resolve, they're worth holding as places where the argument gets more complicated:

- **Child and exceptional at once.** Yon calls 18-year-olds essentially children, unfit to be trusted with full civic rights — and in the same breath holds up a 17-year-old friend who passed SEAL selection as proof of what young people are capable of. The tension isn't necessarily a flaw; it may reflect a real distinction he's making between capability and the moral right to be spent by others.
- **Volunteers who were still, in his telling, exploited.** He's clear that Bryce, Chazray, and many others he served with volunteered after 9/11 out of genuine conviction, not conscription — and he holds real respect for that choice. That sits alongside his broader claim that the infantry system is built to consume the young regardless of how willingly they enter it. Both things can be true in his account: a volunteer can still be part of a system that treats him as expendable.
- **Certainty calibrated differently across topics.** His combat memory is hedged and precise — he corrects himself, admits when he's forgotten a name or isn't sure of a detail. His geopolitical claims are delivered with the same confidence but without the same firsthand vantage point. Whether that's a weakness in the argument or simply the difference between reporting and forecasting is worth the reader's own judgment.
- **What changed his mind about 9/11.** He says he believed 9/11 was "legit" at the time, and respected those who enlisted because of it — and separately says he no longer holds that view. The transcript doesn't explain the shift, which leaves a real gap for anyone wanting to understand his reasoning.
