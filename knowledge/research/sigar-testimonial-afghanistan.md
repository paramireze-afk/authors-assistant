# SIGAR Testimony 23-22-TY — John F. Sopko, April 19, 2023
### "Hearing Before the Committee on Oversight and Accountability, U.S. House of Representatives"

> Source: SIGAR-23-22-TY, testimony of Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction John F. Sopko before the House Committee on Oversight and Accountability, April 19, 2023. Government work, public domain. This note is built directly from the uploaded text.

## 1. Document Purpose and Context

This is Sopko's 27th congressional testimony since his appointment as SIGAR roughly 11 years earlier, and his 12th before this specific committee. It was delivered in response to a September 10, 2021 bipartisan request from the committee — made days after the final US withdrawal — asking SIGAR to investigate the causes of the Afghan government's collapse. The testimony has two parts: (1) an update on SIGAR's *current, ongoing* oversight work regarding post-withdrawal US assistance to Afghanistan, and (2) a synthesis of findings from **five SIGAR reports** examining why the Afghan government and security forces collapsed in August 2021.

**[EXTRACT] SIGAR (Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction)** — created by Congress in 2008, six years into the war, specifically because of "growing concerns about gaps in overseeing a massive multi-agency government effort." Its founding logic, per the testimony: *"a whole-of-government effort requires whole-of-government oversight"* — so Congress gave SIGAR authority spanning all agencies spending Afghanistan funds, not just one department.

**[EXTRACT] John F. Sopko** — Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction since 2012; author of this testimony.

## 2. SIGAR's Underlying Explanatory Model

Reading across both halves of the testimony, SIGAR's explanatory model is not a single-cause account — it's a layered structure that separates **long-term, structural preconditions** from **short-term, precipitating triggers**, and insists the two must be read together. This distinction is explicit in the report's own framing and is the single most important organizing idea in the document:

> "These factors, some of which dated to the very beginning of the U.S. reconstruction effort... created the conditions by which more recent precipitating factors acted as accelerants that ultimately led to the collapse."

This "conditions + accelerants" model recurs as SIGAR's core analytical move, applied twice — once to the government's collapse, once to the security forces' collapse — with parallel five-and-six-factor structures. The model has several distinct mechanisms worth understanding on their own terms:

### 2.1 Structural preconditions (decades in the making)
SIGAR lists eight long-running, systemic problems that it says predate any single administration's withdrawal decision:
- Constantly shifting US strategy
- Chronic underestimation of the time needed, paired with **spend-driven timelines** — pressure to disburse money fast, which SIGAR explicitly links to *increased corruption and reduced program effectiveness*
- Built infrastructure/institutions that were not sustainable
- Counterproductive civilian/military personnel policies
- Persistent insecurity undermining all other efforts
- Poor US understanding of Afghan context, and failure to tailor programs accordingly
- Insufficient monitoring and evaluation
- The Afghan government's centralization and "endemic corruption," which undermined its own legitimacy

This list functions in the report as the **substrate** — the condition of the system before the acute crisis — onto which the short-term factors act.

### 2.2 Precipitating factors as accelerants
SIGAR's metaphor is explicit: short-term factors "acted as accelerants." This means SIGAR does not treat the 2021 collapse as a sudden, unpredictable event, nor as caused solely by the Doha Agreement or the withdrawal decision. Instead, withdrawal is treated as the spark applied to material that had been made highly flammable over two decades. This is the report's single most load-bearing interpretive claim, and it is used to organize both the government-collapse and security-forces-collapse sections.

### 2.3 Dependency as a designed-in vulnerability
A second major mechanism, most visible in the ANDSF section, is that the United States built the Afghan security forces "as a mirror image of itself" rather than around Afghan capacity — creating **structural dependencies** in three specific domains SIGAR names directly:
- **Resource management** — Afghan commanders often did not know what supplies existed or where, partly because the US-designed inventory system (**[EXTRACT] CoreIMS**) was inaccessible to Afghan personnel without US contractors.
- **Maintenance** — most visibly in the shift from Soviet-legacy **Mi-17 helicopters** (which Afghans could maintain themselves) to US-made **UH-60 Black Hawks** (whose maintenance "depended almost entirely on non-Afghan contractors"). **[EXTRACT] Afghan Air Force UH-60 transition**
- **Military leadership/command structures** — modeled on US command patterns rather than Afghan practice or prior experience.

The mechanism SIGAR describes here is important as a general pattern: dependency wasn't a side effect of the program, it was built into its design choices, which meant that *withdrawal of the dependency-creating actor (US contractors/advisors) was functionally equivalent to withdrawing a load-bearing structural element*, not just a support service.

### 2.4 Contractor withdrawal as a distinct causal chain
SIGAR traces a specific causal chain, worth preserving as a reusable mechanism: contractor withdrawal → loss of aircraft maintenance capacity → reliance on an overstretched Afghan Air Force for ground resupply that should have moved by truck → shortages of "ammunition, food, water" at isolated units → collapse of morale and combat capability at the unit level. SIGAR states this was foreseeable: SIGAR's own 2021 High-Risk List had already warned that "the withdrawal of contractors from Afghanistan 'may leave the AAF and its Special Mission Wing without vital support.'" This is presented as an instance of a broader pattern in the document — SIGAR repeatedly notes that specific 2021 outcomes were *previously and explicitly warned about* in earlier SIGAR reports (the ghost soldiers issue, the payroll system, contractor dependency), reinforcing SIGAR's institutional argument that the collapse was not just foreseeable in hindsight but was actually foreseen and reported at the time.

### 2.5 Corruption as "the glue that held the Afghan government together — until it didn't"
This phrase, used verbatim in the report's conclusion to the ANDSF section, is SIGAR's own summary formulation and deserves to be preserved as such. The model here is counterintuitive and important: corruption is not framed simply as leakage or waste, but as a **functional political mechanism** — patronage and graft were part of what held together coalitions of power-holders, commanders, and officials. The implication SIGAR draws is that anti-corruption reform was never merely a technical fix, because it required "the cooperation and political will of Afghan elites who most benefitted from" the corrupt system — i.e., asking the system's beneficiaries to dismantle the mechanism that bound the system together.

**[EXTRACT] "Ghost soldiers"** — a specific corruption mechanism given sustained treatment: payroll fraud in which salaries were claimed for army/police personnel who had deserted, been killed, or never existed. SIGAR cites former Finance Minister Khalid Payenda's estimate that at least 80 percent of the nominal 300,000-person ANDSF were ghost soldiers. Framed as both a financial drain and a **data-integrity problem** — it made the true fighting strength of the ANDSF unknowable, including in its final weeks.

### 2.6 Signaling, credibility, and perceived intent as a causal category
A distinct and recurring mechanism in the government-collapse section is that *perception of US intent*, independent of the facts on the ground, is treated by SIGAR as a causal variable in its own right. Two examples:
- Afghan officials, having watched years of announced-but-unrealized withdrawal intentions across multiple administrations, did not believe the 2021 withdrawal was real until very late — SIGAR frames this as a rational inference from a documented pattern of prior "vacillation," not simple denial.
- The secrecy of parts of the Doha Agreement (classified provisions, undisclosed verbal side-agreements) created an information vacuum that SIGAR says the Taliban filled with disinformation about a purported secret US-Taliban arrangement — and SIGAR explicitly notes an expert's judgment that "the misinformation appeared more damaging than what was actually in the agreement." This is a reusable point: the *absence of authoritative information*, not just the substance of an agreement, functioned as a causal factor in the ANDSF's morale collapse.

### 2.7 Exclusion from negotiations as a legitimacy-eroding mechanism
The Afghan government's exclusion from direct US-Taliban talks is treated by SIGAR as structurally, not just symbolically, damaging: it "bolstered the Taliban's argument that the Afghan government were U.S. puppets," reduced the Republic's own incentive/capacity to negotiate a settlement, and shifted the Taliban's strategy from negotiation toward battlefield resolution once its core demand (US withdrawal) was already secured through a channel that bypassed Kabul entirely.

### 2.8 Centralization and personalized power as an amplifying vulnerability
SIGAR devotes a full factor (Government Factor 5, echoed in ANDSF Factor 4) to how President Ghani's governance style — concentrating power in a small circle nicknamed the **"three-man republic"** (Ghani, national security advisor Hamdullah Mohib, and administrative office head Fazal Mahmood Fazli) — amplified the system's fragility. The mechanism described is: centralization → filtered/selective information reaching the top ("shoot the messenger" dynamics) → decisions disconnected from ground truth → alienation of regional power-brokers and military leadership → loss of the very networks (e.g., regional strongmen like **[EXTRACT] Abdul Rashid Dostum, Atta Muhammad Noor, Ismail Khan**) that had proven capable of resisting the Taliban in the 1990s.

## 3. Institutional Perspective and Oversight Themes

A significant portion of this testimony (roughly its first third) is not retrospective at all — it is SIGAR asserting an ongoing institutional battle over its ability to do its job, and this deserves treatment as its own theme because it recurs at both the start and end of the document, effectively framing everything in between.

### 3.1 Non-cooperation as an active, current obstacle
SIGAR states directly that the Department of State, and to a lesser extent USAID, are refusing to comply with legal obligations to provide requested information — including instructing employees not to talk to SIGAR, and in one case instructing a contractor not to participate in a SIGAR audit. SIGAR's own conclusion from this: *"I cannot report to this Committee or the American people on the extent to which our government may be funding the Taliban... We simply do not know."* This is presented not as a caveat but as a central finding in its own right — an absence of information, caused by agency non-cooperation, is itself one of the testimony's headline points.

### 3.2 The "High-Risk List" as a recurring institutional tool
**[EXTRACT] SIGAR High-Risk List** — referenced multiple times as SIGAR's mechanism for flagging forward-looking risks (both the 2021 warning about contractor withdrawal and the 2023 list on post-withdrawal aid risks are cited). Worth its own note as a recurring SIGAR product/method, distinct from SIGAR's after-the-fact audits.

### 3.3 Post-withdrawal funding streams (2023 present-day findings)
The testimony details three major categories of ongoing US-related financial flows into Afghanistan as of 2023, useful as a standalone reference:
- **$3.5 billion** transferred to the **[EXTRACT] Afghan Fund** (est. September 2022, Geneva) from frozen Afghan central bank (**Da Afghanistan Bank/DAB**) reserves held in the US, aimed at economic stabilization while withholding funds from Taliban control; a further $3.5 billion remains tied up in US litigation.
- **~$2 billion** appropriated for humanitarian/development aid, ~60% as food aid, delivered not by US government personnel but through NGOs and international organizations (e.g., World Food Programme, UNICEF).
- **~$2.8 billion** obligated by DOD to transport, house, and feed evacuated Afghan allies.

### 3.4 Documented Taliban methods of diverting/taxing aid
SIGAR itemizes specific mechanisms by which the report says the Taliban extracts value from ongoing aid flows even without direct control of funds:
- Customs charges on incoming shipments and direct taxes/fees on NGOs
- Fees imposed on NGO vendors (landlords, suppliers, cell-phone companies), who pass costs to NGOs
- Redirection of aid away from disfavored groups — the report specifically names the **Hazara** ethnic minority as a group whose international education/humanitarian funding has been diverted
- Coercive vendor requirements (e.g., a 2022 case where NGOs in northern Afghanistan were permitted to rent vehicles only from the Taliban)
- The December 2022 ban on Afghan women working for NGOs, and an April 2023 extension of that ban to women working for the UN — significant because women aid workers are required (under Taliban gender-segregation rules) to serve women recipients, who constitute 70 percent of USAID's beneficiaries

**[EXTRACT] "Follow the Money" Capital Flight Project** — a named, ongoing SIGAR investigation (with Treasury/FinCEN) tracing asset removal by senior Afghan officials and politically connected individuals in the 18 months before the government's collapse, including wire-transfer analysis and open-source intelligence from the UAE and Turkey, and at least one nomination for Global Magnitsky Act sanctions.

## 5. Government-Collapse: The Five Short-Term Factors (as named by SIGAR)

1. **The Afghan government did not believe the US would actually leave** — rooted in a documented history of prior US statements about withdrawal that didn't materialize, compounded by contradictory signals around the Doha Agreement (troop-withdrawal commitment) alongside a simultaneous US-Afghan joint declaration reaffirming continued military cooperation.
2. **The Afghan government was undermined by its exclusion from US-Taliban talks** — direct negotiation between the US and Taliban, without Afghan government participation, both legitimized the Taliban's "puppet government" framing of Kabul and removed Kabul's negotiating leverage.
3. **The Afghan government insisted the Taliban be integrated into the Republic** — an unrealistic negotiating stance given the government's weak position (low credibility, contested 2019 election, high Taliban battlefield strength), compounded by President Ghani's reported unwillingness to compromise or step down, based partly (per Fatima Gailani's account to SIGAR) on his expectation that a Biden win would reset negotiations.
4. **The Taliban were unwilling to compromise** — once the core Taliban demand (full US/coalition withdrawal) was secured via Doha, the movement pursued battlefield victory rather than a negotiated settlement; a US intelligence assessment by April 2021 reportedly concluded the Taliban was confident of military victory.
5. **President Ghani governed through a small circle of loyalists ("three-man republic")** — centralization and personalized decision-making destabilized the government at a critical juncture, alienating power-brokers whose support might otherwise have mattered.

## 6. ANDSF-Collapse: The Six Short-Term Factors (as named by SIGAR)

1. **The US-Taliban agreement and subsequent withdrawal of US troops/contractors degraded ANDSF morale** — compounded by a lopsided prisoner exchange (5,000 Taliban militants released for 1,000 Afghan-government-held prisoners), many of whom returned to the fight.
2. **The US military slashed support (especially airstrikes) almost overnight** — airstrikes fell 78 percent from 2019 (7,423) to 2020 (1,631), enabling the Taliban to mass forces openly; the ANDSF was also placed under an "active defense" posture that constrained offensive operations.
3. **The ANDSF never achieved self-sustainment** — structural dependencies in resource management, maintenance, and leadership (detailed in §2.3 above), plus corruption (ghost soldiers, fabricated combat reports, collusion in skimming supply contracts) as a cross-cutting factor undermining all three.
4. **Politicization of the ANDSF and centralization of security planning** — frequent rotation of security leaders under Ghani, and reported direct micromanagement of military operations by national security advisor Hamdullah Mohib (target selection, commander appointments, troop deployments bypassing the normal chain of command).
5. **The Afghan government failed to develop a national security plan** — resistance to consolidating indefensible checkpoints for political/ethnic reasons (a Pashtun president reportedly unwilling to be seen abandoning Pashtun areas), failure to arm/support locally organized "public uprising forces" against the Taliban out of fear of empowering rival strongmen, and a national security strategy only announced July 26, 2021 — weeks before the collapse.
6. **The Taliban's military campaign effectively exploited ANDSF weaknesses** — a deliberate campaign sequence (surrounding district centers, prioritizing the north, seizing border crossings), combined with psychological/media operations and negotiated local surrenders brokered through tribal elders rather than centrally coordinated resistance.
