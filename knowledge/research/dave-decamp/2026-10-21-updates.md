# Netanyahu Talks Regime Change, the UK Joins Saudi Arabia's Air War, and the Quiet Escalation in Somalia

*Based on the October 20, 2026 edition of Antiwar News, hosted by Dave DeCamp of Antiwar.com.*

## Netanyahu: "Finishing the Job" Means Toppling Iran's Government

For the third day running, the news began with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. Speaking Tuesday at a university near Israel's border with Lebanon, Netanyahu said Israel was committed to "finishing the job" in the war on Lebanon, and then made clear that the job extends well beyond Lebanon.

"Finishing the job means bringing down the terrorist regime in Iran," he said. "It is the head of the poisonous octopus. You cut it off and it will fall. We will also have peace with Lebanon. We must complete the elimination of the Iranian regime, that is the primary objective. I have spoken about this with President Trump more than once, including the day before yesterday."

By Netanyahu's account, then, when he and Trump talk about finishing the job, they are talking about regime change in Tehran. The US-Israeli war has not come close to achieving that. DeCamp's assessment is that Netanyahu would likely settle for something short of it: destroying and thoroughly destabilizing the country, which is what a resumed full-scale bombing campaign would aim for. DeCamp considers a renewed campaign unlikely, but says Netanyahu's intentions are plain.

The remarks follow a period of close US-Israeli coordination, including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's trip to Israel. Hegseth has acknowledged the visit and presented it as a trip to see US troops stationed there. DeCamp doubts that explanation. The visit was never formally announced, only leaked to the press, which gave it a secretive cast. He suggests the steady stream of threats may be meant to keep Iran off balance, create the impression that an attack is imminent, or possibly provoke Iran ahead of Israel's election.

## Poll: A Plurality of Active-Duty Troops Oppose the Iran War

A poll reported by Blaise Malley at *Responsible Statecraft* found that 48% of active-duty service members oppose the use of the military in the US-Israeli war with Iran.

The survey was conducted by Change Research for Common Defense, a veterans' group that advocates progressive policies and endorses Democratic candidates. It polled 156 people currently serving, 431 veterans, and 144 immediate family members of active-duty personnel between September 15 and September 28. Despite the sponsor's leanings, 47% of respondents identified as Republicans, nearly 20% as pure independents, and 35% as Democrats.

Republicans backed Operation Epic Fury 77% to 16%, but heavy opposition among Democrats and independents left respondents opposed overall, 49% to 44%. The breakdown by group is striking. Veterans were the most supportive, at 53% in favor and 44% opposed. Active-duty members opposed the war by 16 points, and military families opposed it by 38 points.

The sample is small, but DeCamp notes it is significant that more of the people actually serving oppose the war than support it, and unsurprising that military families are strongly against it.

### Vance Confronted by a Grieving Widower

Over the weekend, Vice President JD Vance held a CBS News town hall where he was questioned by Joey Amor, whose wife Nicole Amor was one of six US soldiers killed by an Iranian strike on a military base in Kuwait in the war's opening days. Reporting has suggested the deaths may have been avoidable. The soldiers were reportedly working in a makeshift operations center, essentially a double-wide trailer, that was badly exposed.

"There's a failure in leadership," Amor told Vance, "and I want to know how this administration is going to start holding these leaders accountable for the actions and the deaths of the soldiers that we honor and that we love."

Vance replied that it was "literally the first time" he had heard of these failures, though he pledged to find out exactly what happened and what could be done better. DeCamp called it "not the best showing."

## Saudi Airstrike Kills Eight Yemeni Fishermen

Saudi airstrikes hit two fishing boats off Yemen's coast on Tuesday, killing at least eight fishermen, according to Yemeni media. The Saba news agency reported that the boats were struck off the coast of the Dhubab district in southwestern Taiz province, near the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. The area was held by Saudi-backed forces until last month, when Ansar Allah (the Houthis) launched a major offensive and seized territory along the strait.

Footage aired by Al-Masirah TV showed bodies and wreckage of the boats washed up on the beach, indicating the attack happened very close to shore.

The agriculture and fisheries ministry of the Sanaa-based Ansar Allah government said fishermen were targeted while working in coastal waters and were pursued and attacked as they tried to flee. The ministry rejected claims from the Saudi-led coalition that traditional boats were being used to smuggle weapons, missiles, and military equipment, saying such allegations do not justify targeting civilian fishermen. The Sanaa-based health ministry said there were two strikes: the first hit a fishing boat anchored near the shore, and the second targeted the survivors as they tried to escape in another boat, killing them all.

A day earlier, coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki released footage of strikes on small vessels he claimed were smuggling weapons. Those boats appeared somewhat larger than the fishing boats hit Tuesday, and that was a separate strike. DeCamp said he had seen no Saudi statement on Tuesday's attack, which all indications suggest killed civilians.

There is long precedent. The Saudi-led coalition routinely bombed fishing boats during the 2015–2022 war. Human Rights Watch reported in 2019 that over roughly a one-year period beginning in 2018, the coalition killed at least 47 Yemeni fishermen, including seven children. The coalition's campaign also struck farms and food supplies, while a strict blockade contributed to mass starvation. DeCamp notes that the current Saudi campaign has again produced major civilian casualties, and that it enjoys strong backing from the US and other Western powers.

## UK to Deploy Typhoon Fighter Jets to Saudi Arabia

That Western support is growing. On Tuesday, Britain announced that RAF Typhoon fighter jets will fly over Saudi Arabia to help repel attacks from Ansar Allah, an escalation of British involvement in the war.

"The UK will fly defensive counterair missions to support the protection of Saudi cities and critical national infrastructure against aerial attack using RAF Typhoon fighter jets," the Ministry of Defence said. The statement added that the Typhoons will not conduct offensive strikes against the Houthis, and that the UK will also send a military transport aircraft to move defense materiel within Saudi Arabia.

DeCamp describes this as clear mission creep. Britain first sent air defense equipment and military advisers, then began refueling Saudi warplanes, and now is sending fighter jets. They are billed as defensive, meant to shoot down missiles and drones, but DeCamp warns that this could quickly turn into direct strikes.

So far, none of the countries lining up behind Riyadh, including Pakistan, Turkey, the US, the UK, and most recently Egypt, has taken direct military action against Ansar Allah. DeCamp attributes this to Ansar Allah's warnings to the US and EU that such action would bring renewed disruption to shipping through the Bab el-Mandeb, warnings these governments appear to be taking seriously.

He also notes how little coverage Yemen's civilian casualties receive in the US, and probably in Britain and Europe as well, even though anyone who has followed the conflict should expect them. The same was true of the US bombing campaign in Yemen last year.

### Saudi Executions and Selective Outrage

Antiwar.com also reported that Saudi Arabia executed a Bangladeshi couple for what Saudi authorities called "indecent acts" in Mecca. The Bangladeshi government was reportedly unaware of the case before the execution. DeCamp contrasts the heavy Western focus on human rights in Iran with near-silence about Saudi Arabia, a country whose air force flies American and British jets, drops American bombs, and relies on US intelligence, British advisers, and Western refueling.

## Tom Barrack: Military Action in Yemen Is "Fruitless"

According to *The Cradle*, US envoy Tom Barrack said at a global security forum in Qatar on October 20 that a US ground war in Yemen would be pointless.

"I think the president's been clear that he supports Saudi Arabia and the backing that they need logistically he will supply," Barrack said, "but America getting involved in a ground kinetic battle somewhere else seems fruitless." He also acknowledged Saudi Arabia's difficult position: "These kinetic reactions have never worked. It doesn't work. It's probably not likely that it's going to work."

DeCamp observes that unconventional Trump appointees occasionally say true things other US officials would not, even if Barrack has made plenty of outlandish statements. Barrack appeared to be conceding that the entire Saudi campaign will fail, which is consistent with history. Earlier wars against the Houthis in the 2000s, fought when the movement was based in its home mountains of Saada province on the Saudi border, also failed. Military campaigns against the group have never worked out, yet they keep being tried.

## Israeli Military Kills Four Palestinians in Gaza

Israeli attacks across Gaza killed at least four Palestinians on Tuesday, as the military continued violating the US-backed ceasefire. According to the Palestinian news agency WAFA, a strike on an apartment building in Gaza City killed a man and his wife, identified as Akram Yasin and Dina Abu Bakr. Photos showed their 10-year-old daughter, Dima, mourning her parents.

"We're supposed to be in a ceasefire, but honestly, we are not seeing a ceasefire or anything," the dead man's brother told Reuters. "The war is still going on. Assassinations are continuing, and the shelling has not stopped."

In Khan Younis, in southern Gaza, Israeli forces shot and killed a 17-year-old boy. WAFA later reported that another Palestinian was killed by shelling in northern Gaza.

The killings came a day after the World Food Programme announced it was drastically cutting aid to Gaza. Israel had cut off commercial goods entering the territory; some were supposedly set to resume Tuesday, but it was unclear whether that happened, and DeCamp doubts supplies will return to previous levels.

## Jerusalem Church Leaders Condemn Settler Attack on Taybeh

The Patriarchs and Heads of Churches in Jerusalem, a body that includes Catholic, Orthodox, and some Protestant leaders, condemned an attack by masked Jewish settlers on Saturday, October 17, on Taybeh, the last entirely Christian village in the West Bank. The settlers targeted local Palestinians during the olive harvest, along with visiting Belgian, French, and Polish diplomats and a French mayor.

The church leaders called the attack "heinous" and said it was "not an isolated incident," describing widespread settler terror against West Bank villages that peaks during the olive harvest, when families go to their land to gather what they need to live.

They also condemned a religious ruling by Rabbi Dov Lior, an influential figure in the religious Zionist and settler movements, which said Jews had the right to take olives from Palestinian orchards. The church leaders called the ruling unfounded, dangerous, and absolutely unacceptable, while welcoming the fact that several rabbis and Jewish voices had publicly rejected it. They noted that Israeli police made arrests after the attack and expressed hope that enforcement would continue seriously and consistently.

DeCamp is skeptical. Despite considerable international attention, Taybeh keeps being attacked, and in many high-profile cases, arrested settlers are simply released.

### Huckabee's Absence

On Sunday, US and other foreign diplomats visited Taybeh. US Ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee did not. He was attending a settler conference in the West Bank. Huckabee, a Christian Zionist, has condemned some of the most egregious settler attacks but defends the movement as a whole and holds that Jews have a God-given right to Palestinian land.

The same council of church leaders has previously called Christian Zionism a damaging ideology. DeCamp notes that the Jerusalem churches, Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant alike, generally do not embrace Zionism as a theological position; it is largely a feature of certain strands of American evangelicalism. He argues that one reason settlers feel so emboldened is that the US ambassador himself has said Israel is entitled to all the land from the Nile to the Euphrates.

## Lebanon: Escalating Threats and Daily Strikes

### Katz Targets the Lebanese Army

As reported by Antiwar.com's Jason Ditz, Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz is ratcheting up threats against Lebanon, now accusing the Lebanese army of negligence for failing to disarm Hezbollah in 2024 and 2025 and saying Israel will hold it responsible for dragging Lebanon into war.

DeCamp argues that any real attempt by the Lebanese army to disarm Hezbollah would almost certainly spark a civil war, and that this may be exactly what Israel wants.

There are also reports that the State Department has passed Katz's threats on to Lebanon, saying Trump would give Israel a green light to push its occupation farther north if Hezbollah joins Iran in attacking Israel. DeCamp sees this as a sign of serious preparation for renewed war with Iran: Washington and Tel Aviv want to deter Hezbollah from firing on northern Israel if that happens.

### Strikes Continue in the South

Meanwhile, Israel continued heavy airstrikes, drone strikes, and artillery fire across multiple villages in southern Lebanon. No casualties had been reported, but the strikes, demolitions, and occupation are now a daily routine.

## US Launches Its 87th Airstrike of 2026 in Somalia

US Africa Command (AFRICOM) announced Tuesday that it conducted an airstrike in Somalia on October 17 targeting al-Shabaab near the Dhobley airport, close to the Kenyan border in the Lower Juba region. AFRICOM released no further details, and there was nothing from Somali media or the Somali government.

It was the first strike AFRICOM has announced this month, following an apparent lull, but it still brings the 2026 total to 87. That figure is historically very high. Before last year, the record was 63, set during Trump's first term. Last year, the US launched at least 124 strikes.

This air war receives essentially no coverage in the US, apart from occasional stories in military-focused outlets. With almost no information available, DeCamp says, the least that can be done is keep count.

## The Human Cost of the Somalia Air War

An Al Jazeera report from the ground in Somalia documents a family that lost many members, including children, in November 2024 in Jamame. The deaths were caused either by a US airstrike or by shelling from US-backed ground forces, and survivors say it was an aircraft. DeCamp believes the evidence points clearly to a US drone strike.

AFRICOM said it assessed the reports and concluded that the information "did not meet the military's more likely than not threshold" for determining that US strikes caused the harm. It stressed that this finding does not establish that US strikes caused no harm, only that the evidence did not meet that bar. DeCamp calls this no real answer: either way, the deaths were caused by the US or the forces it backs.

The report also lays out the scale of the escalation using New America's data:

| Administration | US airstrikes in Somalia |
|---|---|
| George W. Bush | 11 |
| Barack Obama | 48 |
| Donald Trump (first term) | 219 |
| Joe Biden | 51 |
| Donald Trump (second term, to date) | 214 |

DeCamp adds one caveat: the Bush-era count may be understated, given the difficulty of tallying strikes during the US-backed Ethiopian invasion of Somalia in 2006, though probably not by much. The main point is stark. In less than two years, Trump's second administration has conducted nearly as many strikes in Somalia as his entire first four-year term.

## Ukraine: Witkoff to Meet Putin After Zelensky Talks

US envoy Steve Witkoff, who is handling both the Ukraine negotiations and the Iran talks, met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and European officials in Norway and was set to meet Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on Wednesday with an undisclosed new proposal. Jared Kushner, also involved in the talks, has reportedly come down with the flu and is participating virtually.

DeCamp is openly skeptical. He notes that Witkoff's Iran diplomacy failed badly, unless the actual purpose was to avoid a deal and bomb Iran, which DeCamp believes it was. He says the current round feels like a repeat of past efforts, though he hopes he is wrong.

Zelensky claims the US has fully restored intelligence sharing, including early warnings of Russian ballistic missile launches. Washington, however, denies ever deliberately cutting it off, leaving the real story unclear.

No details of the new proposal have been released. Zelensky said Article 5-style, NATO-like security guarantees were discussed. If Ukraine and its backers still insist on the deployment of NATO troops in Ukraine, as they did in last year's negotiations, DeCamp sees no scenario in which Russia accepts it.

## South Korea Seeks Wartime Command Transfer From the US

According to Singapore's *Straits Times*, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung said on October 19 that his country is fully capable of taking back wartime operational control of the combined US–South Korean forces, and that talks with Washington are progressing. He said South Korea has built a powerful military able to counter North Korea, describing Pyongyang's nuclear weapons as fundamentally a means of last-resort defense rather than tools for fighting a war.

"To put it plainly, we should take back wartime operation control at an early time," Lee said. "We are fully capable of it, and discussions with the US are basically going well."

The US has held wartime operational control over South Korea's military for roughly 70 years. Peacetime control was returned decades ago, but wartime control remains in American hands. DeCamp notes that some of Trump's moves, such as scaling back military exercises, align with Lee's goals, though they have yet to produce any real easing of tensions on the peninsula.
