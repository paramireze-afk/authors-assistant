# A Clean Break: A New Strategy for Securing the Realm (1996)

> **Entry type:** Primary-source analysis / historical-political reference
> **Core subject:** A 1996 Israeli policy paper and its long tail of influence on Israeli strategic thought, American neoconservatism, the Iraq War, and subsequent Middle East regime-change politics through 2026.
> **Status:** Foundation entry — designed to anchor future notes on Oslo, PNAC, the Iraq War, the Israel lobby debate, and the 2025–2026 Iran wars.

This entry treats the report as three overlapping things at once: (1) a specific, short, and dateable policy memo with a traceable text; (2) a symbol invoked in decades of subsequent argument about the causes of the Iraq War and the "Israel lobby"; and (3) a lens through which very different political traditions — realist foreign-policy scholars, paleoconservatives, libertarian antiwar writers, progressive critics of Israeli policy, and Israeli commentators themselves — have told competing stories about how the modern Middle East got the way it is. These three things should not be collapsed into one another, and this entry tries to keep them distinct.

## 2. What the Document Actually Says

It's worth being precise about the text itself, because it is frequently characterized (by admirers and critics alike) in ways that outrun what it literally says. The paper is not a government white paper, not an official Israeli policy, and not a single coherent "grand strategy" so much as a short, rhetorically punchy memo interleaving analysis with draft talking points (explicitly marked "TEXT:") that Netanyahu could use in speeches.

**The diagnosis.** The report opens with a domestic argument, not a foreign-policy one: it claims that "Labor Zionism," which had dominated Israeli politics for seventy years, had produced "a stalled and shackled economy," and that the Oslo-era "peace process" — organized around the slogan of a "New Middle East" — had obscured Israel's "eroding national critical mass" and left the country strategically exhausted. Economic liberalization (tax cuts, privatization, ending dependence on U.S. aid) is presented as the true starting point of the paper's strategy, with foreign policy following from it.

**The break with "land for peace."** The paper's central rhetorical move is to reject the Oslo framework of trading territory for peace treaties in favor of what it calls "peace through strength" and "peace for peace" — the idea that Arab states and the Palestinian leadership should extend *unconditional* recognition of Israel's claims before, not instead of, any territorial concessions. It argues Israel should reassert "the principle of preemption" rather than "retaliation alone."

**Three regional prescriptions.** The memo's concrete recommendations cluster around three fronts:

- **Lebanon and Syria:** Israel should confront Syria and Hezbollah directly — including "striking Syria's drug-money and counterfeiting infrastructure in Lebanon," establishing that Syrian territory is "not immune to attacks emanating from Lebanon," and, if lower-level strikes prove insufficient, "striking at select targets in Syria proper." The paper leans heavily on allegations about Syrian-linked narcotics trafficking and counterfeit-currency production in the Bekaa Valley, and invokes the 1982 Hama massacre as evidence of regime character.
- **Iraq and Jordan:** The paper's most elaborate and, in hindsight, most-discussed section urges Israel to work with Turkey and Jordan to "contain, destabilize, and roll-back" Syria, and identifies removing Saddam Hussein as "an important Israeli strategic objective in its own right" — valuable primarily as a way of cutting off Syria's regional ambitions. It floats the idea of restoring the Hashemite monarchy (Jordan's ruling family) to power in Iraq, reasoning that the Shia religious establishment in Najaf might defer to a Hashemite king (as descendants of the Prophet Muhammad) and that this could be used to pull Lebanese Shia away from Hezbollah, Iran, and Syria.
- **The Palestinians:** Rather than land concessions, the paper recommends Israel assert a right of "hot pursuit" into Palestinian-controlled areas, hold the Palestinian Authority to "compliance" standards via a proposed "Joint Compliance Monitoring Committee," and "cultivate alternatives" to Yasser Arafat's leadership.

**On the U.S.-Israel relationship**, the paper argues for a shift from a patron-client dynamic to "self-reliance, maturity and mutuality," including a proposal that Israel voluntarily give up U.S. economic aid and loan guarantees (though not military aid, at least "for the moment") in order to remove "a significant lever of pressure" Washington had used against Israeli policy. It also recommends Netanyahu use warmer relations on missile defense to build congressional goodwill that could, among other things, help move the U.S. embassy to Jerusalem.

**The closing rhetoric** is grandiose rather than programmatic: Israel, the paper concludes, should not merely "contain its foes" but "transcend" the Arab-Israeli conflict altogether, becoming — through economic and moral renewal — "the basis of a truly new and peaceful Middle East." It closes by quoting, unnamed, "a senior Iraqi opposition leader," almost certainly a reference to a figure connected to Ahmed Chalabi's Iraqi National Congress, whose relationship to the paper's authors would matter enormously in the run-up to 2003 (see §6).

A useful discipline when reading commentary on Clean Break: separate (a) what the text says, (b) what its authors did later in government, and (c) what critics infer about intent or causation from the overlap between (a) and (b). Much of the controversy around the document lives entirely in the gap between these three things.

## 3. The People: A Small, Interlocking Network

The report's importance in later historiography rests less on its prose than on its signatories' subsequent careers. It is one of relatively few unclassified documents where you can watch a specific policy vision on paper in 1996 and then trace several of its authors into the U.S. national-security bureaucracy that planned the 2003 Iraq War.

**Richard Perle** (study-group leader) had been U.S. Assistant Secretary of Defense for International Security Policy under Reagan, earning the nickname "the Prince of Darkness" for his hawkishness on the Soviet Union. He studied under the philosopher Leo Strauss at the University of Chicago and began his career working for Senator Henry "Scoop" Jackson (D-WA) — a Cold War hawk whose office became an incubator for a generation of figures, including Paul Wolfowitz and Elliott Abrams, who migrated from Great Society liberalism to Reagan-era hawkishness and eventually into the neoconservative movement. Perle had been investigated multiple times by the FBI over suspected passing of classified material to Israeli officials in the 1970s, though never charged. Under George W. Bush he became chairman of the Defense Policy Board, an influential Pentagon advisory panel, giving him informal access without a formal cabinet role.

**Douglas Feith** ran a small Washington law and lobbying firm (Feith and Zell) in 1996; he became Under Secretary of Defense for Policy — the Pentagon's third-highest civilian post — under Bush, where he set up the Office of Special Plans (OSP), discussed below.

**David Wurmser**, then at IASPS, was arguably the paper's principal drafter. He elaborated its Iraq/Jordan/Syria theory at greater length in a companion IASPS paper, *"Coping with Crumbling States: A Western and Israeli Balance-of-Power Strategy for the Levant,"* and then in a 1999 book, *Tyranny's Ally: America's Failure to Defeat Saddam Hussein* (American Enterprise Institute Press). He later served in the State Department and then as Vice President Dick Cheney's senior Middle East adviser — arguably the most direct institutional line from "Clean Break" author to a seat inside the executive branch's most hawkish node on Iraq policy.

**Meyrav Wurmser**, David Wurmser's wife, was a scholar at Johns Hopkins and later co-founded the Middle East Media Research Institute (MEMRI) and directed the Center for Middle East Policy at the Hudson Institute.

**James Colbert** represented the Jewish Institute for National Security Affairs (JINSA), a hawkish think tank that would later place several alumni inside the Pentagon's Iraq-planning apparatus.

**Charles Fairbanks Jr.**, of Johns Hopkins SAIS, was a scholar of Soviet and post-Soviet politics.

**Robert Loewenberg**, IASPS's president, and **Jonathan Torop**, of the Washington Institute for Near East Policy (WINEP — itself an AIPAC-linked research spin-off), rounded out the group.

None of the participants held Israeli government positions when they wrote the report; it was an outside advisory paper from foreign (mostly American) policy intellectuals to an incoming prime minister, not a product of Israel's own foreign ministry or defense establishment. That distinction matters for how much authority to assign the document, and different commentators weigh it very differently (§8).

## 4. The Institutions

- **IASPS (Institute for Advanced Strategic and Political Studies):** A small Jerusalem/Washington think tank, no longer active in its original form, that positioned itself as a forum for hawkish, market-liberal Israeli strategic thought — explicitly hostile to "Labor Zionism" and the Oslo framework. Clean Break was its most historically consequential publication by a wide margin.
- **AEI (American Enterprise Institute):** Perle's institutional home and, more broadly, a central Washington node of neoconservative foreign-policy writing through the 1990s and 2000s; it published Wurmser's *Tyranny's Ally*.
- **JINSA (Jewish Institute for National Security Affairs):** A hawkish think tank whose board and fellows overlapped heavily with the Clean Break/PNAC/OSP network.
- **WINEP (Washington Institute for Near East Policy):** Founded in 1985 by former AIPAC staff as an ostensibly independent research institute; it has long occupied a contested position in these debates — mainstream and Beltway-respectable to most observers, but flagged by critics (including Mearsheimer and Walt) as institutionally close to AIPAC's policy priorities.
- **PNAC (Project for the New American Century):** Founded in 1997 by William Kristol and Robert Kagan, a year after Clean Break, with a founding statement of principles signed by Cheney, Rumsfeld, Wolfowitz, Perle, and others who would occupy senior Bush-administration posts (see §6).

## 5. The Israeli Political Moment, 1995–96

Clean Break cannot be understood outside the specific crisis atmosphere of Israeli politics in 1995–96, and this is a point on which historians of quite different political persuasions largely agree, even when they disagree about everything else.

**The assassination of Yitzhak Rabin** on 4 November 1995 by Yigal Amir, a right-wing religious nationalist opposed to the Oslo Accords, was the central rupture. Rabin, as prime minister, had signed the 1993 Oslo I and 1995 Oslo II accords with the PLO, premised on a gradual, negotiated land-for-peace transition. His assassination did not just remove a leader; it discredited, in the eyes of much of the Israeli right, the entire framework he had staked his premiership on. Netanyahu had, a month before the assassination, appeared at a rally where a mock coffin bearing Rabin's name was paraded and the crowd chanted "Death to Rabin" — an episode critics of Netanyahu (e.g., the *Hill* op-ed cited in §8) invoke as foreshadowing the "clean break" from Oslo that followed within a year.

**Netanyahu's narrow 1996 victory** — the first Israeli election with direct prime-ministerial voting, won by less than 1 percentage point over Shimon Peres — installed a leader whose Likud party had campaigned explicitly against the Oslo framework and against further withdrawals, even as Israel remained formally bound by agreements Rabin and Peres had signed. Clean Break was written and delivered squarely into that transition, explicitly timed: the document itself notes that "if Israel wants to test certain propositions that require a benign American reaction, then the best time to do so is before November, 1996" (the U.S. presidential election), suggesting its authors were thinking about the report as a narrow tactical window as much as a grand strategic vision.

**Labor Zionism's economic critique** is often underemphasized in later commentary that focuses almost entirely on the report's foreign-policy sections, but the report itself frames security strategy as downstream of a domestic argument about Israeli statist economics — an argument that fit into a broader 1990s current of market-liberal, Reagan/Thatcher-inflected economic thought moving through parts of the Israeli and American right simultaneously.

## 6. Intellectual Lineage: Where Clean Break's Ideas Came From

### 6.1 The "Scoop Jackson" Democrats and Straussian neoconservatism
Perle, Wolfowitz, Feith, and Abrams all passed through Senator Henry Jackson's office in the 1970s, a formative institutional experience for the neoconservative movement's turn from Cold War liberal anticommunism toward the Republican right. Perle's Straussian training under Leo Strauss at the University of Chicago is frequently cited by both sympathetic and hostile commentators as shaping a worldview emphasizing moral clarity, elite strategic vision, and skepticism of both détente-style realism and multilateral institutions.

### 6.2 The Wolfowitz Doctrine (1992 Defense Planning Guidance)
Four years before Clean Break, Paul Wolfowitz — then Under Secretary of Defense for Policy under Dick Cheney, with I. Lewis "Scooter" Libby and Zalmay Khalilzad as principal drafters — authored a draft Pentagon planning document arguing the United States should act to prevent the reemergence of any rival superpower and should be prepared to act preemptively and unilaterally to protect access to regions such as the Persian Gulf. Leaked and widely criticized (including by the elder Bush's own State Department) at the time, it is now treated by most historians of the era as the earliest programmatic statement of what later became "Bush Doctrine" preemption — a genuinely separate lineage from Clean Break's Israel-centered logic, but one that converged with it through shared personnel (Wolfowitz, Libby, Khalilzad all later held senior roles alongside Clean Break's Iraq-focused authors).

### 6.3 PNAC and the Iraq Liberation Act (1997–98)
In 1997, Kristol and Kagan founded PNAC with a statement of principles signed by, among others, Cheney, Rumsfeld, Wolfowitz, Perle, Jeb Bush, and Elliott Abrams. In January 1998, PNAC sent an open letter to President Clinton — signed by Rumsfeld, Wolfowitz, Perle, Abrams, Zalmay Khalilzad, and others — explicitly calling for a strategy aimed at removing Saddam Hussein from power, warning that containment through sanctions and inspections was failing. Congress responded that October with the Iraq Liberation Act of 1998, which made regime change in Iraq official (though non-binding) U.S. policy under the Clinton administration — three years before Bush took office and five years before the invasion.

### 6.4 "Rebuilding America's Defenses" (2000)
PNAC's most comprehensive statement, published just before the 2000 election, argued for sustained U.S. military dominance and a "substantial American force presence in the Gulf" that "transcends the issue of the regime of Saddam Hussein" — i.e., framing a durable military footprint, not merely Iraq policy, as the strategic goal. Critics have noted (with real controversy over how much weight to place on it) the document's since-notorious observation that this transformation would likely require "a new Pearl Harbor" to accelerate — language cited by 9/11-conspiracy theorists far beyond what the document's authors intended or what mainstream historians credit, but genuinely present in the text and worth knowing about for anyone encountering the claim.

### 6.5 The Yinon Plan — a contested precursor
Some critics trace an even older lineage to Oded Yinon's 1982 essay "A Strategy for Israel in the Nineteen Eighties," published in the World Zionist Organization-affiliated journal *Kivunim* and translated into English by the dissident Israeli scholar Israel Shahak. Yinon's essay argued Israel's long-term security depended on the fragmentation ("balkanization") of surrounding Arab states — Iraq into Kurdish, Sunni, and Shia components; Syria into sectarian statelets — along ethnic and confessional lines. **A note on sourcing here is important:** the most widely circulated claims connecting the Yinon Plan directly to Clean Break and to actual, implemented post-2003 Iraq/Syria policy come overwhelmingly from outlets such as Global Research and the Voltaire Network, both of which are broadly regarded by mainstream fact-checkers and academic Middle East specialists as unreliable, conspiratorially inclined sources with a track record of overstating documentary evidence for coordinated Israeli grand strategy. Yinon's 1982 essay is a real, verifiable document; whether it functioned as an operative blueprint for later Israeli policy, or merely as one hawkish Israeli journalist's speculative think-piece later retrofitted into a grand unified theory by very different political traditions, is a genuinely disputed question rather than settled fact. Treat any single source claiming a direct causal chain from Yinon (1982) through Clean Break (1996) to the 2003 invasion and the post-2011 Syrian civil war with real skepticism, and prefer sources that distinguish "documented intellectual echo" from "proven operational plan."

## 7. Did Netanyahu Actually Adopt It?

Wikipedia's entry and most sober historical treatments converge on a specific, important qualification: **certain aspects of the report were rejected by Netanyahu.** He did not, for instance, launch the strikes on Syrian territory the paper contemplated, nor did Israel unilaterally renounce U.S. economic aid. Douglas Feith himself has pushed back over the years against characterizations of Clean Break as an adopted Israeli government blueprint, and mainstream fact-checking summaries note that "available sources do not establish a single, definitive causal chain proving the report 'caused' later wars," with some analysts drawing strong connections and others treating the overlap as "indicative of shared ideas rather than direct implementation orders."

At the same time, journalist Brian Whitaker, writing in *The Guardian* in September 2002 as the Iraq War drums were beating, observed that "with several of the Clean Break paper's authors now holding key positions in Washington, the plan for Israel to transcend its foes by reshaping the Middle East looks a good deal more achievable today than it did in 1996" — adding the pointed line that "Americans may even be persuaded to give up their lives to achieve it." Patrick J. Buchanan, from an entirely different (paleoconservative, immigration-restrictionist, foreign-policy-realist) end of the political spectrum, wrote in March 2003 that the "principle of preemption" the paper urged "has now been imposed by Perle, Feith, Wurmser & Co. on the United States" — a striking example of hard-left and hard-right critics converging on an almost identical causal narrative, for very different underlying reasons. It is worth noting explicitly for the reader: Buchanan and antiwar-left writers arriving at similar conclusions about Clean Break's influence does not make the underlying claim more or less true — convergent conclusions from opposed ideological starting points is suggestive, not dispositive, and should be weighed as such.

The most defensible synthesis, on the current documentary record: Clean Break's specific tactical recommendations (Hashemite restoration in Iraq, strikes on Syria proper, ending U.S. aid) were **not** implemented as such by Israel or the United States. But several of its authors — Perle, Feith, and Wurmser chief among them — occupied senior U.S. national-security roles during the actual 2002–2003 decision to invade Iraq, and continued to publicly argue (in different venues, over the intervening years) for many of the same underlying premises: that Saddam's removal would serve a broader regional realignment, that Syria and Iran needed to be "contained, weakened, and rolled back," and that force could produce a friendlier regional order more reliably than negotiation. Whether the 1996 paper was a *cause* of the 2003 war, a *preview* of the same authors' later thinking, or simply the earliest dated expression of ideas they held independently of the document, is a real historiographical question rather than a settled one — and different serious writers you might read (Horton, Mearsheimer/Walt, Feith himself, mainstream diplomatic historians) answer it differently.

## 8. Personnel and Machinery: The Path to 2003

Whatever weight one assigns Clean Break causally, the *personnel overlap* between its authors and the Iraq War's architects is not in dispute and is documented across mainstream and dissenting sources alike:

- **Richard Perle** — chairman, Defense Policy Board (2001–2003; remained a member after resigning the chairmanship amid conflict-of-interest allegations).
- **Douglas Feith** — Under Secretary of Defense for Policy (2001–2005); established the **Office of Special Plans (OSP)**, an "expanded Iraq desk" inside the Pentagon's policy shop, run day-to-day by Abram Shulsky (a former Perle aide) and retired Navy officer William Luti. The OSP's function, according to critics including Air Force Lieutenant Colonel Karen Kwiatkowski (a Pentagon whistleblower who worked in the Near East/South Asia policy office at the time), was to sift raw intelligence — including material the CIA had already assessed and discounted — for anything supporting the case that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and had ties to al-Qaeda, then pass conclusions directly to the White House and press, bypassing normal intelligence-community vetting.
- **David Wurmser** — held posts at the State Department and the Pentagon (where he ran the Policy Counter-Terrorism Evaluation Group, which promoted claims — since discredited — about a Salman Pak terrorist-training facility and an alleged Prague meeting between a 9/11 hijacker and Iraqi intelligence), before becoming Vice President Cheney's senior Middle East adviser.
- **Paul Wolfowitz** — Deputy Secretary of Defense; a close Perle/Feith associate, though not himself a Clean Break signatory, and the drafter of the 1992 Defense Planning Guidance discussed above.

A recurring figure in this story who was **not** a Clean Break author but who several accounts (Horton, George Packer's *The Assassins' Gate*, and others) identify as central to converting the paper's Iraq/Jordan theorizing into concrete Washington advocacy is **Ahmed Chalabi**, the Iraqi exile whose CIA- and later Pentagon-funded Iraqi National Congress (INC) supplied several of the defectors whose claims about hidden Iraqi WMD programs later proved false. Wurmser's papers cite Chalabi-linked sources by name; the INC's own internal memos, later reported publicly, boasted of placing over 800 stories in American and British media in the year before the invasion, feeding lines directly to Pentagon and vice-presidential-office contacts.

The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's 2004 bipartisan report on prewar intelligence — a document independent of any of the above authors or their critics — concluded that intelligence claims about Iraqi weapons programs were, in its words, "either overstated or were not supported by the underlying intelligence reporting," a judgment that stands regardless of how one weighs Clean Break's specific causal role.

## 9. The Israel Lobby Debate: Preserving a Real Disagreement

Any serious treatment of Clean Break eventually runs into the broader — and much more contentious — argument about whether "the Israel lobby" (loosely defined) meaningfully shaped the decision to invade Iraq. This is a debate where good-faith, credentialed participants disagree sharply, and it should be presented as such rather than resolved by editorial fiat.

**The Mearsheimer-Walt thesis.** In 2006, University of Chicago political scientist John Mearsheimer and Harvard's Stephen Walt published "The Israel Lobby" in the *London Review of Books*, later expanded into a full book. They argued that a loose, non-conspiratorial coalition of pro-Israel individuals and organizations — not a secret cabal — had pushed U.S. Middle East policy, including the Iraq War, in directions that served Israeli interests (as the coalition understood them) more than American ones, and that Clean Break's authors were emblematic of this dynamic. They explicitly and repeatedly denied that their argument constituted or implied an antisemitic "Jewish conspiracy" framing, noting the lobby as they defined it "includes gentiles as well as Jews" and that many American Jews reject its positions.

**The response.** Critics — ranging from the Anti-Defamation League to individual scholars like Jeffrey Herf and Andrei Markovits to journalists like Jeffrey Goldberg (who called the book "the most sustained attack... against the political enfranchisement of American Jews since the era of Father Coughlin") — argued the thesis, regardless of authorial intent, reproduced older tropes about disproportionate and secretive Jewish political power, oversimplified a genuinely diverse and often internally divided set of American organizations, cherry-picked evidence, and overstated a single-cause explanation for a war that had many contributing causes (oil, WMD threat perception, post-9/11 threat inflation, bureaucratic momentum from the elder Bush administration's unfinished 1991 war, and the DPG/PNAC strain of pure American-primacy thinking that had nothing intrinsically to do with Israel). Brookings' review of the ensuing scholarly back-and-forth, and later reassessments (including a 2024 *Quillette* retrospective marking the article's near-twentieth anniversary), describe a debate that has never fully resolved: defenders maintain the criticism amounts to "smears" avoiding the substance; critics maintain the thesis still slides into, or at minimum provides intellectual cover for, antisemitic conspiracy narratives regardless of the authors' stated intent.

**Where Clean Break sits in this argument.** Mearsheimer and Walt cite the report directly, describing it as evidence that "Feith, Perle and Wurmser were soon urging the Bush administration to pursue those same goals" outlined in 1996. Both defenders and critics of the broader Israel-lobby thesis generally agree Clean Break is a real, documented artifact connecting specific people to specific later government roles; they disagree almost entirely on how much explanatory weight that connection can bear, and on whether it's being invoked as sober documentation or as rhetorical shorthand for a much larger, more contested claim about organized Jewish political influence.

**A parallel, non-overlapping current worth distinguishing from the lobby debate:** paleoconservative and libertarian/antiwar critics (Buchanan, Scott Horton, the *Antiwar.com*/Libertarian Institute circle) generally arrive at conclusions about Clean Break's importance that sound similar to Mearsheimer-Walt's, but from a starkly different tradition — one rooted in general opposition to U.S. interventionism, foreign-aid entanglements, and the national-security state as such, rather than in realist balance-of-power theory (Mearsheimer/Walt's academic frame) or progressive human-rights critique of Israeli policy (a third, again distinct, tradition). Conflating these three critical traditions — realist, libertarian/paleoconservative, and progressive/human-rights — obscures real differences in their underlying arguments and priors, even where their conclusions about Clean Break's historical significance overlap.

## 10. Later Echoes: From Iraq to Syria to Iran (2003–2026)

Clean Break's specific regional theory — using Iraq to pressure Syria, using Syria's weakening to isolate Hezbollah and Iran, and treating the whole exercise as a chain reaction ("Israel will not only contain its foes; it will transcend them") — is frequently invoked by commentators trying to make sense of the string of Middle Eastern upheavals across the following three decades. The connections below range from well-documented to speculative; each is labeled accordingly.

**2003–2011 — Iraq.** The invasion removed Saddam Hussein, as Clean Break's authors (by then inside the Bush administration) had long urged. It did **not** produce a Hashemite restoration or a pro-Israel, pro-Western Iraqi government; instead, the post-invasion power vacuum was filled disproportionately by Shia parties with closer ties to Iran than to Jordan, a result Scott Horton and others describe as close to the opposite of Wurmser's stated Najaf-centered theory — Iranian influence in Iraq is, on most assessments, dramatically *greater* after 2003 than before it, not weaker.

**2011–2024 — Syrian civil war.** Syria's decade-plus civil war, beginning amid the broader 2011 Arab Spring, was not caused by Clean Break or by Israeli policy in any documented, direct sense — its proximate causes (drought, Assad-regime repression, sectarian fault lines, the diffusion of the Arab Spring itself) are extensively documented independent of any Israeli strategic paper. That said, the war's *effect* — a fractured, dramatically weakened Syrian state — is exactly the outcome Wurmser's companion paper, "Coping with Crumbling States," explicitly contemplated and, in its own words, urged the West and Israel to help "expedite." Whether Israel's actual conduct during the war (medical support reportedly extended to some anti-Assad factions near the Golan, periodic strikes on Iranian and Hezbollah supply lines through Syria) reflects that older theorizing, opportunistic real-time adaptation to a war it did not start, or simply ordinary self-interested behavior by a neighboring state, is disputed; treat strong claims of a continuous 1996–2011 "master plan" with the same skepticism flagged in §6.5 regarding the Yinon Plan.

**December 2024 — the fall of the Assad regime.** Documented, non-speculative: on 8 December 2024, a rebel coalition led by Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS), commanded by Ahmed al-Sharaa (formerly known by the nom de guerre Abu Mohammed al-Jolani), took Damascus, ending 53 years of Assad-family rule; Bashar al-Assad fled to Russia. Contributing factors documented by independent analysts (the Stiftung Wissenschaft und Politik institute, the International Crisis Group, and others) include years of Turkish backing for the rebels, the severe weakening of Iran and Hezbollah from over a year of direct Israeli military operations (following the October 2023 Hamas attack and the ensuing regional war), and Russia's diverted attention and resources toward its war in Ukraine. In the immediate aftermath, Israel carried out roughly 500 airstrikes across Syria within 48 hours, destroying an estimated 70–80% of the former regime's remaining military hardware, and moved ground forces into and beyond the 1974 demilitarized buffer zone in the Golan Heights, later announcing it would remain "indefinitely." Whatever one concludes about older strategic papers, this sequence — Iran and its regional proxy network (the so-called "axis of resistance") measurably weakened by direct Israeli military action, followed by the collapse of a long-hostile Syrian regime, followed by rapid unilateral Israeli moves to secure military advantage in the resulting vacuum — is close in structure, if not in stated intent, to the "weaken, then exploit the chaos" logic Wurmser articulated in 1996–99. Analysts differ sharply on how much of that resemblance reflects continuity of doctrine versus the ordinary opportunism any state would show toward a suddenly weakened, historically hostile neighbor.

**June 2025 — the Twelve-Day War.** On 13 June 2025, Israel launched "Operation Rising Lion," a major air campaign against Iranian nuclear and military facilities and senior personnel; Iran retaliated with large missile and drone barrages against Israel. On 22 June, the United States struck three Iranian nuclear sites, including the underground Fordow enrichment facility. A ceasefire brokered by President Trump took effect 24 June. Netanyahu described the outcome as a "historic victory," saying Israel had "removed two immediate existential threats" (the nuclear program and the ballistic-missile arsenal); U.S. intelligence assessments of how much damage the strikes actually inflicted on Iran's enrichment capability were disputed within the U.S. government itself, with an initial low-confidence Defense Intelligence Agency assessment suggesting only a few months' setback and CIA Director John Ratcliffe later citing information indicating years of rebuilding would be required — an unresolved intelligence dispute worth flagging on its own terms, independent of Clean Break.

**February–May 2026 — renewed war and Khamenei's death.** After U.S.-Iran negotiations collapsed, the United States and Israel launched a joint operation ("Operation Epic Fury," in U.S. military parlance) on 28 February 2026, opening with a strike that killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei along with dozens of senior officials — a documented, unprecedented decapitation of the Islamic Republic's leadership, distinct in kind from the June 2025 campaign. President Trump publicly called for regime change. Iran's Assembly of Experts named Khamenei's son, Mojtaba Khamenei, as successor; Iran launched large-scale retaliatory strikes against U.S. and Israeli targets across the region, including U.S. bases in Bahrain, Jordan, Kuwait, Qatar, and the UAE. The conflict continued for roughly ten weeks before concluding around 5 May 2026. As of this entry's writing (July 2026), the succession crisis inside Iran remains unresolved and is the subject of ongoing reporting; readers consulting this entry after mid-2026 should search for updates rather than rely on the summary above, which reflects the picture as understood at time of writing.

**A note on how to read this section.** It would be easy to read the 2024–2026 sequence back into Clean Break as proof the 1996 paper "predicted" or "caused" three decades of subsequent history. Resist that temptation. Clean Break's authors did not foresee October 7, 2023, Hamas, HTS, drone warfare, or the specific chain of events that led to Khamenei's death in 2026; the paper's actual predictive content is far narrower (Hashemite restoration in a Saddam-free Iraq) and was, on the available record, simply wrong. What is genuinely traceable is something more modest and more interesting: a *durable strategic logic* — that Israel's security is best pursued by weakening a chain of hostile state and non-state actors (Iraq, then Syria, then Hezbollah, then Iran) rather than through negotiated settlement with any of them — that first received a clear, dated, textual articulation in 1996 by a specific named group of people, several of whom went on to hold real power, and that has recurred, in modified form, in the strategic thinking of a wider circle of Israeli and American officials across three decades, independent of whether any of them ever reread the original 1996 memo.

## 11. The Critics, in Their Own Terms

This entry has tried to attribute claims rather than adjudicate them; this section collects the major critical voices so future writing can draw on them directly, with the important qualification that these traditions do not agree with each other even where they agree about Clean Break.

- **Scott Horton** (director of the Libertarian Institute, editorial director of Antiwar.com, host of *The Scott Horton Show*) offers probably the single most detailed, document-heavy popular treatment of Clean Break, serialized from his 2021 book *Enough Already: Time to End the War on Terrorism*. Horton's framing is explicitly libertarian/antiwar rather than realist-academic or progressive: his central complaint is with U.S. interventionism and the national-security state as such, and he treats Clean Break's authors' theorizing about Hashemite restoration and Najaf clerical loyalty as, in his words, "hallucinating" — badly wrong on the actual religious and political dynamics of Iraqi Shia Islam, not merely aggressive. Horton traces a granular institutional map of how Clean Break's authors and their allies (Bolton, Libby, Hannah, Edelman, Ledeen, Shulsky, Luti, Rubin, Schenker, Makovsky, and others) came to occupy specific Bush-administration posts, and cites Karen Kwiatkowski's whistleblower testimony extensively.
- **John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt**, from an academic-realist tradition centered on international-relations theory rather than opposition to state power as such, treat Clean Break as one piece of evidence for their broader (and, as documented in §9, heavily contested) Israel-lobby thesis.
- **Patrick J. Buchanan**, from a paleoconservative, immigration-restrictionist, "America First" tradition with its own long and sometimes genuinely antisemitic history (a point his critics regularly raise), argued in 2003 that Clean Break's "principle of preemption" had been "imposed... on the United States" by its authors — a claim structurally similar to Horton's or Mearsheimer/Walt's but emerging from a very different, older current of American right-wing non-interventionism.
- **Karen Kwiatkowski**, a retired Air Force lieutenant colonel who served in the Pentagon's Near East/South Asia policy office in the run-up to the Iraq War, became an influential whistleblower source (cited by Horton, Seymour Hersh, and others) describing the Office of Special Plans' intelligence practices from the inside; she has publicly drawn the connection between Clean Break's authors and OSP's later conduct.
- **George Packer**, in his acclaimed 2005 narrative history of the Iraq War's origins, *The Assassins' Gate*, reads Clean Break primarily through David Wurmser's subsequent AEI-published book, arguing Wurmser's thesis was that removing Saddam would solve Israel's strategic problems and leave the Palestinians "essentially helpless" — a more measured, historian's framing than either Horton's or Buchanan's polemical registers, useful precisely because it doesn't share their political priors.
- **Progressive Israeli and American critics** (e.g., the 2024 *Hill* op-ed cited above, written from a religious-progressive American-Jewish perspective invoking the biblical prophet Micah) connect Clean Break directly to the Gaza war and to Netanyahu's post-2022 coalition with far-right figures like Itamar Ben-Gvir, arguing the "preempt rather than build a positive future" logic the paper articulated in 1996 remains visible in Israeli policy conducted under Netanyahu's later governments — an argument about ideological continuity in Netanyahu's own thinking specifically, distinct from the U.S.-policy-focused arguments of Horton or Mearsheimer/Walt.
- **Ian Buruma**, writing in the *New York Times* in 2003, offered one of the more skeptical mainstream treatments, suggesting Netanyahu may have wanted to follow Clean Break's advice in 1996 but "just wasn't in a position to do so" — an argument for reading the report as an unrealized aspiration rather than an implemented plan, at least on the Israeli side.
- **Mainstream fact-checking and encyclopedic summaries** (Wikipedia, and aggregator services drawing on multiple sources) consistently flag that the causal question — whether Clean Break shaped later events or merely anticipated views its authors held independently — remains genuinely disputed among people who agree on all the documented facts about authorship and later career paths.

## 12. Open Questions for Further Research

A knowledge-base entry should flag what remains unresolved rather than paper over it:

1. **Causal weight.** No document in the public record establishes that the 2003 Iraq War decision-makers (principally Bush, Cheney, and Rumsfeld) treated the 1996 Clean Break memo itself as an operative planning document, as opposed to Clean Break's authors independently carrying forward similar views they'd have held with or without the 1996 paper. This distinction matters and is easy to elide in secondary sources.
2. **Netanyahu's own view.** Sources agree he "rejected" parts of the report but disagree on which parts, when, and how consciously the rejection was communicated; a systematic look at Netanyahu's own memoirs and Israeli-press interviews from 1996–99 would sharpen this considerably.
3. **The Yinon Plan lineage.** As flagged in §6.5, the strongest versions of this claim rest on sourcing that mainstream researchers regard as unreliable; a rigorous treatment would need to separate Yinon's actual 1982 text (verifiable) from the much larger conspiratorial apparatus built on top of it by outlets like Global Research and Voltaire Network.
4. **Comparative weight of Clean Break versus the 1992 Defense Planning Guidance** as intellectual sources for the Bush Doctrine — these are two distinct lineages (Israel-centered regional strategy vs. general American unipolar-primacy theory) that get conflated in a lot of popular writing but had different authors, different institutional homes, and different core arguments, even though they shared several people (Wolfowitz, Libby, Khalilzad) and converged in practice after 2001.
5. **What actually happened to Jordan's Hashemite ambitions in post-2003 Iraq.** Sources agree Jordan "proved to have little influence" post-invasion, but a deeper dive into Jordanian archives or diplomatic memoirs (rather than the American commentary reviewed here) would be a genuinely useful addition to this entry.
