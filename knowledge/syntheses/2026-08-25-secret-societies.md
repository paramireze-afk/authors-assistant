# Five Hidden Worlds: Webster, Antelman, Griffin, Crowley, and Fortune in Their Own Words

Between roughly 1900 and 1994, five writers each concluded that visible reality conceals a deeper structure. For three of them, that structure was historical and conspiratorial: a hidden hand behind revolutions, heresies, and central banks. For the other two, it was psychological and initiatory: a hidden architecture of consciousness accessible through ritual and symbol. This piece reconstructs each writer's system primarily through their own words — quoted directly from their major works, with chapter references (exact pagination varies across the reprinted and public-domain editions consulted, so chapters are cited where pages are not reliably fixed) — before stepping back to sort documented fact from interpretation from material that doesn't hold up.

## Nesta Webster: "It Is Impossible to Follow Any Consecutive Political Plan... Yet"

Nesta Helen Webster (1876–1960) began as a historian of the French Revolution before writing ***Secret Societies and Subversive Movements*** (1924), her attempt to trace "the course of revolutionary ideas through secret societies from the earliest times." She is explicit, in her preface, about both her method and her limits:

> "The object of the present book is therefore to carry further the enquiry I began in *World Revolution*, by tracing the course of revolutionary ideas through secret societies from the earliest times, indicating the role of the Jews only where it is to be clearly detected, but not seeking to implicate them where good evidence is not forthcoming. For this reason I shall not base assertions on merely 'anti-Semite' works, but principally on the writings of the Jews themselves." (Preface)

This is worth taking seriously as a statement of self-understanding: Webster presents herself as a careful historian working from primary and even sympathetic sources, not a polemicist. She closes the same preface with a direct challenge: "If I then fail to convince the incredulous that secret forces of revolution exist, it will not be for want of evidence."

**Her theory of continuity.** The book's central methodological move is to establish that a single organizational technique — recruiting large numbers of people through graduated degrees of initiation, most of whom never learn the final, materialist purpose of the society — recurs across radically different eras and cultures. Describing the ninth-century Ismaili organizer Abdullah ibn Maymun, whose system used seven degrees of initiation to gradually strip away belief in revealed religion from unsuspecting recruits, Webster writes:

> "I quote this passage at length because it is of immense importance in throwing a light on the organization of modern secret societies. It does not matter what the end may be, whether political, social, or religious, the system remains the same — the setting in motion of a vast number of people and making them work in a cause unknown to them. That this was the method adopted by Weishaupt in organizing the Illuminati and that it came to him from the East will be shown later on." (Chapter 2, "The Revolt Against Islam")

This sentence is the clearest statement of her actual thesis: not that one continuous organization persisted for a thousand years, but that a *technique* — manipulation through hierarchical secrecy — recurs, and that later organizers (she names Weishaupt) either learned it from earlier ones or independently reinvented it. Earlier in the same chapter she calls the Essenes "the first of the secret societies from which a direct line of tradition can be traced up to the present day" (Chapter 1) — a claim of genealogical, not merely structural, continuity that sits somewhat in tension with the more modest "technique recurs" argument, and Webster does not fully resolve which she means.

**Her evidence and her explicit qualification.** In Chapter 8, "The Jewish Cabalists," Webster examines Jewish involvement in revolutionary movements from the English Civil War onward, and is careful to state a qualified thesis rather than a maximal one:

> "It was not then necessarily that Jews created these movements, but they knew how to make use of them for their own ends." (Chapter 8)

Her evidentiary method throughout this chapter is what might be called argument-by-improbable-coincidence: she assembles a set of eighteenth-century occult figures — Jacob Frank, the Comte de Saint-Germain, Cagliostro, the "Ba'al Shem of London" Hayyim Falk — and asks:

> "Are we to believe that all these men, so strangely alike in their careers, living at the same time and in the same places, were totally unconnected? Is it a mere coincidence that this group of Jewish Cabalist miracle-workers should have existed in Germany and Poland at the precise moment that the Cabalist magicians sprang up in France?" (Chapter 8)

This rhetorical structure — real, verifiable facts (these figures did exist, did know occult doctrine, were sometimes contemporaries) assembled into a pattern, with the connecting explanation offered as the most economical reading rather than as documented fact — recurs throughout the book and is the single most important thing to understand about how Webster reasons. Her concluding chapter states the limit of her own certainty directly:

> "On the other hand it may be that the hidden centre of direction consists in a circle of Jews located in the background of the Grand Orient, or perhaps... located nowhere but working in accord... On this point I think it would be dangerous at present to dogmatize." (Conclusion)

**Where the evidence stands.** The Bavarian Illuminati, the Essenes, Sabbatai Zevi's movement, Jacob Frank's sect, and the historical figure of Hayyim Falk are all real, and Webster's factual raw material is drawn from genuine (if sometimes hostile or unreliable) nineteenth-century sources like the *Jewish Encyclopedia* and Heinrich Graetz. What she herself concedes she cannot prove — a "hidden centre of direction" connecting them — remains unproven by her own account. Her 1920 *Morning Post* series (published as *The Cause of World Unrest*) leaned on the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, and she continued to describe the forged document's authenticity as "an open question" even after the 1921 *Times* exposé identified its plagiarized source. That specific choice — treating a document proven to be a forgery as still potentially genuine — is a documented failure of method, not a matter of interpretation.

## Marvin Antelman: "The Frightening Inside Story of... Efforts to Destroy Jews, Judaism and Israel"

Rabbi Marvin S. Antelman (1934–2015), an American-Israeli Orthodox rabbi and research chemist, wrote from inside the community his book addresses, in a project categorically different from Webster's. The full title of his 1974 book signals this immediately: ***To Eliminate the Opiate: The Frightening Inside Story of Communist and Conspiratorial Group Efforts to Destroy Jews, Judaism and Israel***. His stated occasion, per his own introduction, was a wave of violence against Orthodox institutions in Boston between 1968 and 1971 — he was writing as a defender of traditional Judaism against what he perceived as forces hollowing it out from within.

**His core historical claim.** Antelman's argument traces a lineage from Sabbatai Zevi (1626–1676) through his self-proclaimed reincarnation Jacob Frank (1726–1791), into the Bavarian Illuminati, and from there into the nineteenth-century Reform and Conservative Jewish movements, which he regarded not as legitimate developments but as institutional continuations of Frank's "redemption through sin" theology — the idea, genuinely documented within Sabbatean-Frankist thought, that the deliberate transgression of religious law could hasten messianic redemption. This concept is real and is treated seriously in mainstream academic scholarship: Gershom Scholem's landmark essay **"Redemption through Sin"** (in *The Messianic Idea in Judaism*, Schocken, 1971, p. 141) is the standard academic source describing exactly this Sabbatean-Frankist doctrine — Antelman's use of the term is not his invention but draws on real, documented theological history.

**The Eybeschütz claim — and what Scholem actually concluded.** Antelman's most consequential and most carefully argued historical position concerns Rabbi Jonathan Eybeschütz (1690–1764), a prominent eighteenth-century talmudist accused by Rabbi Jacob Emden of secretly being a Sabbatean, based on Kabbalistic amulets Eybeschütz distributed that Emden read as containing coded messianic allusions to Sabbatai Zevi. This is a real, extensively documented historical controversy that split European Jewish communities in the 1750s. Critically — and this is a point worth stating precisely rather than approximately — **Gershom Scholem, the founding academic scholar of Sabbatean studies, independently reached the same conclusion Emden and Antelman did**: based on his own analysis of Eybeschütz's kabbalistic writings and the disputed amulets, Scholem judged Eybeschütz to have likely been a covert Sabbatean, a position that reversed a long tradition of rabbinic scholars (including Ezekiel Landau) who had defended Eybeschütz as innocent of the charge. As the National Library of Israel's Gershom Scholem archive documents, when a Boston rabbinic court summoned Antelman over his book, "it wasn't Antelman's outlandish claims concerning the Reform movement and the Sabbatean roots of Communism that raised the ire of the Boston rabbis" — it was specifically his revival of the Eybeschütz charge, "a position also shared by Scholem." Antelman defended himself by invoking prior rabbinic authority and Scholem's scholarship, and a Los Angeles rabbinic court later exonerated him, its letter explicitly naming "Professor Gershom Shalom" as the scholarly authority underlying Antelman's position on Eybeschütz.

Scholem's own reaction to the book as a whole, however, was contemptuous. On the inside cover of his personal copy of *To Eliminate the Opiate* — preserved in the Gershom Scholem Collection at the National Library of Israel — Scholem scrawled: **"Nonsense based on me!!!"** It's important to be precise about what this annotation was and wasn't responding to: per the National Library's own account, it was Scholem's general verdict on Antelman's sweeping conspiracy thesis as a whole (linking Sabbateanism to the Enlightenment, Reform Judaism, and Communism), not a specific rejection of the narrower, better-documented Eybeschütz claim, which Scholem's own independent scholarship supported. This is a genuine and unusual case: a serious academic historian sharing one specific, contested historical judgment with a conspiracist writer, while regarding the writer's larger edifice built upon it as nonsense.

**What Antelman's own project was and wasn't.** Read in his own terms, Antelman was not arguing that Jews as a people secretly control world events — his framing is the opposite: that a heretical current *within* Jewish history worked to corrupt authentic Torah observance, and that identifying it was an act of religious defense, not attack. He went on to found a "Supreme Rabbinical Court of America" and, in one of its more theatrical acts, presided over the symbolic excommunication of Henry Kissinger in 1976. His book's afterlife, however, escaped his control and his framing: the Wikipedia article on the "Sabbatean-Frankist conspiracy theory" documents that Antelman's framework was subsequently adopted by figures entirely outside Orthodox Judaism, including white nationalist David Duke, and extended — in a form Antelman's own religious-defense framing does not support — into claims about contemporary elite families, including, in some recent online iterations, associates of Jeffrey Epstein.

## G. Edward Griffin: "Almost All of History Is an Unbroken Trail of One Conspiracy After Another"

G. Edward Griffin (b. 1931) approaches hidden power from economics and mid-century American libertarian politics rather than religion. His ***The Creature from Jekyll Island: A Second Look at the Federal Reserve*** (1994) opens from a documented event: in November 1910, Senator Nelson Aldrich led a secret gathering of bankers to Jekyll Island, Georgia, to draft what became the Federal Reserve Act. Griffin dramatizes the secrecy involved, quoting the participants' own later accounts of using false first names and traveling under cover of a duck-hunting trip:

> "If it were to be exposed publicly that our particular group had got together and written a banking bill, that bill would have no chance whatever of passage by Congress." (Part I, quoting Frank Vanderlip)

Griffin's own summary of the meeting's secrecy — "It is difficult to imagine any event in history — including preparation for war — that was shielded from public view with greater mystery and secrecy" (Part 1, Chapter 1) — sets up his central interpretive move: treating concealment itself as evidence of a "master plan."

**His explicit statement of method.** Unlike Webster, who hedges her central claim, Griffin states his interpretive framework as a direct methodological thesis, defending the term "conspiracy" against ridicule:

> "The reason it is difficult is that we have been conditioned to laugh at conspiracy theories, and few people will risk public ridicule by advocating them. On the other hand, to endorse the accidental view is absurd. Almost all of history is an unbroken trail of one conspiracy after another. Conspiracies are the norm, not the exception."

This is worth reading as Griffin's genuine epistemological position, not a rhetorical throwaway: he explicitly rejects what he calls the "accidental view" of history (that outcomes emerge from diffuse, uncoordinated causes) in favor of a default assumption that coordinated, secret human agency explains events — the inverse of the standard historiographical default, and one Griffin defends openly rather than concealing.

**The Rothschild Formula.** In Section III of the book, Griffin generalizes from the historical rise of the Rothschild banking family to a proposed psychological and strategic profile of financiers generally:

> "A study of these and similar events reveals a personality profile, not just of the Rothschilds, but of that special breed of international financiers whose success typically is built upon certain character traits. Those include cold objectivity, immunity to patriotism, and indifference to the human condition. That profile is the basis for proposing a theoretical strategy, called the Rothschild Formula, which motivates such men to propel governments into war for the profits they yield. This formula most likely has never been consciously phrased as it appears here, but subconscious motivations and personality traits work together to implement it nevertheless."

Griffin's own qualification here — that the "formula" was likely never consciously articulated but operates through "subconscious motivations" — is important: he is proposing an inferred psychological tendency among a class of actors, not claiming to have found a written plan. He supports the broader thesis with a specific claim about Waterloo, that the Rothschild banking house used advance knowledge of Napoleon's defeat to profit on the London exchange — a widely circulated historical anecdote whose precise details (including whether Nathan Rothschild actually traded on early news in the way popularly described) are disputed among historians rather than settled fact.

**Where the evidence stands.** The Jekyll Island meeting, its secrecy, and its role in drafting Federal Reserve legislation are acknowledged by mainstream historians of the Fed — Griffin's factual core here is real. His broader claims are more contested: economist and law professor Peter Conti-Brown's assessment that the book should be read "for entertainment but not information" reflects a broader consensus among historians and economists that Griffin's citation practices misrepresent several of his own sources, including Carroll Quigley's *Tragedy and Hope* (1966) — a real work of scholarship documenting genuine elite Anglo-American financial networks, which Quigley himself treated as an influential lobby, not the omnipotent, unified conspiracy Griffin builds from it. Griffin's book, unlike Webster's or Antelman's, is not organized around Kabbalah or Jewish religious history at all; its central figure is the Rothschild family treated as a banking dynasty, not a religious or mystical one — though independent of Griffin's own framing, historians note that centering a multi-generational conspiracy on this specific family sits inside a much older nineteenth-century antisemitic literary tradition (e.g., Alphonse Toussenel's 1845 *Les Juifs, rois de l'époque*) that Griffin does not cite and whose direct influence on him is undocumented.

## Aleister Crowley: "That Which Can Be Taught Shall Be Taught"

Aleister Crowley (1875–1947) is not asking who secretly controls history — he is asking how a practitioner can systematically map and transform consciousness. His ***777*** (privately published 1909, expanded posthumously as *777 Revised*) is not a historical argument at all but a correspondence table: a reference tool assigning the ten sefirot, the twenty-two paths of the Tree of Life, tarot trumps, astrological signs, colors, incenses, plants, and deities from multiple world religions into a single unified grid, built on the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn's teaching, of which Crowley had been an initiate.

**His stated purpose.** In his introduction to *777*, Crowley frames the project as an attempt to solve what he sees as the central problem of comparative religion — that superficially different religious symbols and gods are often pointing at the same underlying realities, obscured by "human sectarianism" in "speech" rather than in "thought":

> "This: That when a Japanese thinks of Hachiman, and a Boer of the Lord of Hosts, they are not two thoughts, but one. The cause of human sectarianism is not lack of sympathy in thought, but in speech; and this it is our not unambitious design to remedy." (Introduction)

He is explicit that the resulting system is meant to be usable, not merely descriptive, and rejects secrecy for its own sake:

> "It is simply bad faith to swear a man to the most horrible penalties if he betray..., etc., and then take him mysteriously apart and confide the Hebrew Alphabet to his safe keeping... That which can be taught shall be taught, and that which cannot be taught may at last be learnt." (Introduction)

**The Tree as a working tool, not a historical claim.** Crowley describes the Tree of Life itself in explicitly instrumental terms — "the skeleton on which this body of truth is built" (Introduction) — and is candid that the resulting table is provisional and incomplete by design: "Since all things whatsoever (including no thing) may be placed upon the Tree of Life, the Table could never be complete" (Introduction). This is a fundamentally different relationship to evidence than Webster's, Antelman's, or Griffin's: Crowley is not claiming his correspondences are historically or theologically "true" in a documentary sense, but that they function — that meditating on them produces usable ritual and psychological results, verified (in his account) through practice rather than through historical argument. As his own preface notes, roughly ninety percent of the Hebrew symbolism, the four color scales, and the tarot attributions are inherited directly from Golden Dawn teaching, not original historical research.

**The mixed record on prejudice.** This instrumental, practice-oriented relationship to Kabbalah did not prevent Crowley from holding and expressing personally prejudiced and inconsistent views about Jews as a group. In his *Confessions* he made a documented and indefensible claim that human sacrifice was "still practised by the Jews of Eastern Europe" — a falsehood with no factual basis, echoing centuries-old blood libel tropes. Elsewhere, in a letter to a German student of his Ordo Templi Orientis, he dismissed Nazi-era antisemitic rhetoric outright: "As for the ravings about the Jews, they are simply unintelligible... Almost the whole of life in Germany above brutality, stupidity and cruelty, servility, and bloodthirst was Jewish. Germans are far below Jews, generally speaking." His closest magical collaborator and literary executor, Israel Regardie, was Jewish. The most defensible summary, consistent with his biographers' assessments, is that Crowley's views on Jews were personally prejudiced and self-contradictory across his life, but never organized into any systematic historical or political theory of Jewish conspiracy — which is precisely what separates his project from Webster's or Antelman's, despite the superficial overlap of subject matter (all discuss Kabbalah).

## Dion Fortune: "The Tree of Life Is a Compendium of Science, Psychology, Philosophy, and Theology"

Dion Fortune (born Violet Mary Firth, 1890–1946) shares Crowley's Golden Dawn lineage — she joined the Alpha et Omega, a successor order, in 1919 — but trained first in psychology and psychoanalysis, and her major work, ***The Mystical Qabalah*** (1935), explicitly frames Kabbalistic symbolism as a technology of consciousness rather than a historical or theological claim about the world.

**Her explicit self-positioning relative to rabbinic tradition.** Fortune is unusually direct about the fact that her system is a modern development, not a transmission of unmodified ancient doctrine:

> "Let it be clearly understood, therefore, that I do not say, This is the teaching of the ancient Rabbis; rather do I say, This is the practice of the modern Qabalists, and for us a much more vital matter, for it is a practical system of spiritual unfoldment; it is the Yoga of the West." (Chapter IV, "The Unwritten Qabalah")

She is equally candid about her own qualifications, or lack of them, in classical scholarship: "So far as actual scholarship goes, I am in the same class as William Shakespeare, having little Latin and less Greek... Of any knowledge of Hebrew as a language I am guiltless" (Chapter IV) — a disarming admission that she is working as a practitioner-synthesizer, not a philologist, and inviting exactly the distinction this article is trying to draw between her project and academic Jewish studies.

**The Tree as algebra.** Fortune's central methodological metaphor, developed at length in Chapter III ("The Method of the Qabalah"), is mathematical rather than historical:

> "It is exactly the same principle as algebra. Let X represent the unknown quantity, let Y represent the half of X, and let Z represent something we know... The initiate... uses a symbol-system... as an algebra by means of which he will read the secrets of unknown potencies; in other words, he uses the symbol as a means of guiding thought out into the Unseen and Incomprehensible." (Chapter III)

And her summary statement of what the whole system is for: "In brief, the Tree of Life is a compendium of science, psychology, philosophy, and theology" (Chapter III) — a claim to functional, cross-disciplinary usefulness, not historical accuracy.

**Binah as Saturn, and as psychology.** Fortune's chapter on Binah gives the clearest primary-source confirmation of the "Binah = Saturn" correspondence discussed in Golden Dawn-derived systems, listed plainly in her own chapter-heading table: "MUNDANE CHAKRA: Shabbathai, Saturn." In the body text she elaborates this astrological attribution together with an explicitly psychological reading of the sefirah as the archetypal feminine principle of form and containment:

> "Kether differentiates into Chokmah and Binah before it achieves phenomenal existence, and these two are called by the Qabalists, Abba, the Supernal Father, and Ama, the Supernal Mother. Binah is also called the Great Sea, and Shabbathai, the Sphere of Saturn." (Chapter VII, "The Three Supernals")

> "Binah... is two-aspected, and these aspects are distinguished as Ama, the Dark Sterile Mother, and Aima, the Bright Fertile Mother. We have already noted that she is called the Great Sea, Marah, which not only means Bitter, but also is the root of Mary; and here we meet again the idea of the Mother, at first virgin, and then with child by the Holy Spirit." (Chapter VII)

This passage shows her method in miniature: she takes a real, traditional Zoharic epithet (Binah as "supernal mother," a genuine motif in the Zohar), layers onto it the Golden Dawn's astrological attribution (Saturn), and then extends both through free etymological and symbolic association (Marah/Mary) into a synthesis that is her own construction — offered, consistent with her stated method, as a modern development rather than a claim about what the ancient rabbis themselves believed.

**On the thinner historical record regarding prejudice.** Unlike Crowley, no single widely-documented passage in Fortune's major magical works parallel to his blood-libel remark or to Webster's Chapter 8 was located in this research. Some scholars of interwar British esotericism have noted that her wider corpus, including her novels, reflects period-typical class and ethnic assumptions common to her milieu, but the specific evidentiary record here is considerably thinner and less settled than for Webster, Antelman, or Crowley, and readers wanting a confident judgment on this specific point should consult dedicated biographical scholarship rather than take this summary as final.

## Comparing the Five

**Where they genuinely intersect.** All five treat Kabbalah as a serious body of knowledge worth engaging on its own terms, at a time when mainstream secular intellectual culture in Britain and America largely dismissed it. Webster and Antelman share a deeper, more specific intersection: both build arguments around Sabbatai Zevi and Frankism as evidence of covert historical continuity — Webster locating it in world revolution generally, Antelman in the corruption of religious Judaism specifically — and, remarkably, one piece of Antelman's argument (on Eybeschütz) converges with the independent academic conclusion of Gershom Scholem, even though Scholem rejected the larger edifice Antelman built on it.

**Where the resemblance is superficial.** Crowley and Fortune are often grouped with Webster and Antelman simply because all four write extensively about "Kabbalah" — but Crowley's and Fortune's own texts explicitly and repeatedly flag their work as modern, practice-oriented synthesis ("this is the practice of the modern Qabalists," not "the teaching of the ancient Rabbis," in Fortune's own words), evaluated by ritual and psychological usefulness rather than historical evidence. Webster's and Antelman's engagement is historical-forensic, offered as evidence in a causal argument about real-world events, and judged (by their own stated standards) by documentary proof. Treating Fortune's Binah-as-unconscious-mother reading and Webster's Sabbatai-Zevi-as-revolutionary-node reading as two data points in a single investigation combines a self-declared psychological metaphor with a historical-causal claim — a category error the two pairs of writers, whatever else divided them, would likely each recognize as a category error in the others' work.

**Griffin stands apart from all four.** His subject — central banking, fractional-reserve mechanics, war finance — requires no engagement with Kabbalah or Sabbateanism at all, and the book can be read start to finish without either concept appearing except in passing references to the Bavarian Illuminati.

**Documentary evidence of direct influence.** This is where the record is thinnest, and should be stated plainly. No document examined here shows Webster reading or influencing Antelman, Griffin, Crowley, or Fortune, or vice versa. Crowley and Fortune's relationship *to each other* is genealogically real and well documented — both drew on the shared curriculum of MacGregor Mathers' Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, and Fortune explicitly cites and builds on Crowley's published *777* material to "supplement the points upon which MacGregor Mathers, Wynn Westcott, and A. E. Waite... are silent" (Chapter IV), while noting she "never known [Crowley] personally" (Foreword). Griffin's demonstrable intellectual debts run to Carroll Quigley and W. Cleon Skousen, not to any of the other four. Antelman's demonstrable debt runs to Gershom Scholem's academic scholarship and to prior rabbinic sources (Emden), not to Webster. The only confirmed cross-influence among these five, in short, is the Crowley–Fortune relationship via the Golden Dawn; the broader sense that all five belong to "one conspiratorial-occult tradition" is a retrospective grouping — performed today largely by online writers splicing Golden Dawn correspondence tables together with Webster- and Antelman-derived political claims — rather than a documented historical fact about how these five writers actually read or responded to one another.
