# Usury

## Griffin's Definition, Up Front

Griffin does not treat usury as a minor moral footnote. He treats it as one of the load-bearing pillars of his entire case against the Federal Reserve, and he does so by explicitly discarding the dictionary and writing his own:

> "Centuries ago, usury was defined as any interest charged for a loan. Modern usage has redefined it as excessive interest... The dictionary, therefore, needs a new definition. Usury: The charging of any interest on a loan of fiat money."

Everything else in this note unpacks what that sentence means, why Griffin believed it was necessary, and how he builds the rest of his argument on top of it.

---

## Where This Concept Sits in the Book's Overall Argument

In the Preface, Griffin lists seven reasons the Federal Reserve should be abolished. He repeats this same list, verbatim, as the closing summary of the entire book — meaning usury is one of the very few ideas that literally bookends *The Creature from Jekyll Island* from first page to last:

1. It is incapable of accomplishing its stated objectives.
2. It is a cartel operating against the public interest.
3. **It is the supreme instrument of usury.**
4. It generates our most unfair tax (inflation).
5. It encourages war.
6. It destabilizes the economy.
7. It is an instrument of totalitarianism.

Reasons 3 and 4 are both developed in the same chapter — Chapter 10, "The Mandrake Mechanism" — under headers Griffin literally titles "THIRD REASON TO ABOLISH THE SYSTEM" and "FOURTH REASON TO ABOLISH THE SYSTEM." Usury is reason three. Griffin is explicit that this reason alone would justify his conclusion:

> "Even if there were no other reasons to abolish the Fed, the fact that it is the supreme instrument of usury would be more than sufficient by itself."

So usury isn't background texture in Griffin's book — it is one of a small number of independently sufficient grounds for his central conclusion, and understanding it is a prerequisite for understanding what he thinks the Fed actually *does* for a living.

## Traditional Usury vs. Griffin's Redefinition

| | Classical / historical definition | Modern dictionary definition | **Griffin's redefinition** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What triggers the charge of "usury"** | Charging *any* interest at all on a loan | Charging *excessive* or above-market interest | Charging *any* interest — regardless of rate — on a loan of **fiat money** |
| **What's being judged** | The mere existence of interest | The size of the interest rate | The **origin of the principal**, not the rate |
| **Where the line falls** | All interest is suspect | Only "excessive" interest is suspect | All interest on money that was never earned, saved, or actually possessed by the lender is suspect — even at a "normal," competitive market rate |

Griffin's move is deliberate and rhetorical: he notes that modern usage has softened usury into a question of *rate* ("excessive interest"), and he rejects that softening. But he doesn't simply revert to the ancient blanket prohibition on all interest either. He builds a third position — interest itself is not the problem; the problem is interest charged on money the lender never actually had to lend in the first place. That distinction is the entire hinge of his argument, and it only makes sense once you understand *how*, in Griffin's account, the Federal Reserve System actually creates money.

---

## The Foundation: How a "Pretended Loan" Comes to Exist

Griffin spends the chapters leading up to his usury discussion (Chapters 7–9, "The Barbaric Metal," "Fool's Gold," and "The Secret Science") laying the groundwork, because the usury argument is meaningless without it.

**The goldsmith origin story.** European goldsmiths, who had vaults to store their own gold, began renting vault space to depositors and issuing paper receipts redeemable in coin. Those receipts began circulating as money in their own right. Eventually the goldsmiths realized that since depositors rarely all withdrew at once, they could issue *more* receipts than they actually had gold to back — lending out receipts for coin that didn't exist. This, in Griffin's telling, is the birth of fractional-reserve banking.

**The Bank of England, 1694.** The world's first central bank institutionalized this trick as a permanent arrangement between bankers and politicians: politicians got spendable money created out of nothing, without having to raise taxes, and in return the bankers received, as Griffin puts it, "a commission on the transaction — deceptively called interest — which would continue in perpetuity."

**The Federal Reserve, today.** Griffin argues the Fed does exactly what the goldsmiths did, with one difference: the goldsmiths were at least constrained by the need to hold *some* real gold in reserve. The Fed holds none. He states flatly that the gold/silver backing of the American dollar is not 54%, not 15%, but 0% — pure fiat money.

He calls the actual money-creation process the **Mandrake Mechanism**, after the comic-strip magician who creates things out of nothing. In simplified form, as Griffin lays it out:

1. The federal government issues bonds (an IOU) to cover spending Congress won't fund through taxes.
2. The Fed buys those bonds with a check backed by nothing — new fiat dollars created at that instant.
3. The bonds are relabeled "reserves."
4. Using those "reserves" as a base, the banking system creates roughly **nine additional dollars** for every one dollar of reserves, available to lend to the public.
5. The public borrows this money — a "pretended loan," in Griffin's phrase, because the bank never actually possessed anything of value to lend.
6. Interest is charged on that pretended loan.

Griffin is emphatic on one technical point that matters to the usury argument: money isn't created and then loaned — it is *created by the act of borrowing itself*, and it vanishes again the instant the debt is repaid. As he puts it, "it is the act of borrowing which causes it to spring into existence." This is why he insists our money supply, in aggregate, represents debt rather than any actual store of value: if every debt in the country were repaid simultaneously, the entire money supply would disappear, down to the last dollar.

---

## The Moral Argument: Honest Transactions vs. Institutionalized Usury

Griffin is careful to say he has no objection to interest as such. He describes what he calls an **honest transaction**: a borrower pays an agreed sum for the temporary use of a lender's actual asset — a car, a tool, or money the lender genuinely earned through labor or saved. In that case, interest is simply rent, and the lender has "earned" the right to collect it:

> "If the money we are borrowing was earned by someone's labor and talent, they are fully entitled to receive interest on it."

But he draws a hard line at money that was never earned, saved, or owned by anyone before the instant it was lent — money summoned into existence, as he puts it, "by the mere stroke of a pen or the click of a computer key." His question is rhetorical but central to the whole chapter: *why should anyone collect a rental fee on that?*

He extends this into a description of ordinary bank lending itself. When a bank places a "loan" in your checking account, Griffin argues, it is not lending you anything it possesses — even the deposits of non-borrowing customers were themselves originally created out of nothing in response to someone else's loan. The bank, in his words, is "merely pretending to lend you money."

Griffin explicitly separates the legal question from the moral one. Legal tender laws compel people to accept fiat dollars in payment; that's a matter of law. Whether the banking cartel is *entitled*, in a moral sense, to collect interest on something it never possessed is, for Griffin, a completely separate question — and he invokes Thomas Jefferson's opposition to central banking to frame it:

> "No one has a natural right to the trade of money lender, but he who has money to lend."

This is the crux of Griffin's redefinition: usury, for him, is not about the *rate* charged, but about whether the "lender" ever actually had anything real to lend in the first place.

---

## Quantifying the Injustice: The $100,000 House

To make the abstraction concrete, Griffin walks through a numerical example. Consider a $100,000 home: $30,000 covers land, fees, and commissions; $70,000 covers labor and materials. If the buyer puts $30,000 down and borrows the remaining $70,000 at 11% over 30 years, the total interest paid comes to **$167,806** — roughly two and a half times what was paid to everyone who actually supplied the labor and materials.

Griffin concedes that if the lender had genuinely earned and saved that $70,000, this could be defended as fair compensation for the "time-value" of surrendering the use of real capital for decades. But since the lending institution never earned or saved the money — it created it — he poses the question that closes the example: *"What is the time-value of nothing?"*

He reinforces the point with a short, sharp quotation from Thomas Edison, criticizing financiers who contribute nothing physical to a project yet profit more from it than the people who actually build it:

> "People who will not turn a shovel full of dirt on the project nor contribute a pound of materials will collect more money... than will the people who will supply all the materials and do all the work."

(Griffin adds a caveat here worth preserving: he thought Edison only grasped half the problem. Edison objected to *interest-bearing* private bank money, but was comfortable with interest-free *government* fiat money — a position Griffin considers incomplete, since he believes fiat money is destructive through inflation and boom-bust cycles regardless of who issues it or whether interest is attached.)

---

## The Universal Toll: Usury as a Tax on Everything

Griffin generalizes from the single house example to the entire economy. Since, in his account, every dollar in circulation originated as a bank loan, every dollar in existence is perpetually earning compounded interest for the banking system that created it. He argues that a portion of *every* transaction — every business venture, every investment, every profit, even losses and tax payments — is effectively earmarked as tribute to the banks, even though the banks contributed neither their own capital nor their depositors' savings to generate it. This unearned, perpetual claim on the entire economy is what leads him to his verdict:

> "The flow of such unearned wealth under the guise of interest can only be viewed as usury of the highest magnitude."

---

## Resolving the Paradox: Where Does the Money to Pay the Interest Come From?

Griffin anticipates an obvious objection: if a bank creates exactly $10,000 for a loan but demands $10,900 back, where does the extra $900 come from, since the Fed never created it? Doesn't this force endless additional borrowing just to cover interest?

He calls this "a partial truth." It's true that the money supply doesn't include enough currency to cover principal *plus* interest simultaneously — but he argues the resolution isn't more borrowing, it's labor. He illustrates this with a small scenario: a borrower who owes $80 a month in interest takes a part-time job — say, waxing the bank's floors — for $80 a month. The same dollars the bank collected as interest go out again as wages, and come back again as loan repayment. The dollars never run short, because interest is ultimately extinguished through human work, not through fresh money creation.

He generalizes this into one of the chapter's most pointed conclusions:

> "The total of this human effort ultimately is for the benefit of those who create fiat money. It is a form of modern serfdom in which the great mass of society works as indentured servants to a ruling class of financial nobility."

For Griffin, this is the real mechanism by which usury is paid: not by the money supply expanding to cover it, but by the labor of the entire population, funneled continuously back to whoever holds the franchise to create money out of nothing.

## Corroboration: The System's Own Words

Griffin builds this argument almost entirely from primary sources — Federal Reserve publications and congressional testimony — rather than from outside critics, which he treats as more damning than any external accusation could be.

- **Federal Reserve Bank of New York**, in a booklet titled *I Bet You Thought*, states that currency "cannot be redeemed, or exchanged, for Treasury gold or any other asset used as backing," and that banks "create money by 'monetizing' the private debts of businesses and individuals."
- **Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago**, in *Modern Money Mechanics*, states plainly that neither paper currency nor deposits have any value as commodities — a dollar bill is "just a piece of paper," and deposits are "merely book entries."
- **Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis** describes the monetary system's "fiat base — literally money by decree."
- **Marriner Eccles**, Federal Reserve Governor, testified before Congress in 1941 under questioning from Congressman Wright Patman:

  > ECCLES: We created it.
  > PATMAN: Out of what?
  > ECCLES: Out of the right to issue credit money.
  > PATMAN: And there is nothing behind it, is there, except our government's credit?
  > ECCLES: That is what our money system is. If there were no debts in our money system, there wouldn't be any money.

- **Robert Hemphill**, Credit Manager of the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, wrote in the foreword to Irving Fisher's *100% Money* that if every bank loan in the country were repaid, not one dollar of currency or deposit would remain in existence — calling this dependency on debt-based money "a staggering thought."

Griffin uses this testimony to establish that his claim isn't speculative: the system's own officials and publications confirm, in their own words, that the "loans" underlying the money supply are conjured rather than lent from any real reserve — which is precisely the condition his redefinition of usury targets.

---

## How Usury Connects to the Rest of Griffin's Framework

Usury isn't isolated from the book's other arguments — Griffin ties it directly into two of the "Natural Laws" he lays out in Appendix B as the underlying behavioral logic of the whole book:

- **Natural Law No. 4** (fractional money degenerates into fiat money): Griffin notes that fractional money "earns just as much interest for the bankers as does gold or silver," which is precisely why the temptation always exists to expand the money supply until the reserve fraction shrinks to zero. Usury, in other words, is the *motive* that drives fractional-reserve banking inexorably toward pure fiat money.
- **Natural Law No. 5** (men with power over the money supply will eventually use it to confiscate their neighbors' wealth): Usury, for Griffin, is the concrete mechanism through which this abstract "natural law" plays out in practice — it's how the confiscation actually happens, transaction by transaction.

He also situates usury within the same chapter as **Reason #4** ("It generates our most unfair tax") — inflation. Both reasons flow from the identical Mandrake Mechanism: usury is the *direct* extraction (interest collected on created money), while inflation is the *indirect* extraction (the loss of purchasing power caused by expanding the money supply to create that same money). They are, in Griffin's structure, twin consequences of one mechanism rather than separate problems.

Finally, the broader theme of controlling a nation through control of its money recurs later in the book. In Chapter 11 ("The Rothschild Formula"), Griffin quotes Mayer Amschel Rothschild's famous line, "Let me issue and control a nation's money and I care not who writes the laws" — not as a discussion of usury specifically, but as evidence that the power Griffin attributes to institutionalized usury (a permanent, compounding claim on a nation's wealth) is, in his account, the same power that has historically translated into political and geopolitical control.

---

## Griffin's Conclusion

Griffin's chapter-ten summary distills the entire argument into a single, deliberately blunt line:

> "Charging interest on pretended loans is usury, and that has become institutionalized under the Federal Reserve System."

For Griffin, this is not a peripheral defect that better regulation could fix — it is the System's actual business model. Congress gets spendable money without the political cost of raising taxes; the banking cartel gets what he calls "a perpetual override on every American dollar that exists in the world," collected in perpetuity on capital it never earned, saved, or possessed. He states that understanding this one arrangement is functionally equivalent to understanding the entire Federal Reserve System: "If you understand this paragraph, you understand the Federal Reserve System."

Because this extraction is, in his telling, structural rather than a matter of individual wrongdoing or fixable policy, his conclusion is not reform but abolition — usury, as he redefines it, is offered as one of seven independently sufficient reasons why the Federal Reserve System should be dismantled entirely, restoring money creation to a foundation Griffin considers honest: one where interest can only be charged by someone who actually had something of real value to lend.

## Quick-Reference Glossary (Griffin's Terms)

| Term | Griffin's meaning |
|---|---|
| **Usury** (redefined) | The charging of *any* interest on a loan of fiat money — regardless of the rate |
| **Fiat money** | Money with no commodity backing, valuable only because law compels its acceptance |
| **Pretended loan** | A "loan" in which the lender never possessed any real asset to lend; the money is created at the moment of borrowing |
| **Honest transaction** | A loan of an asset (money or otherwise) that the lender genuinely earned, saved, or owned before lending it |
| **The Mandrake Mechanism** | Griffin's name for the process by which the Fed converts government debt into fiat money via bond purchases and fractional expansion |
| **Modern serfdom** | Griffin's characterization of a society whose labor perpetually services interest owed on money it never truly borrowed from a real lender |
