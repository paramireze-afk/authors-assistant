# Case Studies in Proximity: Chabad's Documented Encounters with Political and Financial Power

This piece is a companion to a biography of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson. It does not attempt to connect the following case studies into a single network or argue that they reflect coordinated action. Each is investigated on its own terms, using the evidence-type framework below, because that is the only way to keep a document like this honest: a donation is not a conspiracy, a blessing is not a mandate, and a shared building is not evidence of shared purpose — but each of these is also a real, specific, and often genuinely interesting fact worth understanding precisely rather than either inflating or dismissing.

**A framework for what kind of fact each case represents:** (1) personally met Schneerson; (2) Chabad member or religious participant; (3) attended Chabad events; (4) donor to a Chabad organization; (5) family relationship with someone involved in Chabad; (6) institutional or business relationship; (7) political relationship; (8) ideological or theological influence; (9) evidence of actual coordination; (10) an apparent association for which stronger evidence could not be found. Categories 1–7 are documented facts. Categories 8–9 require a much higher evidentiary bar and are not established merely because 1–7 are true.

## Benjamin Netanyahu and Schneerson: What the Documented Meetings Actually Show

**Category: 1 (personally met Schneerson); 3 (participant in a recurring Chabad practice); 7 (political relationship, later in his career).**

The best-documented episode is a private meeting in November 1990, captured on video and widely circulated (including by Chabad's own media outlets and by Israeli press), between Schneerson and Netanyahu, then a Knesset member who had recently served as Israel's ambassador to the United Nations. In the recorded exchange, Netanyahu asks for Schneerson's blessing "in all areas, both personal and political"; Schneerson responds that "many things have progressed" since their previous meeting but that Moshiach "still hasn't come," tells him to "hasten the coming," and when Netanyahu replies "we're doing," answers that it is "apparently not enough" since hours of the day remain. This exchange is real and precisely as described — it is one of thousands of comparable filmed blessings Schneerson gave visitors of every background across four decades, a practice central to his public role rather than a mark of special favor toward Netanyahu specifically.

Netanyahu's participation in this practice was not unique among Israeli politicians of the era; seeking a rebbe's blessing before an election or political milestone was and remains common across the religious-Zionist and Haredi-adjacent spectrum of Israeli politics, regardless of party. What is *not* documented, despite wide circulation of the claim in commentary and some religious-nationalist media (e.g., Israel National News, byfaith.org), is any primary transcript or recording in which Schneerson specifically predicts Netanyahu will become "Israel's last prime minister" before the Messiah's arrival, or any documented instruction from Schneerson to Netanyahu on a specific policy question. This is a documented instance of Category 10: an apparent claim, repeated across secondary sources, for which the underlying primary evidence (the video itself, and Chabad's own contemporaneous archival material) does not support the specific, more dramatic version now circulating. What the video establishes directly is Netanyahu's participation in a widespread religious practice and Schneerson's characteristic insistence — extended to nearly every visitor — that redemption remained unfinished. As prime minister, Netanyahu has at various points publicly invoked Schneerson approvingly and Chabad-affiliated institutions have operated in Israel with government cooperation typical of major religious organizations, but no primary source located here documents a sustained personal correspondence or advisory relationship between the two men comparable to, say, Schneerson's documented relationships with his own emissaries.

## Sheldon and Miriam Adelson: A Three-Decade, Well-Documented Philanthropic Relationship

**Category: 1 (personally met Schneerson, once, before marriage); 4 (substantial, sustained donor); 6 (institutional relationship with a specific Chabad house).**

This is among the more concretely documented relationships in this article, drawn from Chabad's own detailed account (published on Chabad.org following Adelson's 2021 death, corroborated by independent Jewish press coverage in the Times of Israel, JTA, and CrownHeights.info) and from public philanthropic records. Roughly two weeks before their 1991 wedding, Sheldon and Miriam Adelson traveled to 770 Eastern Parkway — reportedly at the urging of a rabbi named Osher Bukiet, who accompanied them — to receive Schneerson's blessing, joining the general line of visitors who came for this purpose. Schneerson gave the couple several dollars for charity, including, according to Chabad's account, one designated "for the family," and when Miriam Adelson (speaking Hebrew) asked for a blessing for a child, Schneerson reportedly responded, "Why do you speak in singular? A child needs a brother and sister," and gave two additional dollars — the couple later had two children. This was, on the documented record, a single meeting rather than an ongoing personal relationship with Schneerson himself, who died three years later.

The far more substantial and sustained relationship was institutional, developing after that meeting with Chabad-Lubavitch of Southern Nevada, established in Las Vegas in 1991 by Rabbi Shea and Dina Harlig — the same year as the Adelsons' meeting with Schneerson. Over subsequent decades the Adelsons became major and, per Rabbi Harlig's account, personally close supporters of Chabad of Southern Nevada, funding a building that bears the family name (one of eight Chabad centers in Las Vegas) and, according to Harlig, engaging in a wide range of additional, often unpublicized charitable giving through this specific relationship. The Adelsons' broader philanthropic footprint — documented through the Adelson Family Foundation and the Adelson Charitable Trust, both of which file public disclosures — runs into the hundreds of millions of dollars for Jewish and Israel-related causes generally (Birthright Israel alone received roughly $400 million over time, according to Aish and Chronicle of Philanthropy reporting), of which support for Chabad institutions (including, per InsidePhilanthropy's grant database, Chabad at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas) is one part among many recipients spanning Jewish day schools, medical research, and Israel advocacy organizations — meaning Chabad was one beneficiary within a much larger philanthropic portfolio, not a uniquely privileged one. Sheldon Adelson's separately, extensively documented role as a major Republican political donor (widely reported using the term "kingmaker") is a distinct fact from his family's Chabad philanthropy; no source located here establishes a causal or coordinated relationship between the two beyond the fact that both were expressions of the same individual's broader convictions about Jewish identity, Israel, and conservative politics.

## The Kushner Family and Harvard Chabad: A Genuinely Substantial, Well-Documented Relationship

**Category: 3 (sustained, personal, multi-year attendance); 4 (documented, large individual and family-foundation donations); 5 (a relationship spanning two Kushner generations and extending through marriage); 6 (institutional relationship, later including political consequence).**

This is the most extensively documented and, on the evidence, most substantial individual relationship examined in this article, corroborated across multiple independent outlets (Times of Israel, JTA, Ami Magazine, Forward, eJewishPhilanthropy) drawing on on-the-record interviews with Rabbi Hirschy Zarchi, who founded Harvard Chabad in 1997.

Jared Kushner's relationship with the institution began during a pre-freshman visit to campus, where he attended a Shabbat dinner, and continued through his undergraduate years (he graduated in 2003). Per Zarchi's detailed, on-record account, Kushner was "instrumental in bringing undergraduates to Chabad," which had previously been oriented toward graduate students; he attended services and Shabbat dinners regularly, recruited fellow students (including bringing friends from his final club, the Fly Club), performed manual labor for the house (a widely repeated anecdote has him shoveling snow after a study session), and — while still an undergraduate, "a sophomore or junior" per Zarchi — handed the rabbi a personal check for $18,000, which Zarchi describes as one of the largest single gifts the house had received even from a parent, let alone a student. Kushner spoke at Harvard Chabad's 2003 building dedication, with his father Charles Kushner in attendance. Separately, the Charles and Seryl Kushner Family Foundation gave a documented $342,500 to various Chabad causes between 2003 and 2013, according to tax-filing analysis reported by Ami Magazine. During the 2004–2005 period when Charles Kushner was facing federal prosecution (he pled guilty in 2004 to tax evasion, witness tampering, and illegal campaign contributions, and served roughly a year in federal prison), the extended Kushner family — including Jared's younger brother Joshua, then also a Harvard undergraduate — reportedly continued to have Friday-night dinners with the Zarchis at Harvard Chabad, a period Zarchi describes as demonstrating the same family loyalty Kushner later showed his father-in-law.

The relationship persisted well past graduation: Kushner helped organize Harvard Chabad alumni events over subsequent years, and he and Ivanka Trump hosted a Chabad alumni Shabbat dinner together at some point before his White House role began (JTA, 2017). Three days before the 2016 election, Kushner and Ivanka Trump visited the Ohel — Schneerson's gravesite in Queens, a site of ongoing pilgrimage for Chabad followers and others seeking blessing — a documented visit reported at the time and again referenced in later coverage of the family's Chabad ties. As a senior White House adviser, Kushner was reportedly behind President Trump's 2017 clemency decision for Sholom Rubashkin, a prominent Chabad community figure convicted of financial fraud in 2009 (Category 7, a specific and well-documented instance of political action connected to Chabad-community advocacy, though distinct from any claim about Schneerson's personal wishes, since Schneerson died fifteen years before Rubashkin's conviction). In Washington, Kushner and Ivanka Trump settled near, and according to multiple reports attended, "The Shul" — the Chabad Lubavitch synagogue led by Rabbi Levi Shemtov, whose own account (Times of Israel, 2020) describes a "substantive relationship" with the family without elaborating on specifics, citing a preference to let high-profile congregants "escape celebrity" rather than seek it. Most recently, in November 2024, Jared and Joshua Kushner and their respective spouses (Ivanka Trump and Karlie Kloss) jointly pledged $2 million to Chabad of the United Arab Emirates following the murder of Chabad emissary Rabbi Zvi Kogan in Dubai — a discrete, individually verified act of philanthropy explicitly tied to Kushner's separate, well-documented role as a lead negotiator of the 2020 Abraham Accords, which he cited directly in his public statement.

Taken together, this is a rare case in which the underlying record genuinely supports "substantial" rather than "incidental": a multi-decade, multi-generational, financially significant, and reciprocally acknowledged relationship between a specific family and a specific Chabad institution and its rabbi. What it does not establish, absent further evidence, is that this relationship shaped specific U.S. foreign policy decisions beyond the one directly on-record instance (the Rubashkin clemency) — the Abraham Accords donation, for instance, followed rather than preceded or explained Kushner's role in those negotiations.

## Ivanka Trump: A Conversion Outside Chabad, and a Later, Separate Chabad Connection

**Category: 3 (attendance at a Chabad synagogue after 2017, per multiple reports); 10 (the common assumption that her conversion involved Chabad is not supported by the record).**

It's worth stating this precisely because the two facts are frequently conflated in casual commentary: Ivanka Trump's conversion to Judaism, completed before her 2009 marriage to Jared Kushner, was overseen by Rabbi Haskel Lookstein of Congregation Kehilath Jeshurun, a prominent Modern Orthodox — not Chabad — synagogue on Manhattan's Upper East Side. Lookstein has no documented Chabad affiliation; Israel's Supreme Rabbinical Court's 2016 decision not to recognize his conversions (a separate controversy concerning the boundaries of Orthodox conversion authority in Israel, unrelated to Chabad) briefly raised questions about Trump's Jewish status under Israeli religious law without affecting her status in the U.S. Modern Orthodox community that recognized the conversion. The couple's documented Chabad connection is a distinct, later fact: after relocating to Washington in 2017, multiple reports (CNN, Times of Israel) describe them attending "The Shul," Rabbi Levi Shemtov's Chabad congregation near their D.C. home — an attendance relationship (Category 3) rather than a doctrinal or conversion-related one.

## Mark Zuckerberg and Harvard Chabad: A Thinner Record Than Often Assumed

**Category: 10, primarily, with a modest exception.**

Harvard Chabad, founded by Rabbi Hirschy Zarchi in 1997, was an established campus institution — including, from 2003, its own dedicated building — throughout Zuckerberg's time as an undergraduate (fall 2002 through spring 2004, when he left to develop Facebook full-time). This overlap is real and worth stating plainly. What is not well corroborated is any sustained, personal relationship between Zuckerberg and the institution comparable to Kushner's. The most specific claim circulating — that Zuckerberg was photographed dancing at a Harvard Chabad Purim party in March 2004, shortly after Facebook's February 2004 launch — traces, in this research, to a single social-media post (an X/Twitter account) rather than to a dated primary photograph independently verified by a news organization, Chabad's own archives, or Zarchi himself; it should be treated as an unverified claim rather than an established fact pending stronger sourcing. What *is* independently and repeatedly documented, across Chabad's own Lubavitch.com coverage and social media, is a much later event: in May 2017, when Zuckerberg returned to Harvard to receive an honorary degree and deliver the commencement address, Rabbi Zarchi greeted him publicly, posting a photo captioned "Welcome back, @zuck!" — a friendly, on-the-record acknowledgment of some prior familiarity, but one consistent with either a substantial student-era relationship or a much lighter one (Zarchi's Chabad House, as a general campus institution serving "Jews of all backgrounds and degrees of observance," would very plausibly have had at least incidental contact with a prominent Jewish undergraduate without that rising to sustained personal involvement). No primary source examined here — biographies of Zuckerberg, contemporaneous Harvard Crimson coverage of Facebook's founding, or Zuckerberg's own public statements — describes him as a regular participant in Harvard Chabad programming during his student years in the way Zarchi describes Kushner. The claim examined and rejected in earlier research in this series — that Zuckerberg's father sought Rabbi Schneerson's personal blessing before Mark's birth, later inspiring Facebook's founding — remains, as previously established, fabricated internet folklore with no primary corroboration and family biographical details that don't match Zuckerberg's actual background; it should not be conflated with the separate, much more mundane, and considerably better (if still thin) documented fact of Harvard Chabad's general campus presence during his time there.

## Berel Lazar and Vladimir Putin: A Documented, Institutionally Consequential Relationship With a Clear Origin Story

**Category: 7 (a sustained, high-level political relationship), fully documented in its own right rather than traceable to Schneerson personally.**

This case is worth treating in real depth because it is, on the evidence, the single most institutionally consequential relationship in this article — and because it developed entirely after Schneerson's 1994 death, making it a clear example of a post-Schneerson institutional development rather than a legacy relationship he personally cultivated.

Rabbi Berel Lazar, born in Milan to Chabad-emissary parents, was dispatched to the Soviet Union as a young Chabad emissary in the late 1980s, part of the movement's long-standing (and, under Schneerson, genuinely prioritized) effort to rebuild Jewish religious life suppressed for seven decades under Communism. Lazar settled in Moscow in 1989 and built a career within Chabad's post-Soviet infrastructure, participating in the founding congress of the Russian Jewish Congress (RJC) in 1996 — an umbrella group then led by the oligarch Vladimir Gusinsky — before helping establish, in 1997, the Federation of Jewish Communities of the CIS (FJC), representing Chabad-affiliated communities across former Soviet states. In 1999–2000, in a move multiple independent sources (JTA, Times of Israel, the Forward, Jewish Standard) describe as deepening a real and lasting split within Russian Jewry, the FJC elected Lazar as chief rabbi of Russia, a title also separately claimed and held since 1993 by Rabbi Adolf Shayevich of the rival Congress of Jewish Religious Organizations and Associations in Russia (KEROOR) — the two bodies eventually signed a formal 2015 nonaggression pact agreeing not to criticize each other publicly, without resolving the underlying dispute over the title itself. Per Wikipedia's sourcing (flagged there as needing citation, but consistent with the broader independent press narrative), Lazar had his first meeting with Vladimir Putin the same month the FJC was founded, in late 1999 — shortly before Putin became acting president. This timing is significant to the independent journalistic accounts of the relationship: Gusinsky and Shayevich had publicly criticized Russian government conduct in the Chechen war and alleged human-rights abuses; Lazar, breaking from that umbrella structure, adopted an explicitly different posture, later telling JTA directly that "challenging the government is not the Jewish way" and that a chief rabbi should be "apolitical, not a government critic." Roman Bronfman, a former Israeli lawmaker who has written on Russian-Jewish emigration, is quoted by JTA describing the Lazar-Putin relationship as a "beneficial symbiosis": Lazar's consistent public support functions, in Bronfman's assessment, as "the basis of his claim to the title of chief rabbi," while Putin gains a religious ally whose public statements about Russia's treatment of Jews and religious minorities serve the Kremlin's broader narrative.

The relationship is not merely characterized in retrospective commentary — it is documented in real time through recurring, publicly recorded meetings, including a formally documented January 2026 Kremlin meeting (transcript published on the Russian presidential website, en.kremlin.ru) between Putin, Lazar, and FJC president Alexander Boroda, at which Putin stated, "I am delighted to see you; we do meet on a regular basis," and both men spoke about Holocaust remembrance and interfaith cooperation in Russia — a genuine, primary-sourced, and long-standing pattern of institutional access rather than a one-time photo opportunity. Lazar has publicly and repeatedly credited Putin as Russia's "most pro-Jewish leader," crediting him with fighting antisemitism "more vigorously than any Russian leader before him" (Times of Israel, 2015) — a characterization some critics, cited in the same reporting, view as providing political cover for a government whose broader human-rights record they contest. The FJC under Lazar has grown to operate in roughly 160 cities across Russia and other former Soviet states (compared to 34 for the rival KEROOR structure), per JTA's reporting, giving Chabad's institutional structure in the region — a structure with no equivalent anywhere else in the world in terms of proximity to a single head of state — real, ongoing, and well-documented political access. What this case does not establish, and what no source examined here claims, is that Chabad as a global movement, or Schneerson's own teachings, directed or anticipated this specific relationship; it is a development of Chabad's decentralized post-Soviet institution-building under Lazar's own leadership choices, made years after Schneerson's death, in response to the specific political conditions of 1990s Russia.

## U.S. Presidents and Chabad: A Long, Formalized, Bipartisan Pattern

**Category: 7, well documented, and structurally different from the individual relationships above because it runs through Congress and the presidency as institutions rather than through personal friendship.**

Beginning in the Carter administration and continuing through every subsequent president into the Clinton years, the U.S. government issued annual proclamations recognizing "Education and Sharing Day, U.S.A." on Schneerson's birthday (11 Nissan), commemorating his emphasis on moral and character education; the underlying joint congressional resolution was renewed multiple times, including in 1991, under both Republican and Democratic congressional majorities. A representative documented instance: in 1993, a delegation of eight Lubavitcher rabbis met with President Clinton at the White House in connection with that year's Education Day, exchanging gifts (Clinton received a tzedakah box, a dollar bill, and a personalized yarmulke; the delegation received a presidential proclamation and congressional resolution), reported contemporaneously by JTA. In April 1994, following Schneerson's death that June, Congress passed and President Clinton signed a resolution posthumously awarding him the Congressional Gold Medal for "outstanding and lasting contributions toward improvements in world education, morality, charity, and acts of kindness" — a formal, documented act of the U.S. government reflecting broad cross-denominational and bipartisan respect accumulated over decades of public visibility, philanthropic work, and moral-education advocacy, rather than evidence of any specific policy relationship. This pattern of presidential and congressional engagement with Chabad as an institution — visits, proclamations, White House menorah lightings that have continued and arguably expanded since Schneerson's death — is genuinely bipartisan and long-standing, spanning presidents across the ideological spectrum, which is itself informative: it indicates a broadly recognized charitable and educational institution accumulating the same kind of ceremonial governmental recognition major religious and civic organizations of comparable scale typically receive, not a relationship unique to any single administration or political tendency.

## After 1994: Who Actually Controls Chabad, and How the Money Works

This section stands somewhat apart from the individual case studies above because it concerns institutional structure rather than personal relationships, and it directly addresses how a religious movement without a living central leader has continued to expand for three decades.

**The succession vacuum and the legal fight over Chabad's assets.** Schneerson died on June 12, 1994, without naming a successor — a genuine break from every prior transition in Chabad's roughly 200-year leadership history. Authority over the movement's central institutions passed, per his will, to Agudas Chasidei Chabad, the umbrella nonprofit corporation that holds title to core communal property, including 770 Eastern Parkway itself and Chabad's extensive library of religious books and manuscripts. This proved genuinely and publicly contentious: a protracted legal dispute, formally *Agudas Chasidei Chabad of United States v. Gourary*, litigated in U.S. federal court through the 1980s and resolved in a 1987 judgment (predating Schneerson's death but directly bearing on post-1994 control) and further disputes into the 1990s, pitted Agudas Chasidei Chabad against Barry Gourary, a grandson of the sixth Rebbe (Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn) and nephew by marriage to Schneerson through the Gourary branch of the family, over ownership of thousands of books and manuscripts from the movement's central library, which Gourary had removed and attempted to sell. The federal court ruled decisively that the library belonged to the organization, not to the Schneersohn/Gourary family personally — a legally significant precedent establishing that Chabad's core assets were institutionally, not dynastically, owned, which mattered enormously for how authority could function without a hereditary successor after 1994.

**Institutional rather than personal governance.** In practice, authority over Chabad's central functions since 1994 has rested with the lay and rabbinic boards of three organizations Schneerson himself had headed for decades before becoming Rebbe: Merkos L'Inyonei Chinuch (education), Machne Israel (social services), and the Kehot Publication Society (publishing), together with Agudas Chasidei Chabad as the overarching legal entity. No individual has held, or claimed with broad recognition, the title of eighth Rebbe. This is a genuinely unusual arrangement for a Hasidic dynasty, where leadership has traditionally been both personal and hereditary, and it is inseparable from the movement's internal messianism debate discussed in the biography: for the "meshichist" faction that maintains Schneerson remains, in some sense, the living head of the movement, the absence of a successor is not an institutional gap to be filled but confirmation of an ongoing reality; for others within Chabad, it is simply an unresolved practical question the movement has, so far, managed by committee rather than by charismatic succession.

**How the shluchim system is actually financed.** This is the structural feature most directly relevant to understanding Chabad's post-1994 growth. Each Chabad House, whether serving a university campus, a small city, or a foreign capital, is financed overwhelmingly through local fundraising conducted by the resident emissary couple — membership dues where applicable, local philanthropic gifts (of exactly the kind documented in the Kushner and Adelson cases above), event revenue, and increasingly, in recent decades, systematic annual giving campaigns and endowment-building — rather than through a continuous subsidy from Brooklyn. Merkos L'Inyonei Chinuch provides training, program materials, some seed funding for new placements, and a shared institutional brand and identity, but a Chabad House that fails to sustain local fundraising does not receive open-ended central bailout in the way a traditional denominational branch office might. This decentralized financing model is, according to the analysis of scholars including Sue Fishkoff and Samuel Heilman, the single most important structural reason Chabad's expansion did not stall after 1994: each of the movement's now several thousand emissary families is, in effect, running an independently sustained local nonprofit franchise bound to the others by shared training, shared publications, shared holiday programming, and shared identity rather than by financial dependency on a central office or a living central leader. This is also precisely the structural feature that makes wealthy individual donors — Adelson to a single Las Vegas Chabad House, Kushner to Harvard Chabad — proportionally significant to specific local institutions in a way they would not be to a more centralized religious bureaucracy, and it explains why documented Chabad philanthropy in this article clusters around specific local rabbis and houses (Harlig in Las Vegas, Zarchi at Harvard, Shemtov in Washington) rather than around a single central Chabad treasury.

## Synthesis: What the Case Studies Show, and What They Don't

Read together, without forcing them into a single narrative, these cases show a consistent pattern worth stating plainly: Chabad, across Schneerson's lifetime and especially after his death, built an unusually effective structure for generating deep, personally meaningful, often financially significant relationships with individuals who later became prominent in business, politics, or both — not because Chabad targeted the powerful specifically, but because its decentralized, locally rooted, welcoming-to-all-levels-of-observance model was, by design, equally available to a Las Vegas casino developer before he was famous, a Harvard undergraduate before his father's political career mattered, and a chief rabbi navigating post-Soviet Russia's specific power structure. Some of these relationships — Kushner's with Harvard Chabad, Adelson's with Chabad of Southern Nevada, Lazar's with the Russian state — are genuinely substantial by any reasonable standard: sustained over years or decades, financially significant, reciprocally acknowledged by named individuals on the record, and in Lazar's case, institutionally consequential for Chabad's standing in an entire country. Others — the Zuckerberg case in particular, and the more dramatic claims about Netanyahu — turn out, on close examination of the primary record, to be considerably thinner than widely circulated commentary suggests, resting on a single ambiguous photograph claim or a single filmed meeting whose actual content is more modest than the retrospective narrative built around it.

What none of these cases individually or collectively establish is coordination: no primary source examined in this or the companion biography shows Chabad, Schneerson, or his successor institutions directing the political or business conduct of any of the individuals discussed, as opposed to receiving their voluntary religious participation, philanthropy, and public goodwill — the same currency any successful religious or civic institution accumulates from its most engaged and, in some cases, wealthiest adherents. The most defensible summary is also the least dramatic one: Schneerson built an institution whose specific organizational design — radical decentralization, welcome extended regardless of observance level, financial self-sufficiency at the local level — made it unusually well-positioned to form deep, lasting bonds with individuals wherever they happened to be in their lives, including in the years before some of them became powerful. What those individuals did with the power they later acquired, and how much their formative religious experiences at a specific Chabad house shaped those later choices, is a genuinely interesting and in most cases still open question — but it is a different, harder, and more human question than whether a network exists, and the documentary record assembled here answers the second question more confidently than the first.
