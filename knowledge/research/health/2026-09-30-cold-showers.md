# The Case Against Fluoridation: What the Critics Argue

## Introduction

For nearly 80 years, a persistent group of scientists, activists and parents has argued that adding fluoride to public drinking water is a mistake. Their case rests on four pillars: possible harm to the developing brain, other physical side effects, a claim that fluoride works mainly on the tooth surface rather than when swallowed, and an ethical objection to medicating a whole population without individual consent.

The most prominent organized voice is the [Fluoride Action Network](https://fluoridealert.org/researchers/fluoride-iq-studies/) (FAN), led for years by retired chemistry professor Paul Connett, co-author of *The Case Against Fluoride*. In recent years Robert F. Kennedy Jr., through Children's Health Defense and now as Secretary of Health and Human Services, has carried the argument into national politics.

This article lays out the case as those critics make it, in their own terms, and closes with the main counterarguments so readers can weigh both.

## The brain: fluoride and children's IQ

Critics' central claim is that fluoride is a developmental neurotoxin, and that the fetus and infant are most at risk. FAN counts 87 human studies on fluoride and intelligence published between 1989 and 2023, and says 78 of them linked higher fluoride exposure to lower IQ ([FAN](https://fluoridealert.org/researchers/fluoride-iq-studies/)).

They point to several landmark papers:

- **Choi et al., 2012**, a Harvard-affiliated meta-analysis that critics summarize as finding roughly 7 fewer IQ points in higher-fluoride groups in 26 of 27 studies, most from China.
- **Bashash et al., 2017** (Mexico) and **Green et al., 2019** (Canada, *JAMA Pediatrics*), which tied fluoride levels in pregnant women's urine to lower IQ in their children. Critics stress that Canadian mothers lived in ordinary fluoridated cities.
- **Riddell et al., 2019**, which reported links between fluoride and ADHD symptoms in Canadian youth.

The 2024 federal National Toxicology Program review is the critics' strongest card. It concluded with moderate confidence that fluoride above 1.5 mg/L is associated with lower child IQ. FAN argues this effectively puts fluoride alongside lead as a brain toxin, and that several of the best studies came from areas fluoridated at the U.S. level of 0.7 mg/L ([FAN on the NTP report](https://fluoridealert.org/content/ntp-report-finds-large-body-of-evidence-that-fluoride-exposure-is-consistently-associated-with-lower-iq-in-children/)).

Critics also highlight formula-fed babies. At trial in 2024, FAN's attorney Michael Connett described infants fed formula mixed with tap water as receiving the highest dose of any age group relative to body weight. Paul Connett has long urged that pregnant women and parents who bottle-feed be warned to avoid fluoride.

That lawsuit, *Food & Water Watch v. EPA*, ended in September 2024 with Judge Edward Chen ruling that fluoridation at current levels poses an "unreasonable risk" under the Toxic Substances Control Act and ordering the EPA to respond. Critics treat the ruling as vindication after decades of dismissal.

They also frame the history as one of suppression, citing toxicologist Phyllis Mullenix, whose 1995 animal study suggested brain effects and who was fired from the Forsyth dental institute soon after ([Connett's account](http://fluoridealert.org/content/a-ringside-view-of-the-battle-between-science-and-politics-over-fluorides-neurotoxicity/)).

## Teeth, bones and hormones

Beyond the brain, critics argue fluoride's effects show up across the body, starting with the teeth it is meant to protect.

**Dental fluorosis.** Critics point out that fluorosis, white streaks or spots on enamel caused by too much fluoride while teeth form, now affects a large share of American adolescents. Some critics cite figures around 40%. They argue these marks are visible proof that children are swallowing more fluoride than intended, and a sign that other tissues are absorbing it too ([Society of Environmental Journalists summary](https://www.sej.org/node/1404)).

**Bones.** Roughly half of the fluoride a person ingests over a lifetime is stored in bone. Critics warn that early skeletal fluorosis produces joint pain and stiffness that is easily mistaken for arthritis, so it may go unrecognized. Crippling fluorosis is rare in the U.S. but common in parts of India with high natural fluoride. Critics also raise a debated link between fluoride and osteosarcoma, a bone cancer, in adolescent boys.

**Thyroid and other glands.** Critics lean on a 2006 National Research Council report, which noted thyroid effects in humans at certain daily fluoride doses, and at lower doses when iodine intake is poor. They argue some Americans in higher-fluoride areas reach these ranges. British researcher Jennifer Luke's finding that fluoride accumulates in the pineal gland, which makes melatonin, is also frequently cited.

**Kidneys and sensitive groups.** Because fluoride is cleared through the kidneys, critics argue people with kidney disease retain more of it. They also note that heavy water drinkers, such as athletes, outdoor workers and people with diabetes, get a larger dose from a "one size fits all" concentration.

## "It works on the tooth, not in the stomach"

Critics argue that even if fluoride helps teeth, swallowing it is unnecessary. Fluoridation began in 1945 on the theory that fluoride had to be ingested and built into developing teeth. Since then, critics note, even fluoridation's supporters have come to say most of the benefit comes from fluoride touching the tooth surface, as toothpaste does.

If the benefit is topical, critics reason, putting fluoride in water exposes every organ to a substance that only needs to reach the teeth. Toothpaste can be spat out; tap water cannot.

They also point to Western Europe, where most countries do not fluoridate water, yet tooth decay has fallen at rates similar to fluoridated countries. Critics credit fluoride toothpaste, better diets and dental care for that decline, not water.

Finally, they cite a 2015 Cochrane review, an independent evidence summary widely respected by researchers. It found few high-quality modern studies on fluoridation's benefit, and much of the strong evidence predated widespread fluoride toothpaste. Critics read this as showing the benefit today is smaller and less certain than officials claim.

## Consent, dose and the chemicals themselves

Many critics say their deepest objection is ethical rather than scientific. Fluoridation, in their view, is mass medication: a substance added to treat a disease, given to everyone without individual consent or medical supervision.

**No control over dose.** A doctor prescribes medicine by weight, age and health. Water fluoridation sets a concentration, not a dose. An infant on formula, a marathon runner and a person with kidney disease all drink different amounts, and fluoride also arrives from toothpaste, tea and processed foods. Critics argue nobody knows how much any one person is getting.

**Hard to opt out.** Avoiding fluoridated water requires reverse-osmosis filters, distillation or bottled water. Critics note these cost money, so lower-income families, often the stated targets of fluoridation, have the least choice.

**The source of the fluoride.** Most U.S. systems use fluorosilicic acid, which critics highlight is recovered from the pollution scrubbers of phosphate fertilizer plants. It is not pharmaceutical grade and can carry trace contaminants such as arsenic. Critics argue this chemical has never been tested for safety the way a drug would be.

**Freedom of choice.** Critics add that anyone who wants fluoride can easily get it from toothpaste, rinses or a dentist. Taking it out of water, they argue, removes nothing from people who want it while restoring choice to those who don't.

## Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and the political turn

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. turned a long-running fringe debate into national policy. Before the 2024 election he said a new Trump administration would advise every U.S. water system to remove fluoride on its first day, and he called fluoride "industrial waste."

Kennedy's case draws on the same evidence FAN has championed, but he states it more sweepingly. He has linked fluoride to IQ loss, ADHD, thyroid problems, arthritis, bone fractures and cancer. Standing with Utah lawmakers in April 2025, he declared the evidence against fluoride overwhelming ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/fluoride-supplements-fda-rfk-jr-ken-paxton-restrictions)). His former organization, Children's Health Defense, covered the 2024 EPA trial day by day and presented it as a turning point.

Since Kennedy became Secretary of Health and Human Services, the critics' movement has won real ground:

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 2026 | EPA published a preliminary plan for a new fluoride toxicity assessment ([Genetic Literacy Project](https://geneticliteracyproject.org/2026/04/08/rfk-jr-vs-fluoride-the-next-big-health-brawl-science-be-damned/)) |
| 2025 | FDA acted against ingestible fluoride supplements for children; Texas opened probes into fluoride toothpaste marketing |
| April 2025 | Kennedy said he would tell the CDC to stop recommending fluoridation; EPA announced a review of new science ([PBS/AP](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/rfk-jr-will-tell-cdc-to-stop-recommending-fluoride-in-drinking-water)) |
| 2025 | Utah became the first state to ban fluoridation, followed by Florida; several other states weighed bills |
| September 2024 | Federal judge ordered EPA to address fluoride's risks under toxic substances law |

For supporters, this marks the moment decades of warnings were finally taken seriously. Kennedy has acknowledged removing fluoride from water may lead to more cavities, but he and his allies argue that is a price worth weighing against possible harm to children's brains.

## What the other side says

Dentists, most public health researchers and fluoridation's defenders dispute much of the critics' case. Readers should weigh these points too.

- **The IQ evidence is mostly about higher doses.** The NTP's moderate-confidence finding applied to water above 1.5 mg/L, more than twice the U.S. level of 0.7 mg/L. Many studies in FAN's count come from regions in China, India and Iran with much higher natural fluoride, and many were rated low quality. Studies in New Zealand, Australia and elsewhere at normal fluoridation levels found no IQ effect ([ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2025/april/rfk-jr-to-tell-cdc-to-change-fluoride-guidelines/)).
- **Association is not causation.** Critics' key pregnancy studies are observational. Skeptics argue they cannot fully rule out other factors and that their results have not been consistently replicated.
- **Most fluorosis is cosmetic.** Defenders say the fluorosis seen in fluoridated U.S. areas is overwhelmingly very mild, often invisible to a layperson, and not a health harm.
- **Several health claims are overstated.** The ADA calls some of Kennedy's claims false, including thyroid damage, weakened bones and arthritis at normal levels, and his claim that Europe bans fluoride. Several European countries fluoridate salt or water instead.
- **The benefit is real and equalizing.** Supporters credit fluoridation with reducing decay by roughly 25%, with the biggest gains for low-income children who see dentists least. Early reports from places that stopped fluoridating, such as Calgary, found more cavities in children afterward.
- **Removing supplements removes the alternative.** Critics say fluoride should come from toothpaste or supplements, yet the FDA and Texas actions have made some of those harder to get, especially in rural areas.

Where things stand: both sides agree high fluoride is harmful, and both agree the evidence at 0.7 mg/L is contested. The open questions are whether effects exist at that level, how big they are, and how they compare to the dental benefit. The EPA's new assessment, still under way in 2026, may shape the next chapter.
