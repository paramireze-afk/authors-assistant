# The Bolshevik Revolution

## Griffin's Thesis, Up Front

Griffin's central claim, stated at the close of Chapter 13, is unambiguous: "The Bolshevik revolution was not a spontaneous uprising of the masses. It was planned, financed, and orchestrated by outsiders... It was a perfect example of the Rothschild formula in action." For Griffin, the Revolution is not a digression from his monetary history of the Federal Reserve — it is one of his central pieces of evidence that central banking, fiat money, and a small network of allied financiers are capable of manufacturing the outcome of a national revolution, arming both sides of it, and profiting from whichever faction survives. He devotes two full chapters (13, "Masquerade in Moscow," and 14, "The Best Enemy Money Can Buy") to the case, and treats it as the clearest available illustration of the **Rothschild Formula** operating in the twentieth century.

---

## Setting the Stage: The Russo-Japanese War and Schiff's Grudge

Griffin traces the origins of Wall Street's involvement back to 1904, when **Jacob Schiff**, head of the New York investment firm Kuhn, Loeb & Company, financed large war loans to Japan in its war against Russia — funding that helped Japan cripple the Russian fleet. Schiff was personally decorated by the Japanese Emperor for his role. But Griffin identifies a second, less publicized dimension of Schiff's involvement: during the war, Schiff paid for the printing of a ton and a half of Marxist propaganda, distributed to Russian prisoners of war by Russian-speaking revolutionaries trained in New York. When roughly 50,000 of those prisoners returned home after the war, Griffin argues they became "virtual seeds of treason against the Tsar," later playing a significant role in fomenting military mutiny during the 1917 revolution.

---

## Trotsky as Schiff's Agent

Griffin presents **Leon Trotsky's** time in America as direct evidence of financial sponsorship. Expelled from France, Trotsky arrived in the United States in January 1916 at Schiff's invitation, with his travel expenses paid by his host. While in New York, Trotsky wrote for the socialist paper *Novy Mir* and gave revolutionary speeches — all while his family enjoyed, in Trotsky's own account from his memoir *My Life*, a chauffeured limousine placed at their service by "a wealthy friend identified as Dr. M." Griffin notes the irony directly: "the family of the great socialist radical, defender of the working class, enemy of capitalism," enjoying "the very symbols of capitalist luxury" — almost all of it, Griffin states, paid for by Jacob Schiff.

When the Tsar abdicated in March 1917, a mass celebration was held at Carnegie Hall. A telegram from Schiff, unable to attend, was read to the crowd and published in the *New York Times*, describing the revolution as "what we had hoped and striven for these long years." Griffin also cites a 1949 newspaper report in which Schiff's own grandson stated that Schiff had given roughly $20 million toward the triumph of Communism in Russia.

---

## The Halifax Arrest — and the Release

When Trotsky finally departed New York for Russia in 1917, he was intercepted by British and Canadian naval authorities at Halifax and found to be carrying $10,000 — a sum Griffin notes was widely assumed at the time to be German money, given that Germany was at war with Russia and had an obvious interest in destabilizing it. Griffin argues the actual evidence points instead to Kuhn, Loeb & Company as the source.

Trotsky's detention triggered an immediate, high-level intervention. Griffin documents telegrams arriving at Halifax from sources as varied as an obscure New York attorney, the Canadian Deputy Postmaster-General, and a senior British military officer, all urging his release. The mechanism behind this pressure, per Griffin: Sir William Wiseman, head of British Secret Service in America, lived in the apartment directly above **Edward Mandell House** — President Wilson's closest personal adviser — and the two were close friends. House informed Wiseman that Wilson wanted Trotsky released; Wiseman relayed this to his government; the British Admiralty ordered Trotsky's release on April 21. Griffin underscores that Trotsky could not even have obtained an American passport to begin the journey without Wilson's personal intervention, quoting historian Anthony Sutton's description of Wilson as "the fairy godmother" of the Russian Revolution.

## The Same Pattern in Britain

Griffin argues this was not a purely American phenomenon. Citing eyewitness historian **Arsène de Goulevitch**, he notes that British financier **Lord Alfred Milner** is reported to have spent over 21 million roubles financing the Russian Revolution, with **Sir George Buchanan**, the British Ambassador to Russia, also implicated. Griffin observes that undermining Tsarist Russia — Britain's own wartime ally — would ordinarily be treasonous, and asks what "higher loyalty" could compel British officials to act against their own battlefield partner. His answer is the subject of the next section.

## The Secret Society Behind the Money

This is the section of the book where Griffin traces the financiers' shared loyalty to its root, building a lineage he treats as historically documented rather than speculative — citing extensively from Carroll Quigley's *Tragedy and Hope*, whose author, Griffin notes, was a Georgetown professor who claimed to have had two years of privileged access to the group's private papers and records, and who described himself as broadly sympathetic to the network's goals.

| Stage | Figure / Institution | Griffin's account |
|---|---|---|
| 1870 | **John Ruskin** (Oxford) | Taught that a small ruling class — "the eternal superiority of some men to others" — should control the means of production for the good of all. |
| Student | **Cecil Rhodes** | So moved by Ruskin's lecture that he copied it by hand and carried it for thirty years; built a fortune (with backing from the Bank of England and Rothschild interests) monopolizing South African diamonds and gold. |
| Rhodes's wills | **The secret society** | Rhodes's first five wills (before the well-known Rhodes Scholarship will) left his fortune to found a secret society dedicated to expanding the British Empire — co-created with his principal trustee, **Lord Alfred Milner**. |
| Inner circle | **The Round Table** | Headquartered in England; Rhodes as leader, with Milner and others (Brett/Lord Esher, journalist W.T. Stead) forming its executive structure. |
| Subordinate branches | **Round Table Groups** | Established in other countries as local extensions of the network. |
| American branch | **Council on Foreign Relations (CFR)** | Griffin states plainly: "The Round-Table Group in the United States became known as the Council on Foreign Relations," initially dominated by J.P. Morgan and later the Rockefellers. |

Griffin quotes Quigley's own description of the network's goal: "nothing less than to create a world system of financial control in private hands able to dominate the political system of each country and the economy of the world as a whole," operated "in a feudalist fashion by the central banks of the world acting in concert." Griffin also recounts Quigley's claim that his publisher suppressed *Tragedy and Hope* after its significance was recognized — destroying the printing plates and falsely claiming the book was simply out of print — which Griffin presents as corroborating evidence that the network did not want this history widely known.

He is direct about the implication for Russia specifically: "Agents of these two groups cooperated closely in pre-revolutionary Russia," with Milner's network operating through official British diplomatic channels while the American contingent required a different kind of cover — which brings the narrative to the Red Cross.

## The Mask: The Red Cross Mission

Griffin argues that the American financiers involved in Russia could not operate as diplomats or declared businessmen, so they entered "disguised as Red Cross officials on a humanitarian mission." He documents that the American Red Cross itself had effectively been captured by Wall Street money well before the war — J.P. Morgan personally contributed $100,000 to its 1910 fundraising campaign, and Morgan partner Henry P. Davison chaired both that campaign and, later, the Red Cross War Council.

### "Colonel" William Boyce Thompson

President Wilson appointed **William Boyce Thompson** — a mining speculator turned financier with directorships spanning Chase National Bank, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and multiple industrial and insurance companies — to head the Red Cross Mission in Russia, personally funding its entire expense including military-style uniforms. Thompson lived lavishly in Petrograd, was given the imperial box at the opera, and was described by contemporaries as "the American Tsar" and, per historian George Kennan, viewed by the Kerensky government as the "real" American ambassador.

Griffin's key piece of evidence: Thompson syndicated the purchase of ten million roubles in Russian bonds, gave over two million roubles to Kerensky for propaganda — **and**, together with J.P. Morgan, gave the rouble equivalent of one million dollars to the Bolsheviks for spreading revolutionary propaganda into Germany and Austria. Griffin includes a photograph of the actual cablegram documenting the transfer. He quotes Anthony Sutton's assessment directly: Thompson's motive was not ideology but market capture — "Thompson was willing to place seed money on any political power vehicle that would achieve his objective... Ideology could sway revolutionary operators like Kerensky, Trotsky, Lenin et al., but not financiers."

### Raymond Robins and the telephone call

When Thompson returned to the U.S., his successor was **Raymond Robins** — a self-described "anti-capitalist" whose two personal heroes, Griffin notes with evident irony, were Theodore Roosevelt and **Cecil Rhodes**. Robins developed an extraordinary personal relationship with Lenin. Griffin reproduces British agent Bruce Lockhart's account of Robins phoning Lenin directly to demand the dismissal of a Bolshevik official (Trotsky's own nephew) who had been rude to him — and getting an answer within ten minutes: Lenin capitulated.

Griffin's summary of what this reveals: a man financed by Wall Street bankers was, in reality, "the intermediary between the Bolsheviks and the American Government." The mission's own personnel eventually admitted the truth of its purpose. Thompson's assistant Cornelius Kelleher, describing a naive colleague who genuinely believed he was running a medical relief operation, wrote that the man "was in reality nothing but a mask — the Red Cross complexion of the mission was nothing but a mask."

## Two Revolutions, Not One

Griffin insists on a distinction he says mainstream history collapses: there were **two** Russian revolutions in 1917, not one.

| Revolution | Date | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **February Revolution** | March 1917 | A genuinely broad, moderate uprising that installed Alexander Kerensky's provisional socialist government; Lenin was still in Switzerland and Trotsky was still in New York when it happened. |
| **October Revolution** | Late October 1917 | Not a popular uprising at all but, in Griffin's words, "a coup d'etat" — a small, disciplined Bolshevik minority exploiting the confusion of the new government to seize military and communication centers by force. |

Griffin cites historian Leonard Schapiro's account to debunk the popular "storming of the Winter Palace" myth: the government's defenders had dwindled to a few hundred women soldiers and roughly two thousand cadets, the famous cruiser *Aurora* fired only a single blank shell, and the eventual "storming" amounted to a small detachment entering an already-abandoned palace, with total casualties of three wounded cadets. He reinforces this with journalist Eugene Lyons's account (from *Workers' Paradise Lost*) that Lenin and Trotsky "did not overthrow the monarchy. They overthrew the first democratic society in Russian history," representing "the smallest of the Russian radical movements" while openly scorning the idea of needing a genuine mass base — quoting Lenin's own words: "Give us an organization of professional revolutionaries... and we will turn Russia upside down."

Griffin's payoff line, closing the loop on the "humanitarian" and "anti-Tsarist antisemitism" cover stories offered by the participants themselves: **"Lenin and Trotsky were not sent to Russia to overthrow the anti-Semitic Tsar. Their assignment from Wall Street was to overthrow the revolution"** — meaning the genuinely popular, moderate February Revolution, which threatened to produce a stable, self-governing Russia outside financier control.

## The Payoff: What Happened After

Griffin's method for testing whether the financing effort actually succeeded is evidentiary rather than speculative: if the plan had failed, he argues, we should expect declining Western interest in Russia; if it succeeded, we should expect continued involvement and visible profit-taking. He presents a list of documented outcomes:

- **Banking:** After the October Revolution, virtually every bank in Russia was nationalized by the Bolsheviks — except the Petrograd branch of Rockefeller's National City Bank.
- **Industry:** Heavy industry was nationalized across the board — except the Westinghouse plant, established by Charles Crane, one of the financiers who had traveled to Russia aboard the same ship as Trotsky.
- **International banking:** In 1922, the Soviets' first international bank was organized not by the state but by a private syndicate including former Tsarist, German, Swedish, British, and American bankers — its foreign division directed by Max May, Vice President of Morgan's Guaranty Trust Company.
- **Contracts:** A steady stream of large, non-competitive Soviet contracts flowed to British and American firms connected to the Round Table network in the years immediately following the revolution — the largest being a fifty-million-pound food contract to Chicago meatpacker Morris & Company, whose ownership was linked by marriage to a former "Major" of the Red Cross Mission.

Griffin treats this pattern — the systematic survival of specific Western financial interests inside an otherwise fully nationalized economy — as the clearest available proof that the original financing effort achieved exactly what it was meant to achieve.

## The Rothschild Formula in Action

Griffin explicitly frames the entire episode as a real-world application of the theoretical model he develops in Chapter 11 (see the companion note on **The Rothschild Formula**). The relevant points of the Formula and their Russian application:

| Formula principle | How Griffin applies it to Russia |
|---|---|
| A credible enemy must exist, or be manufactured | Financing was directed at building a Bolshevik movement capable of credibly threatening the existing order. |
| Uncooperative governments must be replaced | The moderate, self-governing Kerensky provisional government — which threatened to stabilize Russia outside financier influence — was undermined in favor of a faction more susceptible to Wall Street's leverage. |
| No side should win decisively; fund both | Thompson funded Kerensky *and* the Bolsheviks simultaneously; Milner's network funded the Tsar's opponents while Britain remained the Tsar's wartime ally. |
| Profit accrues regardless of outcome | The post-revolution survival of National City Bank, Westinghouse, and lucrative Soviet contracts show the financiers profiting no matter which faction ultimately held power. |

Griffin's own summary line for the episode, echoing his broader theory: it was "a repeat of the ploy that had worked so well for the cabal many times in the past. Round Table members were once again working both sides of the conflict to weaken and topple a target government."

## Sources and Evidence Griffin Relies On

Griffin builds his account almost entirely from named historians, participants' own memoirs, and government documents rather than secondary paraphrase:

- **Anthony C. Sutton**, *Wall Street and the Bolshevik Revolution* (1974) — Griffin's central secondary source, cited repeatedly for financial documentation and direct quotations.
- **Carroll Quigley**, *Tragedy and Hope: A History of the World in Our Time* (1966) — the primary source for the Rhodes-Milner-Round Table-CFR lineage, including Quigley's personal claim of insider access.
- **Leon Trotsky**, *My Life* (1930) — quoted directly regarding his New York patronage.
- **George F. Kennan**, *Russia Leaves the War* and *The Decision to Intervene* — cited for the Kerensky-era diplomatic record.
- **R.H. Bruce Lockhart**, *British Agent* (1933) — the British intelligence officer's memoir, quoted extensively for the Robins-Lenin relationship.
- **Hermann Hagedorn**, *The Magnate: William Boyce Thompson and His Time* (1935) — Thompson's biography.
- **Leonard Schapiro**, *The Russian Revolutions of 1917* — cited to debunk the Winter Palace storming myth.
- **Eugene Lyons**, *Workers' Paradise Lost* — a former Bolshevik sympathizer's later, disillusioned account.
- **Arsène de Goulevitch**, *Czarism and Revolution* — an eyewitness account used for the British financing claims.
- U.S. government records, including Senate Judiciary Subcommittee hearings on Bolshevik propaganda and U.S. State Department decimal files.

## Griffin's Conclusion

For Griffin, the Bolshevik Revolution demonstrates that the Rothschild Formula is not confined to eighteenth-century Europe — it is a living operating principle that carried directly into the twentieth century and, in his account, continued afterward through the Cold War (see "The Rothschild Formula" for the Korea, Vietnam, and Gulf War applications he draws in Chapter 14). The Revolution matters to his larger argument for three reasons: it shows financiers deliberately manufacturing and funding *both* sides of a political conflict; it identifies the specific secret network (Rhodes → Milner → Round Table → CFR) that Griffin argues continues to operate at the top of American foreign policy; and it establishes a direct personnel and institutional bridge — through figures like William Boyce Thompson and financial houses like J.P. Morgan — connecting the same network responsible for financing the Bolshevik coup to the network that, three years earlier, had drafted the Federal Reserve System at Jekyll Island.
