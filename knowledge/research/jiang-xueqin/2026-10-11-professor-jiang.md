# The Rupture: Professor Jiang's Grand Unified Theory of a World Coming Apart

On a weeknight in Toronto, a room of forty-odd paying Substack subscribers sat down to steak and listened to Jiang Xueqin, the former teacher whose "Predictive History" channel went from roughly 500 YouTube subscribers to nearly three million in a single year, lay out what he called a "grand unified theory" of the present moment.

His title came from Mark Carney. Earlier in the year, at the World Economic Forum, Canada's prime minister described the world as living through a *rupture*: the old order, Pax Americana, dying, and something new taking its place. Jiang's project for the evening was to explain what that rupture actually is, why it is happening, and what it predicts about the next three months.

What followed was part geopolitics seminar, part Platonic philosophy, part theology, and, by the end, part spiritual sermon. It is also, by any standard, deeply controversial. What follows is his argument as he made it.

---

## Four crises in one week

Jiang opened by surveying what he saw as the major crises of the week.

**The war in Iran.** Nobody, he said, knows why it started, what American goals are, or how it can end. What began as a US effort to stop an Iranian bomb has spread regionally. The Houthis in Yemen are now fighting Saudi Arabia and its allies, which he listed as including the UAE, Pakistan and Turkey. He warned that the war could eventually link up with Ukraine into a global conflict, and that cutting Middle Eastern energy off from the world would mean not just higher diesel prices (already happening) but potentially global famine.

**The world turning right.** His example was Brazil. Lula, the long-time incumbent credited with lifting millions out of poverty, was expected to win easily. Instead Flávio Bolsonaro, son of the house-arrested former president Jair Bolsonaro, came close to winning outright in the first round and is, in Jiang's telling, expected to win the runoff. Across Latin America, he said, countries are becoming more pro-Trump, pro-Israel and nationalistic.

**The US–Canada trade war.** For a Toronto audience, the most personal of the crises. Carney frames it as a war over Canadian sovereignty, over who Canada is allowed to trade with. Jiang pointed to the Parti Québécois winning a minority government in Quebec on a promise of a third independence referendum, and to a *Globe and Mail* report that Republican operatives had been advising secessionists in Quebec and Alberta. Lose either province, he said, and "Canada is done for." Carney, meanwhile, was retaliating with tariffs aimed at battleground states before the US midterms, which Jiang read as a deliberate attempt to push American voters toward the Democrats. "This is not just a trade war," he said. "It's also a political war."

**The Gen Z protests.** From Nepal earlier in the year to millions of young Indians demanding the resignation of the election commissioner to students in France skipping school for increasingly violent street protests, some of them invoking the Paris Commune. "There doesn't seem to be an off-ramp," he said. "It's almost like the French Revolution over again."

His claim: these are not four stories. They are one.

## Everything you learned in school is upside down

To get there, Jiang first dismantled what he called the standard civics-class model of a democracy. In that model, power flows upward from the people: shared norms and values become common law, common law becomes government, and government builds the schools, the media, the culture and the military that defend our freedoms. This is social contract theory, which he attributed to Rousseau.

"What I want to show you tonight," he said, "is that this is wrong, and this is the opposite of what really happens."

### The cave

His alternative began with Plato's allegory of the cave. Imagine a million people chained in darkness since birth, able to see only a blank wall. Behind them burns a great fire, and around the fire move people Plato simply calls "they." "They" hold up puppets, and the prisoners watch the shadows.

Because the shadows are all the prisoners have ever known, they build a whole civilization around them. They invent language to name the shadows and myths to explain them. They specialize: poets supply the vocabulary, priests turn it into religion, teachers pass it on. Eventually they believe so completely that the shadows are reality that they will kill anyone who tells them otherwise.

### The real stack

Jiang then mapped the cave onto the modern world, layer by layer.

At the top, the puppeteers: an alliance he described as occultists ("people who think they are in connection with God"), secret societies (he named the Freemasons and Jesuits), and ruling families (often called the Illuminati, he said, though who exactly they are "doesn't matter").

Beneath them, the enforcers who keep the prisoners chained: the mafia, intelligence agencies, the military.

Then the shadows themselves. Originally religion: paganism, then monotheism. Today, he argued, the dominant religion is money, produced by a cartel including the Federal Reserve, Wall Street, the City of London and banks like JP Morgan. Money creates the global economy, and the global economy creates corporations.

Above that sit the abstractions we mistake for power: the UN, the World Bank, the IMF, the nation-state. "Rather than us believing that it is these societies that control us, we believe instead that we are controlled by nation states."

And finally the indoctrination layer: schools, media and culture, which produce our norms, our values and our legal system. His example of how culture lies: diamonds. Films are full of heists over them; video games make "diamond" the top rank above gold and platinum. Yet, he insisted, diamonds are "absolutely worthless." The *New York Times*, BBC and CNN, he said, do the same thing with ideas.

## Reason versus emotion

What is this whole edifice teaching? In Jiang's account, a single lesson: **reason is superior to emotion.**

He illustrated with the teaching of evolution. In school, believers in intelligent design are treated as idiots, and Hollywood reinforces the message in *Inherit the Wind*, its dramatization of the Scopes trial, where Clarence Darrow becomes the hero who rescues a teacher's right to teach.

But strip the words down, Jiang argued, and "reason" turns out to mean **capital**. A rational businessman takes money from anyone regardless of race or religion. Capital requires the free movement of people, goods and ideas, which in turn implies the "open society": liberalism, individualism, progress, women's equality, immigration, multiculturalism.

"Emotion," then, is everything that obstructs that flow: religion and spirituality, nationalism, family and community, tradition, prejudice.

"This is the real struggle of modernity," he said. "How to remove people from God and have people embrace capital." Doubt Darwin at school and you're called insane; doubt COVID vaccines and you're an "anti-science bigot."

He was careful to add that this system "led to tremendous prosperity and peace in the world. That cannot be denied." The problem is that every system ossifies.

## How the system rotted

Jiang identified three decays.

**Reason became rules.** Being "reasonable" once meant exercising judgment and empathy. Now it means compliance. His example was COVID vaccine mandates for restaurants, hospitals and flights, which he argued any person using their own reason would have questioned.

**Community became the individual.** Ask a Toronto teenager in the 1980s or 90s what they wanted to be and they'd talk about contributing to their community. Ask today, he said, and they want a million Instagram followers.

**Innovation became bureaucracy.** The entrepreneurial energy of earlier decades has given way, in his view, to a comfortable, tenured class of government employees.

Why do systems always decay like this? Jiang's answer was Peter Turchin's theory of **elite overproduction**: societies generate more aspiring elites than there are positions of power, and the resulting competition ossifies and eventually breaks the system. "This is the time we are living in."

## The war over who gets to print money

Here is the core of the theory.

When an elite becomes stagnant, a **counter-elite** rises to tear it down and replace it. And the central prize in our system, Jiang argued, is the power to create money.

Most people, he said, don't understand that banks create money from nothing. When you take out a mortgage for a Toronto condo, the money doesn't come from depositors; a banker presses a key and it exists. That makes the cartel of private banks (Wall Street, the Fed, the City of London) the true rulers of society.

The counter-elite is Big Tech: the AI companies and the "tech bros." To win, they need to take the money-printing power away from the banks, and their weapon is **digital currency and digital ID**. If you could get a loan from Google or Apple instead of TD Canada Trust, they would hold the keys to reality. And unlike banks, they don't need the US dollar as a reserve currency; they can issue their own stablecoins.

But to replace the banks, the counter-elite must first destroy the entire edifice that supports them: the petrodollar, the US dollar itself, and the intellectual class that legitimizes it. That means Harvard and Yale, the *New York Times* and the BBC, Hollywood. "If I want to rebuild the world, I first need to destroy it."

Then he applied the framework to each crisis.

### The Iran war: a war meant to be lost

Why start a war you're losing? "You can never get to an answer unless you change your perspective and say: what if they went into this war knowing they would lose it, and they wanted to lose?"

His reasoning runs through the petrodollar. Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states sell energy priced in dollars, mainly to East Asia, which turns it into manufactured goods for the world. The Gulf then recycles its dollars into US Treasuries. That arrangement let Americans live extravagantly on cheap borrowing, built a $40 trillion debt, and supplied the liquidity that now fuels the AI boom.

The Iran war breaks the loop. No Gulf oil exports means no fuel for Asian factories and no petrodollars flowing back into Treasuries. Fewer buyers for US debt raises the risk of a sovereign debt crisis. And it threatens to pop what he called the AI bubble, describing OpenAI as essentially a Ponzi scheme that is unprofitable and lives on endless new capital to buy compute.

That sounds catastrophic, he conceded. "But what if all this is intentional?"

If the dollar loses its reserve status, what replaces it? Not the yuan, not the ruble. Stablecoins, issued by tech companies. And if the AI bubble bursts and drags the US into recession, Washington will have no choice but to bail out the AI firms, just as it bailed out the banks in 2008, by nationalizing data centers and taking equity stakes. Freed from the crushing cost of their infrastructure, the AI companies become profitable overnight, backed by state power, and positioned to issue the currency that replaces the dollar.

The losers are the banks, which are heavily exposed to the Gulf. "If the GCC falls, if Ukraine falls, a lot of these private banks go bankrupt."

### APEC in Shenzhen

Wouldn't China and Russia resist? Watch November, Jiang said, when Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin are expected to share a room at the APEC summit in Shenzhen. He predicted they would announce some kind of global AI governance body, and argued that "AI governance" really means governance of digital currency and stablecoins. "They don't care about AGI or ASI. They don't care about that crap."

### Canada: a colony of the City of London

The trade war fits the same pattern. Canada, in Jiang's telling, is "a resource colony for the City of London," the epicenter of the global banking system. Carney's résumé (Goldman Sachs, governor of the Bank of Canada, then the first foreign-born governor of the Bank of England) marks him as London's man, not Canada's. That is why Carney speaks of a "rupture": he intuitively understands that the banks are being pushed aside by the AI companies. And that is why, Jiang said, Trump's people are working to split off Quebec and Alberta. In a trade war between the world's biggest consumer and a seller, he added, Canada cannot win.

### Brazil: the algorithm votes

The mainstream explanation for Brazil's rightward swing, Jiang said, is the rise of evangelical Christians from around 8% of the population to about 20%, voters he characterized as Christian Zionists focused on Israel. What the papers won't tell you, he said, is that Brazil's social media runs on American platforms (Meta, Google, YouTube) and that those companies are "tweaking the algorithm in order to create election results that they want." He offered a local test: in the Toronto mayoral race, where polls show Olivia Chow well ahead of Brad Bradford, he predicted Bradford would win, citing Bradford's outspoken support for Israel.

### Gen Z: AI exposes the priests

Finally, the protests. Young people, he said, are afraid of being replaced by AI. But AI's real function is not to become a godlike superintelligence. It is to expose the "intellectual priest class" (teachers, lawyers, doctors, professors) whose job is to maintain the simulation. "How do we know? Because the shitty ChatGPT is able to do what they're doing." Discrediting that class is, in his framing, the tech companies' main attack vector against the banking cartel.

## Putting the theory on the line

To his credit, Jiang ended the lecture by making the theory falsifiable and naming what would prove him wrong. He listed three events within a month:

| Event | Date (as stated) | What conventional wisdom expects | What Jiang's theory expects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alberta referendum vote | October 19 | Defeated; only 20–30% support independence | Trump calls it rigged regardless; a pro-secession result is possible, possibly via social-media manipulation, opening the door to US interference (he floated "peacekeeping troops") |
| Israeli election | October 27 | Netanyahu defeated | Netanyahu wins, because he is "key to the plans in the Middle East" |
| US midterms | Early November | Democratic sweep of House and Senate | Party matters less than how many incumbents are replaced by candidates backed by AI-industry money, preparing Congress for a bailout |

"If the Alberta referendum is defeated, if Netanyahu loses the Israeli elections, and if the Democrats sweep the midterms, then in theory everything I said tonight is just wrong," he said. (Note that the third condition sits uneasily with his own claim that a Democratic win doesn't matter.)

On the midterms he cited enormous AI-linked political spending, giving rough figures of around $90 million from Elon Musk, about $100 million from Marc Andreessen and his partner Ben Horowitz, and $50 million from another tech donor.

If the theory holds, he laid out this sequence:

1. **An AI-company IPO fails.** He singled out Anthropic, claiming it planned to list right after the midterms seeking a roughly $2 trillion valuation and about $100 billion in capital, which he called impossible. A failed IPO would starve the company of money for compute, which would hit Oracle and Broadcom, and the interlinked AI economy would collapse "like a Ponzi scheme."
2. **A bipartisan bailout.** A Democratic Congress and a Trump White House rescue the AI industry, while the media celebrates the two parties working together.
3. **An AI governance announcement at APEC.** "I almost guarantee you."
4. **Escalation in Iran,** used to bring down the global economy and with it the banks' Gulf collateral.
5. **The introduction of digital ID and digital currency.**

"I have absolutely no idea if this is correct or not," he said. "We'll know over the next three months."

## The Q&A: from Starlink to the Messiah

The question-and-answer session ran nearly two more hours and ranged far beyond the lecture.

### Corporations that want to be countries

Asked about digital ID being built into operating systems, Jiang pointed to everyday conditioning: airline check-in desks replaced by kiosks, AI agents everywhere, workers whose real job is now to train the AI that will replace them, Waymo cars and camera networks in California and Texas.

Asked about Starlink becoming a US mobile carrier, he described it as part of tech companies building their own complete infrastructure, a replacement for the undersea cables that physically are the internet. That led him to the **network state**, the Silicon Valley idea of a corporation functioning as a country. Companies like Google, Meta and OpenAI, he said, resent depending on nation-states and on voters who might complain about data centers draining their water and power. Corporations are monarchies and therefore efficient; the dream is to prove that. He cited Peter Thiel's move to Argentina as an attempt to carve out such a state.

### Why automation won't save the West

An automation-industry worker asked whether a weaker dollar plus robotics might revive American manufacturing. Jiang was skeptical on two counts. Onshoring fails because the US economy is built around finance, few young men want a $30–40/hour factory job when they could gamble on crypto, and no one beats China on cost given its disregard for environmental and labor protections. Automation fails because robots need a global supply chain (lithium from South America's lithium triangle, copper from Chile and Peru, Chinese-processed silicon), and that supply chain is exactly what's breaking. In his blunt formulation, importing illegal immigrants is cheaper. The likely outcome instead: transnational corporations from the US, China and Russia building their own independent supply chains together.

### "The nation-state is an illusion"

Pressed on how stablecoins would change relations among the great powers, Jiang told the audience to drop the idea of nations altogether: "Russia, China, United States, Canada, these are all just illusions." The real struggle inside every country is between the financial elite and the AI counter-elite. The counter-elite has formed what he called a grand alliance of **nationalism, technology and religion**, which put Trump in power, and which Trump needs a third or even fourth term to consolidate against the "deep state." The old financial elites of China, Russia and America, he claimed, are "best friends" who attend the same parties, send their kids to the same schools and intermarry, so Trump, Xi and Putin must cooperate to replace them with AI-aligned power centers.

### Three futures

A listener asked whether the coming suffering might finally lead people to break their chains and turn toward love and neighborliness. Jiang described three possible outcomes for the next decade:

- **The AI elite wins.** New masters, a new simulation, and within a generation nobody remembers life before AI, just as we can't remember life before money.
- **Mutual destruction.** Finance and AI are too evenly matched and civilization collapses.
- **The "messianic age."** He said some religious hardliners, including figures in Israel and Christian Zionists, believe the world must be made unbearable before humanity will awaken and leave the cave.

All three, he argued, historically end in "the same crap": a new elite running a new simulation. His own answer was to commit to children. Every child, he said, comes from the "monad," the divine source, and an education that nurtures a child's spirituality could unlock latent powers he suggested might include telepathy or prophecy. He said he intends to spend the rest of his life building networks of spiritual communities as a counterweight to the network state.

### The war on education

A Nigerian-born former student leader at McMaster spoke passionately about classical education, noting that *educare* means to reach out, and arguing that education should serve the soul rather than make people servile. Jiang agreed and went further. Schools teach children that they are blank slates who need habits of mind and "critical thinking," which Jiang called mere categorization and reduction. Children, by contrast, naturally synthesize; they "see the soul in people, in animals." Cutting them off from God, he said, is "a lobotomy," because creativity, imagination and empathy flow from that connection. The meritocracy, from kindergarten to university to a good job, exists to enforce it. "The school system we have today, it's pretty evil. It's designed to be evil."

### MK-Ultra, Epstein and the elite obsession with children

This was the darkest stretch of the evening. Jiang said the CIA's MK-Ultra and related programs tried to unlock latent abilities in children through trauma, and that they were run not by fringe figures but by the top psychiatrists of their day, whose protégés went on to shape education and medicine. He floated, while saying "I'm not saying anything," a link between Kamala Harris and MK-Ultra's Montreal site near McGill, where her mother worked.

Asked by an audience member about youth organizations and groups that train children, he claimed elites in every culture and era have been "obsessed with children," citing Jeffrey Epstein and Dante's *Inferno*, in which Florentine aristocrats burn for sodomy. He offered three explanations:

- **Love.** Billionaires can buy anything except being loved for themselves; a child with no conception of their wealth might. He said this is what was happening on Epstein's island and named several famous men associated with Epstein.
- **Energy.** He raised the adrenochrome theory, that frightened children emit a rejuvenating chemical, while saying he didn't know if it was true, then reframed it: children have an innocent, divine energy that inspires adults. As a father of three at fifty, he said, he has never worked harder.
- **Trauma.** Elites traumatize their heirs because trauma produces dissociation, and dissociation produces stress tolerance, the "special power of the elite." His example was Trump: "the world hates Donald Trump, but guess what, he disassociates." Having been traumatized, he said, they feel a "natural urge to traumatize children as well."

"These are the three possibilities," he concluded, "and quite honestly they are probably all true."

### Advice for a new father

The mood lifted when an expectant father asked how to think about raising a child into this future. Jiang's answer was gentle. Have faith. Don't plan too far ahead. Your child chose to come into a breaking world, because that is "the easiest path to wisdom." A soul could stay comfortable in the spirit world; it comes here for what it can't experience there, including pain. Love your child unconditionally, which is harder than it sounds.

He contrasted a streamer he'd recently met, who struck him as open-minded and willing to be wrong because his parents clearly loved him, with celebrities who chase fame because they were unloved or bullied. On schooling, he repeated a claim he said he'd heard on Joe Rogan and wasn't sure was true: that the Prussians invented kindergarten because soldiers deliberately missed their targets, and separating mothers from children early bred an anxiety that could be turned against enemies. His practical advice was to keep one parent at home if at all possible and delay full-time school.

### Africa, poets and Kanye West

The Nigerian questioner returned to challenge Jiang's earlier lectures: with the world's youngest population, family-first culture, farming knowledge, little dependence on government, and billionaires now building fertilizer plants locally, why assume Africa would suffer in a collapse? Jiang conceded the point and said African people would be resilient if left alone. His concern was colonialism of ideas: Nigeria's elite, he suggested, lives in London, is educated in English-language curricula designed to indoctrinate, and so cannot produce the native, divinely inspired leader around whom great civilizations historically form. He also argued that official history underrates Africa's role, as part of a broader belief that advanced globalized civilizations existed long before recorded history, citing rumors of pyramids in Antarctica and Egyptian structures near the Grand Canyon.

How can poets, whom the questioner considered on the side of truth, serve "they"? Jiang said real poets channel the spirit world, and historically they sang rather than wrote. The ones elevated by the system "are probably not poets," he said, just mouthpieces of the official narrative. His example of a true poet: Kanye West, "who spoke the truth, who was punished for it."

Later he expanded: creativity isn't creation at all. Beauty and truth resonate across people because they come from the shared source, the monad. "You never create, you channel. If you create, you suck." He said he channels too, when he lectures.

## Jiang's theology

Much of the late Q&A turned on religion, and here Jiang laid out his own beliefs more fully than in the lecture proper.

**Is Earth hell?** Most religions treat this world as a place of exile or penance, he said. Judaism is the exception: a "temporal" religion in which humans exist to redeem the world through love, education and social justice, making it ready for God. In Jewish eschatology, unlike most Christian versions, body and spirit are redeemed together. Jiang said he sides with that view. "Unless you act in the belief that you matter and that you can create a better world, there's really no point to your existence." He added that Jews succeed because their religion makes them work hard, argue deeply and look after one another, and that a world like that would be a better one.

**Jesus's three messages.** In Jiang's simplified reading, Jesus taught that God is with us, that God forgives us (so we must forgive ourselves), and that we are all one and the same. Each was revolutionary because it undercut the authority of priests, laws and elites. He argued that every great prophet, Muhammad included, originally taught the same three truths before institutions transformed them.

**Sabbateanism and Frankism.** Jiang said he is researching Sabbatai Zevi, the 17th-century Jewish messianic claimant whom roughly a third of European Jews followed before he converted to Islam in 1666. Jiang believes Zevi's suppressed message was the same as Jesus's: you need neither rabbis nor law to reach God. In his account the movement later split. **Hasidism** took the joyful, egalitarian path for poor Jews; **Frankism**, under Jacob Frank, concluded that if God forgives everything, one should seek wealth and power by any means. He claimed Frankism spread among elite families in Prague and Warsaw, the European nobility, and families such as the Rothschilds.

**The theology of sin.** Why, he asked, does the Bible treat the blameless Moses so harshly, denying him entry to the Promised Land, while David, an adulterer and murderer, becomes God's beloved and Jacob, who cheated his brother, becomes the father of Israel? His answer, which he presented as the worldview of the elite: God, being incapable of sin, relies on humans to sin and then redeem themselves, so "only if you go into darkness can you actually see the light." He cited Goethe's *Faust*, whose hero is saved at the end despite a pact with the devil, and noted Goethe's ties to the Bavarian Illuminati. An audience member put it neatly: sin "lets you break your chains in the cave." "Exactly," Jiang replied, which is why, he said, the doctrine is so attractive to the powerful.

**Free will and fate.** We are all one, he said, but we are also the sum of our choices; heaven is aligning with God's will and hell is moving away from it. Citing the Jewish tradition, he described history as predetermined in its destination (reunion with God) while we choose how fast we get there. He also said the lizard people (a recurring joke in his talks) can only harm you if you sign a contract with them, as Satan in Milton's *Paradise Regained* can only tempt Jesus, not compel him.

**Why billionaires won't die.** Jiang said his elderly wealthy acquaintances cling to life long past meaning, and cancer doctors he met in Montreal told him the richest patients fear death most. He linked this to his warning that aging societies like Canada are "completely, utterly screwed," because the old hold the political power and every crisis will be resolved in their favor; COVID lockdowns, he argued, were the elderly's fear of germs imposed on everyone. Billionaires, he predicted, will pour resources into longevity research and Elysium-style enclaves. "I don't think these people are rational," he said. Peter Thiel's preoccupation with the Antichrist, in his view, is a sign of derangement.

## Why he keeps going

The final question asked how one finds the courage to speak unpopular truths. Jiang said the meetups themselves sustain him: the diversity of the people who come, the 40 to 60 paying subscribers who show up in every city, the strangers who recognize him on the street and say "please keep doing what you're doing."

He described an explosion he can't otherwise explain, from a few hundred YouTube subscribers in his first year to nearly three million in the second. He said he has refused offers to build a large media organization because organizations recreate the very hierarchy he opposes, and that this is what separates him from the apostle Paul, whom he has elsewhere called a double agent for the rabbis and Rome. Paul built a church and asked people to believe in it; Jiang says his message is "believe in yourself."

"I can't explain otherwise why I'd be so popular," he said. "It's because the monad wants me to do something. I'm just going to intuitively figure out what the monad wants me to do and go do it."

## The scorecard

Jiang gave his theory three months. The dates to watch, as he named them: the Alberta vote (October 19), the Israeli election (October 27), the US midterms (early November), the APEC summit in Shenzhen (November), and any major AI-company IPO afterward. By his own standard, those events will tell us whether "the rupture" is a hidden war over who prints the world's money, or just a lot of separate crises happening at once.
