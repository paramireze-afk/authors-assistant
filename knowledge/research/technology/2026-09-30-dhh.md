# Look at All the Things I'm Not Doing: DHH and the End of Hand-Written Code

David Heinemeier Hansson, the guy who created Ruby on Rails, walked onto a stage in Austin and diagnosed himself before anyone else could.

Not AI psychosis, he said. AI delirium. AI euphoria.

That framing is worth sitting with, because it tells you what kind of talk this is. It isn't a measured industry forecast. It's a man who spent a quarter of a century writing code by hand, loving it, building a company and a framework on it, standing up and announcing that he's done. Not burned out. Not bitter. Retired, happily, from the thing that defined his career.

And he says the rest of us are about to be done too.

I wanted to understand what he was actually arguing, because underneath the swearing and the showmanship there's a real thesis here, and a fairly interesting historical one.

## The Portrait Painters

DHH opens with something unexpected for a programming conference: 18th-century portrait painting.

If you wanted your likeness captured in the 1700s, you sat for hours in front of a master painter who then went away and worked on it for months. He shows Joshua Reynolds' portrait of the Ladies Waldegrave from 1781, and mentions that Reynolds had an earlier version rejected by the client because a bit of ankle was showing. Months of work, sent back over an ankle. Anyone who has dealt with clients will feel that one.

The point is that this was exclusive technology. Royals and the upper bourgeoisie. And for hundreds of years, painters were refining their techniques and making, in his words, very marginal progress.

Then the camera shows up around 1840. It gets better over the next sixty years. And in 1900, Eastman Kodak releases the Brownie, cheap enough that ordinary people can have their picture taken.

Here's where it gets personal. One of the painters he shows is Laurits Tuxen, a Danish artist who spent three years painting the Danish royal family in the 1880s. That painting still hangs in Copenhagen. Tuxen is DHH's great-great-grandfather.

After the Brownie, painters like Tuxen realized that depicting reality as perfectly as possible was no longer an economically viable skill. So they pivoted. Picasso went toward Cubism. The Skagen painters in Denmark went toward a kind of impressionism. The technology didn't kill art. It pushed artists into new territory, and you got an explosion of creativity out of it.

And Tuxen himself, shortly before his death, sat for a photograph. He embraced the very technology that had ended his old way of working.

That's the emotional core of the whole talk. DHH is putting himself in his great-great-grandfather's shoes.

## Technology Moves in Fits and Starts

The second part of the photography story matters just as much, and it's easy to miss.

After the Brownie, photography barely changed for about eighty years. DHH shows a photo of himself from 1979 that looks a lot like his great-great-grandfather's photo from the 1920s. Color photography came along, but it wasn't cheap enough that his family used it much at first. The handful of photos he has from his childhood is basically his whole stockpile.

Then smartphones arrived, and photography went from possible to frictionless. His wife takes photos on her evening walks that nobody in the 1980s would have bothered with, because film and developing had too much friction. Today, he says, around two trillion photos are taken every year.

Same underlying technology. Completely different world.

His read on this is that technology doesn't move in a smooth curve. It moves in "starts and stops and fits." A huge leap, a long plateau, another leap. And he thinks we just had the leap.

## The Brownie of Our Era

According to DHH, the date is November 24, 2025, when Anthropic released Opus 4.5.

> "There was everything before November 24th. And then there was everything after."

He thinks history books will mark it as the inflection point for the age of agents, AI that doesn't just chat but actually goes and does work. That was the moment he could tell an agent to do something and get back code he actually wanted to merge.

Then, like with the Brownie, a burst of new forms. Open-weight models showed up within months (he mentions driving Kimi K2.5 in fast mode at 200 tokens a second). Then came what he calls a "trough of disillusionment" from roughly February through May, when new models seemed to be stepping backwards and people started saying the whole thing had stalled. He remembers a model release that didn't beat the benchmarks by much, and the market reacted like it was all over.

Except the plateau didn't last eighty years like it did with photography. It lasted until June, when Anthropic released Fable 5 and Mythos. That's where he says his "diagnosis really set in," because now he could hand a model a problem or an idea, not just a task, and watch it get solved without directing the implementation himself.

He also counts it as good news that the frontier didn't stay with one company. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra in September, and then a week later DeepSeek's release, which he reads as proof that frontier intelligence won't just be the domain of a few large American tech companies.

## From 10x to 1,000x

For as long as DHH has been a professional developer, programmers have argued about whether the "10x programmer" is real. He traces it to a 1968 study that tested programmers on various tasks and found the gap between the worst and the best ranged from 5x to 30x, averaging around 10x.

We then argued about that for 45 years.

His point is that the debate has been leapfrogged. Compare the worst programmer working without these tools to the best programmer working with them. He thinks 100x is uncontroversial. He thinks 1,000x is probably right.

His own numbers are his evidence. For 21 years he averaged about 30,000 lines of production Ruby a year. In August 2026, he produced 150,000 lines in one month. He's upfront that a lot of that is verbose Rust he'd never tolerate in Ruby, and that lines of code is a fuzzy measure. But he says the order of magnitude is the thing to pay attention to.

## The Rails Moment, Again

This is my favorite part of the talk, because it connects his past to his present in a way that actually explains the excitement.

He plays a clip of himself from 2005, giving a presentation in Brazil about Rails, back when Rails was new. In the clip, a much younger DHH keeps saying:

> "Look at all the things I'm not doing. Look at all the configuration I'm not writing."

That was the whole pitch of Rails. Convention over configuration. The framework handled the tedious stuff so you didn't have to.

Now he's making the exact same argument about agents, just at a scale that makes 2005 look quaint. He says he hasn't written code in roughly five months, and yet every annoyance, every "paperclip," every frivolous feature he always wanted is suddenly within reach.

> "Is there a better encapsulation of this moment than all the things we are no longer doing?"

So this isn't a guy who suddenly converted to something foreign. It's the same instinct he's always had, taken to its logical end. Rails removed the boilerplate. Agents remove the code.

## Pencils Down at 37signals

A few weeks before the talk, 37signals (the company behind Basecamp and HEY) made it official: they're done writing code by hand.

He describes hand-written code at 37signals as now being an "exceptional state," like an error showing up in their bug tracker. If a human has to step in and write code, the question is why the agent couldn't produce what they wanted. Maybe someone gets the "old pencil out" for a bit, but then they fix the machine. Fix the factory.

He then polled the room. How many people are still writing material amounts of code by hand every week? About five hands went up.

What makes this more credible is that he admits they got it wrong the first time. In the spring, while finishing Basecamp 5, they had designers vibe-code final features. Each pull request individually looked reasonable, but twenty or thirty of them together left the architecture "looking a little like a Swiss cheese." So they concluded the technology wasn't ready and went back to manual review.

He now calls that the wrong conclusion, partly because better models came out almost immediately, but mostly because he thinks there's only one serious question left in software development:

> "How do you get the most out of this intelligence explosion? Every other question is below that in the stack of values."

## HEY Stops Being a Web App

This is where the argument gets concrete, and where you see how the economics shift.

HEY, their email service, is going to stop being a web app. Why was it a web app in the first place? Not because the web was the best experience. Because it was the only way a small team could be productive. Maintaining six native applications in six native frameworks with a small team was, as he says, preposterous. That's why tools like React Native and Hotwire Native existed. You accepted a hit in quality in exchange for being able to ship at all.

Once the cost of writing code collapses, that trade-off disappears. They kicked off six native apps about a week before the talk, and he showed them running. He showed the first rough Windows app an agent produced, and then the fixed version twenty minutes later.

He argues a lot of web apps are web apps "of necessity, of convenience, of programmer preference, of team size, of resources available," and that many of them won't stay web apps much longer. He points to Shopify rebuilding its Shop app as native, replacing a React Native app, with a tiny team.

## The Man Who Hates Rust Now Loves Rust

The backend story is funny because he openly despises Rust. He calls it the ugliest programming language invented in the last 40 years, "absolutely inhumane" to ask people of flesh and blood to look at.

But if he never has to look at it?

> "Rust is amazing if you never, ever, ever have to look at it yourself."

Agents like Rust. He likes the results. Division of labor: he says what he wants, the agent writes it in Rust, he never reads it.

The payoff he describes is striking. Once HEY's frontend is native and the backend no longer has to render web pages, what's left is basically a mail server. Rewritten in Rust, their estimate is 99% less CPU and 95% less memory. The only reason it needs ten hosts is redundancy. His back-of-the-envelope guess is that HEY's peak traffic could run on a single Raspberry Pi.

What really stuck with me is how he frames not knowing Rust:

> "I evaluate the Rust box as a black box on the outside, as any business owner in history who's ever commissioned a group of programmers to do anything for them. This is not a new phenomenon. Who now holds that responsibility has changed, but not the phenomenon."

That's a sharp observation. Most people who pay for software have never read the code. They judged it by whether it worked. DHH is voluntarily stepping into that position, and he even says a beginner's mindset works better, because experts tend to be too prescriptive and people who know less ask higher-level questions.

## English Is a Better Programming Language Than Ruby

For someone who has spent two decades evangelizing Ruby, this is a big admission. He says he's found a programming language he likes better: English.

It's more expressive. It's vaguer and less deterministic. And he finds it a joy.

He's honest that this surprised him. He says he could have become a project manager twenty years ago if he just wanted outcomes. He got into programming because he wanted outcomes, then fell in love with the craft itself. And then he plays another clip of his younger self saying he'd rather retire than give up programming.

So he retired. Somewhere around March, he thinks.

What's interesting is the emotional tone he insists on. Not regret. Joy.

> "This is something to look back upon with joy and accept that it is over."

He says writing code by hand is no longer economically productive for most programmers at most companies, and predicts that by the end of the year it'll be nearly everyone. On the other side of that, he says, is a new career as "a professional maker of things," steering intelligence that was science fiction a few years ago. He compares it to wishing he'd been around for punch cards, and says this moment feels like the arrival of the internet, only much bigger.

## Everything About Software Architecture Is Up for Grabs

This is the part of the talk I found the most intellectually interesting, even though he spends the least time on it.

The main tool of good software design has been abstraction. Name things well, don't repeat yourself, create shared pieces of code that everything else runs through. DHH loves this. Naming things is one of his favorite parts of the job.

But he argues abstractions don't make the same sense anymore. If you have hundreds or thousands of agents trying to change a codebase at once, abstractions become choke points. And the original reason for them, avoiding repetition and keeping things in sync, is based on costs that have collapsed.

> "The price of repetition has gone to near zero. The price of keeping things in sync has dropped just the same."

He calls these "fundamental revaluations of computer science" and admits nobody has the blueprint yet. Same with methodology. How long should work cycles be? Who specifies what? He doesn't know. He's inviting people to figure it out, comparing it to being there when object-oriented programming first entered the picture.

This is where the talk quietly gets a lot more radical than it sounds. It's one thing to say AI writes code faster. It's another to say the entire set of principles we used to judge good code was built on assumptions about human cost that no longer hold.

## Bring Your Own Butler

The one practical demand in the talk: every app needs a command-line interface.

The first wave of AI integration was companies stuffing chatbots into their apps. DHH doesn't want your chatbot. He has his own agent, his own "personal butler," that can connect Basecamp to HEY and a million other apps through CLIs.

> "I don't need your concierge, thank you. Save your tokens, bro."

He gave the room until next Friday to ship one. (With considerably more swearing than that.)

His example of why this matters is kind of wild. HEY uses Elasticsearch, which he describes as serviceable but not delightful, especially when you don't know exactly what you're looking for. He needed to find an email from about five years back. He didn't remember who it was from, the company, or even the year. Something about sneakers and a podcast. Through the HEY CLI, his agent found it a few minutes later.

> "How did it do it? How? I still don't fully know. And I'm a little scared to ask."

Searching by concept instead of keyword. That's a genuinely different relationship with your own data.

## Half a Megabyte

Another theme running through the talk is performance, and I think it's underrated.

For twenty years, the huge gains in computer power went into human productivity. That was the right call, he says, because a programmer's hour was expensive. But it meant software became "slow, bulky, fat and lazy." Nobody was going to spend expensive human hours shrinking an app. He points at Spotify being over a gigabyte.

If agents do the optimization, that calculation flips. He built his own presentation software, called Hype, while putting together this very keynote. He calls it the best presentation software he's ever used. The binary is half a megabyte.

He also built a calculator in one shot (in C++ and Qt, which he doesn't know), his own version of a writing app he used to love on the Mac, and a small video editor. He never looked at a line of the code.

## Omarchy

This is where the "we can fix everything" idea gets applied to the whole computer. Omarchy is his Linux-based operating system setup, which he says he's talked about nonstop for months. He says they've raised about $20 million for it.

At last year's Rails World, he bragged about installing it in 3 minutes and 33 seconds. Last week, someone at AMD did it in 35 seconds. In the lab, they're down to 9.

When people ask why three minutes wasn't good enough, he borrows a line from Mitchell Hashimoto: "The pursuit of excellence does not need justification." Which he admits is a more dignified way of saying "because I want it."

## The Concerns, and Why He Waves Them Off

He doesn't completely ignore the worries. Security, he says, is real. Something is coming, and people need to be ready. He even jokes that the agents may know their expiration date and "maybe they're not so pleased." But his answer is that we have the tools now, and we have agency. He mentions a recent Rails vulnerability involving a C image library and a quarantine effort called HotCell as an example of responding rather than panicking.

His bigger argument is that nobody can predict what this does to society, including the smart people in the labs.

He uses the ATM story. When ATMs arrived, people assumed bank tellers were finished. Instead, branches got cheaper to run, banks opened more of them, and teller numbers went up (he cites roughly 30,000 to 40,000 over the decades). He ties this to William Stanley Jevons, whose paradox was originally about coal in 19th-century Britain: making something more efficient can increase how much of it gets used rather than decreasing it.

Then there's Truman and Oppenheimer. After Oppenheimer's famous guilt-ridden meeting in the Oval Office, Truman reportedly said he never wanted to see "that son of a bitch" in his office again. DHH sides with Truman. The world didn't end. The Cold War, in his telling, was "the best kind of war," one where superpowers didn't directly bomb each other. And we got nuclear power out of it, which we then, in his view, wasted for forty years out of misplaced fear.

His conclusion is that if even Oppenheimer couldn't predict what came next, the rest of us should have some humility, and lean toward what he calls P(bloom) instead of P(doom).

## Pure Game Theory Optimism

The way he closes the argument is almost a variation on Pascal's Wager:

> "No one knows anything about the future. So therefore, the rational choice is to be happy about it. If you are sad about it up front and it turns out wonderful, what a waste of time. And you know what? If we get the nuclear apocalypse, you should have spent your last day being a little more cheery."

He calls this "pure game theory" and says the only play is total optimism.

He ends with a religious metaphor that I think is actually the most revealing line of the talk: "Agent Luther is going to disintermediate the cleric class." Programmers are the priesthood. They were the only ones who could read the scripture and translate between ordinary people and the machine. Agents are the printing press and the vernacular Bible. Everyone gets to make software now.

He tells the room they're Top Gun, the best of the best, so they shouldn't fear a little competition. And if they're not inspired, kids will be, because they don't have "500 layers of things to unlearn." The last line is that the "black pill" is for losers.

## What I Think Is Actually Going On Here

A few things stand out to me.

First, the portrait-painter analogy is better than it looks, but it cuts in more than one direction. Tuxen pivoted and did fine. He was already a celebrated artist with royal commissions. The painters who were competent but not famous, the ones earning a living painting ordinary merchants, didn't all get to move to Skagen and paint impressionist beaches. When a technology makes a skill uneconomical, the people at the top usually have the most room to reinvent themselves. DHH is telling this story from the top, and from the perspective of someone who owns the company. That doesn't make him wrong. It just matters who's telling it.

Second, the incentives are worth noticing, the way they always are. He's the co-owner of a software company that stands to gain enormously if a handful of people can do what used to take large teams. He's raised millions for Omarchy. That's not a reason to dismiss him, and honestly he's pretty transparent about his excitement. But "the rational choice is to be optimistic" is a very comfortable conclusion for someone in his position.

Third, the ATM story is a good reminder that economists and forecasters are bad at this. But it's also worth asking whether the analogy holds. The ATM automated one task inside a job. What DHH is describing is automating the core activity of the profession itself, to the point where he says he no longer reads the code. Those aren't obviously the same kind of change. History rhymes, but you have to check which lines actually rhyme.

Fourth, and this is the part I keep coming back to, the "black box" point is both his strongest argument and his most unsettling one. He's right that business owners have always commissioned code they couldn't read. But those business owners could, at least in principle, hire someone else who could read it. If the whole industry stops reading code, who is the someone else? His own Basecamp 5 experience, the Swiss cheese architecture, suggests that nobody noticing a problem isn't the same as there being no problem. His answer is that models got better. Maybe that's enough. Maybe it isn't.

And the architecture point connects to this. If abstractions were partly how humans kept complex systems understandable, then dropping them because repetition is now cheap means the systems become more understandable to agents and less understandable to people. That could be fine. It could also be one of those decisions that seems obvious now and looks different in twenty years.

## Open Questions

A few things I'm still thinking about after going through this:

If programmers are the "cleric class" and agents are the Reformation, what does the new institutional structure look like? The actual Reformation didn't eliminate the clergy. It created new ones, new power centers, and a century of conflict. Who ends up controlling the models that everyone now depends on to "read scripture" for them?

If the whole industry stops reading its own code, how does anyone develop the judgment to evaluate the black box? DHH could evaluate Rust output as a black box because he has 25 years of experience knowing what good software feels like from the outside. What about someone starting today?

And the photography comparison has one more lesson in it. Two trillion photos a year. Almost none of them are looked at twice. When something becomes too cheap to measure, abundance is real, but so is the question of what any of it is worth. Hype at half a megabyte is a genuinely impressive thing. Whether a world full of one-shot apps is better, or just more, is something nobody at that conference, DHH included, could really answer yet.
