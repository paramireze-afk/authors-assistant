# Visas to Freedom: The History of HIAS

*Mark Wischnitzer, 1956*

> **Note:** This summarizes the book's narrative and arguments as the author presents them, based on general knowledge of the book and its subject rather than a page-by-page reading. It describes what Wischnitzer claims, not whether those claims hold up.

## About the Author

Mark Wischnitzer (1882–1955) was a Jewish historian born in the Russian Empire who spent much of his career in Berlin, where he worked with Jewish emigration and relief organizations before fleeing the Nazis and eventually settling in the United States. He taught at Yeshiva University and wrote extensively on Jewish social and economic history, including a major study of Jewish migration. He died shortly before *Visas to Freedom* appeared, so the book was in some sense his final statement on a subject he had lived as well as studied.

That background matters: Wischnitzer was not a detached outsider. He writes as someone who had been inside the world of Jewish emigration work, and the book reads accordingly — informed, detailed, and openly admiring of its subject.

## The Book's Basic Framing

The subtitle promises "seven decades of rescue work," and that framing runs through the whole book. Wischnitzer presents HIAS not as a bureaucracy but as a lifeline: an organization that stood between persecuted Jews and the closing doors of the world. His central claim is that HIAS embodied a Jewish tradition of communal responsibility, the idea that Jews already established in America owed it to newcomers to receive them, protect them, and help them stand on their own feet.

The title itself carries the argument. For the people in this story, a visa is not paperwork; it is the difference between life and death, especially in the later chapters.

## Origins: The Great Migration (1880s–1900s)

Wischnitzer begins with the wave of Jewish emigration from the Russian Empire that followed the pogroms of 1881–82 and the restrictive laws that came after them. Hundreds of thousands, and eventually millions, of Jews headed west, most of them to the United States.

He describes how newcomers arriving in New York often had nowhere to go, no English, and no one to meet them. Small grassroots groups formed to provide shelter, meals, and burial for those who died without family. The book traces how these efforts, including a Hebrew sheltering society and a separate immigrant aid society, eventually merged in the early twentieth century into the organization that became known as HIAS.

A recurring point in these chapters is that HIAS grew from the immigrant community itself rather than from the wealthier, already-assimilated German-Jewish establishment. Wischnitzer frames this as a strength: HIAS understood the newcomers because its founders had been newcomers.

## Ellis Island and the Fight Against Deportation

Some of the book's most vivid material concerns HIAS's work at Ellis Island. According to Wischnitzer, HIAS representatives:

- met arriving ships and helped immigrants through inspection,
- translated for people facing officials they couldn't understand,
- intervened when immigrants were detained or ordered deported, often on grounds such as health or the risk of becoming a "public charge,"
- arranged appeals and posted bonds to keep families from being sent back,
- located relatives already in the country, and
- provided practical help such as kosher food for detainees.

The author presents these interventions as saving large numbers of people from being returned to the dangers they had fled. He portrays HIAS as a persistent advocate that learned to work the immigration system on behalf of its clients.

## Political Battles Over Immigration Law

Wischnitzer devotes attention to HIAS's opposition to the growing restrictionist movement in American politics. He covers the long campaign over literacy tests and then the quota laws of the early 1920s, which sharply cut immigration from Eastern and Southern Europe.

His framing is clear: he treats restriction as a betrayal of America's tradition as a haven and as a disaster for Jews in particular, since it shut the door just as conditions in Eastern Europe were worsening. He argues that immigrants were assets to the country, not burdens, and presents HIAS's lobbying and public advocacy as a defense of both Jewish interests and American ideals.

## World War I and Its Aftermath

During and after the First World War, the book describes HIAS expanding its role beyond the American port. Wischnitzer highlights efforts to:

- reconnect families separated by war and shifting borders,
- transmit money from American relatives to people stranded abroad,
- help refugees who had fled east across Russia toward the Pacific, with HIAS operating in East Asia to move them onward.

This is where, in his telling, HIAS began transforming from a local aid society into an international agency.

## Going Global: HICEM

A major institutional thread is the creation in the late 1920s of HICEM, a joint body combining HIAS with European Jewish emigration organizations. Wischnitzer, who had personal experience in this world, presents HICEM as a crucial step: a network of offices across Europe and beyond that could advise emigrants, handle documents, and steer people toward whatever countries would still accept them.

With the United States largely closed by quotas, he emphasizes that this work increasingly meant finding alternative destinations, especially in Latin America and elsewhere.

## The Nazi Era: Rescue Against Closing Doors

The emotional center of the book is the 1930s and the Second World War. Wischnitzer narrates HICEM and HIAS's efforts to get Jews out of Germany, Austria, and then occupied Europe. He describes:

- the growing desperation as country after country refused refugees,
- escape routes through France, Spain, and Portugal, with Lisbon serving as a key exit point,
- refugees reaching places such as Shanghai, one of the few destinations that didn't require a visa,
- the scramble for visas, transit permits, and ship passage, often secured at the last possible moment.

His argument here is twofold. First, that these organizations rescued many people under nearly impossible conditions. Second, implicitly and sometimes explicitly, that far more could have been saved had the democracies, including the United States, opened their doors. The book's portrayal of bureaucratic barriers is pointed: the tragedy, in his account, was not only Nazi persecution but the world's unwillingness to take in its victims.

## After the War: Displaced Persons and Israel

The later chapters cover the postwar period, when survivors languished in displaced persons camps in Europe. Wischnitzer describes HIAS's role in helping them resettle, including work under the American legislation that eventually admitted displaced persons, and migration to the new State of Israel and to other countries.

He presents this as a continuation of the same mission, adapted to new circumstances.

## The 1954 Merger

The book ends around 1954, when HIAS merged with other Jewish migration services into a combined organization. Wischnitzer treats this as the culmination of decades of growth, positioning the new body to carry the work forward.

## Major Themes and Claims

Pulled together, the book's main arguments are:

1. **Communal responsibility.** HIAS expressed a Jewish obligation of mutual aid, with established immigrants caring for new ones.
2. **Grassroots origins.** The organization grew out of the immigrant community itself, which made it effective and trusted.
3. **Advocacy works.** Persistent legal and political intervention kept many families together and in the country.
4. **Restriction was a moral failure.** Immigration quotas, and the broader refusal of nations to accept refugees, had devastating consequences, above all in the Holocaust era.
5. **Immigrants as contributors.** Jewish immigrants enriched America rather than burdening it.
6. **Continuity of mission.** From Ellis Island to Lisbon to the DP camps, HIAS's work was one continuous story of rescue.

## Reading the Book Today

*Visas to Freedom* is best read as an insider's institutional history: written by someone sympathetic to the organization, focused on its achievements, and shaped by the fresh trauma of the Holocaust. It tells the story HIAS would tell about itself, and it is valuable precisely for that perspective — as a record of how Jewish rescue organizations understood their own mission in the mid-twentieth century.
