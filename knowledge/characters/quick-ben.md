# Quick Ben: Power, Secrecy, and the Art of Having Another Plan

## Spoiler Warning

This revision assumes you have read all ten novels of Steven Erikson's *Malazan Book of the Fallen*. It discusses major events, deaths, identities, and endgame developments openly, including the fate of Whiskeyjack, the destruction of the Bridgeburners, the true identities of Shadowthrone and Cotillion, Kalam's near-death in Seven Cities, and the shape (though not every mechanic) of Quick Ben's role in the series' final convergence. If you haven't finished the series, this is not the place to start.

## Introduction: Absolutely Just a Normal Squad Mage

Quick Ben spends a genuinely startling amount of the series insisting he is a modest, replaceable squad mage doing his humble best. This is the largest and most successful lie in a series built almost entirely out of lies, misdirection, and withheld information, and by the time readers have finished all ten books, they know exactly how false it was from the opening pages of *Gardens of the Moon*.

He is not modest, and he was never simply a squad mage in the way the term implies for anyone else wearing it. What makes him such a durable character isn't only that he turns out to be powerful — Malazan has no shortage of powerful people — but that Erikson refuses to let his two defining traits, immense capability and constant concealment, resolve into anything tidy. Ten books later, readers know a great deal more about what Quick Ben has done than about what Quick Ben actually *is*, and that gap is not an oversight. It's the character.

## Ben Adaephon Delat: Raraku and the Souls He Carries

**Established in the text:** Quick Ben's birth name is Ben Adaephon Delat. He was, as a young man, one of a cabal of twelve mages serving a Holy Falah'd in a Seven Cities city. When Dassem Ultor's Malazan army took the city and killed the Falah'd, the cabal fled into the Pan'potsun Wastes and on into the Holy Desert of Raraku — pursued not by rival mages but by a Malazan military company under a young officer named Whiskeyjack, guided by a local Seven Cities man named Kalam Mekhar. This pursuit is, in fact, the founding trauma of the Bridgeburners themselves: the desert campaign that forged Whiskeyjack's ordinary soldiers into the unit that would carry that name. One by one, over weeks of chase, eleven of the twelve mages were found dead. Quick Ben was the last one standing when Whiskeyjack's company finally caught up with him — and Kalam, it turned out, had been in secret contact with him throughout, working, in whatever quiet way he could, against the very hunt he was guiding.

**What the novels indicate, and what remains a character's inference rather than confirmed narration:** Whiskeyjack himself is the one who works out what likely happened — that each of the dying mages, facing death, shifted their soul and power into Quick Ben rather than let it go to waste. This is offered in the text as Whiskeyjack's own reasoned guess, not as omniscient narrative fact, which is a distinction worth preserving: it's the best available explanation, voiced by a man piecing together evidence after the fact, not a settled account confirmed by the story itself. On that basis, "twelve" is a number the novels do support — the cabal numbered twelve, including Quick Ben, and he is generally understood to carry the souls of the eleven who didn't survive alongside his own.

What the books do not support is turning that into a tidy roster of twelve complete, independently functioning people sharing space behind one man's eyes. Whether those eleven other souls persist as anything like separate identities, whether they contribute specific memories or only raw capability, and how much of "Quick Ben" as a personality is original to Ben Adaephon Delat rather than assembled from what he absorbed — none of this is settled. What's established is the headline fact and its cost: he survived something in that desert that killed everyone else who tried, came out carrying far more than himself, and has spent the rest of his life declining to explain exactly what that means, even to the one man who guided the hunt that made it happen.

## The Bridgeburner

A few terms, for readers who want the shorthand refreshed: **the Bridgeburners** are the Malazan Empire's elite, chronically under-thanked corps of soldiers, sappers, and squad mages — survivors of campaigns that killed most of the units fielded alongside them. Their name is traditionally associated with Raraku having burned the bridges to their former lives. A **warren** is the series' term for a source or path of magic, one of several distinct realms of power each with its own character and risks. **High Mage** is a formal Malazan military rank recognizing exceptional magical skill and command responsibility — Tayschrenn, the Empire's Imperial High Mage, holds the same title in its most senior form — and it is a professional designation, not a rung on a ladder that leads to godhood. **Ascendancy**, by contrast, is not a promotion at all. It's a different metaphysical condition entirely: a being who has, through apotheosis, survival of the impossible, or claim by one of the setting's Houses and Holds, stepped outside ordinary mortal limits altogether, sometimes becoming an object of worship or a figure on the cosmic structure known as the Deck of Dragons. A mortal, however extraordinary, is not an Ascendant just for being extremely good at magic — and the novels are notably careful never to formally classify Quick Ben as one. He operates near that threshold for most of the series. Whether he ever actually crosses it is left, appropriately, unresolved.

Quick Ben serves as squad mage attached to Whiskeyjack's Ninth through the unit's worst campaigns — the Darujhistan mission and the later war against the Pannion Domin that ends at Coral — and comes to be recognized, particularly by the time he's serving as ranking mage to Adjunct Tavore's Bonehunters later in the series, at the level the Malazan military designates High Mage. His influence reaches into the Seven Cities rebellion through plans, contacts, and his partnership with Kalam, but for most of that campaign — the Chain of Dogs, Kalam's own run across the continent — Quick Ben is not physically present; he's occupied elsewhere with the Genabackis theater. The novels are less interested in pinning down exactly when his formal recognition as High Mage arrived than in showing him operating, from very early on, well past what the title would normally imply.

His loyalty to Whiskeyjack is one of the most stable facts about him in a character built almost entirely from instability elsewhere. That loyalty meets its limit at Coral, in *Memories of Ice*, where Whiskeyjack is killed by the ancient, malevolent Ascendant Kallor — an old, never-fully-healed leg wound costing him the half-step he'd have needed to avoid the blow. Quick Ben is not there for the killing blow itself; whatever preparation, contingency, and improvisation he'd built up over the campaign simply hadn't put him in that place at that moment, and there is nothing afterward he can do to reverse it. His account of it to the survivors, delivered later at Whiskeyjack's tomb, is strikingly flat and clinical: it was the leg, he says, that killed him. That controlled, almost procedural register reads, in context, like grief that has nowhere else left to go. Coral stands as the plainest demonstration in the whole series that Quick Ben's power, however vast, has a hard edge to it — not because one climactic spell failed under pressure, but because no amount of preparation, secrecy, or quick improvisation guarantees being in the right place, or having any answer at all, for a loss like this one.

## Quick Ben and Kalam

Kalam Mekhar, former Claw turned Bridgeburner, is Quick Ben's closest friend, and their history runs deeper than the Bridgeburners themselves. Before the Malazan conquest, Kalam served the same Holy Falah'd as Quick Ben — as the ruler's personal assassin, while Quick Ben stood among his cabal of mages — which makes the friendship older than the unit that would eventually give it a name. When Dassem Ultor's army came through and the mage cabal fled into Raraku, Kalam was the local guide Whiskeyjack's company used to track them down — and, unknown to the men he was leading, he spent that pursuit in secret contact with Quick Ben, working against the hunt from the inside. That the truth came out only once Quick Ben was the last mage standing, and that both men chose afterward to throw in with Whiskeyjack rather than run, recasts everything that follows: their partnership didn't start with the Bridgeburners. It survived becoming public inside them.

Their methods sit in deliberate contrast. Kalam solves problems directly: knives, timing, and a professional's discipline, with deception treated as a tool of the trade rather than a personality. Quick Ben solves the same problems sideways, with a talkative evasiveness that treats a straight answer as a last resort. And yet Kalam is one of the very few people who responds to Quick Ben's secrecy not with the suspicion it earns from almost everyone else, but with weary, specific familiarity — the reaction of a man who has had years of practice learning which of Quick Ben's tells mean "I'm about to explain something important" and which mean "I am never explaining this."

During *Deadhouse Gates*, Quick Ben remains behind on Genabackis while Kalam crosses Seven Cities and eventually confronts the Claw near Malaz City, and the separation does more to reveal the friendship than any scene of them side by side. Aboard the *Ragstopper*, Kalam's fellow passenger "Salk Elan" turns out to be Pearl, a Claw agent who has spent the voyage befriending him under a false name — and who, on arrival, paralyzes Kalam with sorcery, stabs him, and throws him overboard into a gauntlet of waiting Claw assassins. Kalam survives, badly hurt, and later recalls the betrayal to Quick Ben in terms that make clear how much it cost him — Quick Ben's response is to say, flatly, that he wants Pearl dealt with. Quick Ben himself is elsewhere for all of this, unable to help, and the anxiety that surfaces around Kalam's fate afterward is one of the clearest windows the reader gets into what he actually feels underneath the constant deflection. It's not staged as a big speech. It doesn't need to be. What the separation shows, more than either man being especially fragile without the other, is how much of their effectiveness depends on the pairing itself — each of them, alone, is formidable and a little bit diminished, missing the one person who reliably reads their methods correctly.

What holds the friendship together, across a series that eventually scatters both men through Seven Cities, the Bonehunters' campaign, and the war's final convergence, is that Kalam is one of the only people who consistently treats Quick Ben as a person rather than a resource. Commanders want to know what he can do so they can plan around it. Gods want to know what he can do so they can use or neutralize him. Kalam mostly just wants to know if he's actually okay, which is a much rarer question in Quick Ben's life than it should be, and the friendship stays funny — furious accusations of withheld information, exasperated sighing, banter with real teeth in it — without ever losing the grief and fear sitting underneath.

## Secrecy as a Way of Life

Secrecy isn't an occasional tactic for Quick Ben; it's closer to a native language, and it does several jobs simultaneously.

- **Protection from enemies**, mortal and otherwise, who cannot plan around a capability they can't accurately measure.
- **Protection from allies and commanders**, who would happily build strategies, obligations, and standing expectations around him the moment his ceiling became known — the Adjunct Tavore's careful, guarded trust in him during the Bonehunters campaign reads as much like managing an unpredictable asset as commanding a subordinate, which is exactly the dynamic Quick Ben's concealment is built to avoid.
- **Resistance to being conscripted by gods**, several of whom — Shadowthrone chief among them — spend the series trying to fold Quick Ben into their long games, with mixed success.
- **Tactical surprise**, the plain, simple advantage of being underestimated at the exact moment it matters most.
- **Control over his own identity**, given the genuine, unresolved possibility that he is internally plural; concealment may not be purely strategic so much as one of the few consistent structures holding an unusual self together.
- **A defense against intimacy** — it is difficult to be fully known by someone who won't disclose where he grew up in detail, what happened to him in Raraku, or what he did last week involving an Elder God.
- **A habit he can no longer fully turn off**, evident in the way he deflects Kalam even when disclosure would clearly cost him nothing and help someone he loves.

The idea worth sitting with is the one the report opened with: once people know exactly what you can do, they begin building expectations, obligations, strategies, and cages around that knowledge. Quick Ben understands this early and never unlearns it. Being fully known, in his experience, tends to precede being claimed — by an empire, by a god's agenda, by a commander's contingency plans — and withholding himself is, among other things, a way of refusing that claim before it can be made.

Whether that refusal is freedom or its own kind of prison is a fair question the novels don't answer for you. There's a strong case that it's freedom: the one part of himself no army, empire, or god can fully lay claim to, precisely because none of them can prove it exists. There's an equally strong case that a man who has spent decades treating disclosure as danger has built himself a very effective cage, one he happens to be standing inside of even with the people who've earned better. Both readings survive contact with the text. Neither cancels the other out.

## The Art of Understatement

Quick Ben's preferred register for anything alarming is aggressive calm. A city on the edge of magical catastrophe is "a bit of a situation." A confrontation with something that could unmake him without particular effort is "just talking." This isn't only bravado — panic is contagious, and a squad takes its emotional temperature from the people it trusts most, so understatement buys him room to work without an audience spiraling. It's also another layer of the same concealment: a crisis described calmly is far harder for a listener to size up accurately than one described honestly, which is, of course, the point.

It also produces a rhythm experienced Malazan readers learn to recognize with genuine, affectionate dread. Quick Ben discovers a problem well past the ordinary definition of dangerous. He insists it's manageable. Under pressure, he reveals he's been sitting on some crucial piece of information the entire time. His allies, reasonably, are furious. He produces another hidden resource nobody knew he had. The situation gets substantially worse before it gets better. He survives it through means most of the people watching didn't fully follow. And when asked to explain, he declines. This is not a joke imposed on the character from outside — it's close to the character's actual operating rhythm, repeated with enough consistency across the series that fans could set a watch by it.

## Quick Ben and the Gods

Quick Ben's habit of walking into conversations with gods, Elder Gods, and Ascendants — beings whose displeasure has historically ended civilizations — and treating those conversations as negotiations rather than audiences, is one of the series' recurring pleasures, and it's rooted in something more specific than nerve.

His history with **Shadowthrone and Cotillion** is real and personal, though the novels are careful about exactly how far it goes. Both are the ascended forms of Emperor Kellanved and his companion Dancer, transformed into gods of the Shadow Realm after their presumed deaths at the founding of the Malazan Empire. Quick Ben, before he was a Bridgeburner in anything but name, was himself a practitioner — by his own account, once a fairly senior one — on the Path of Shadow, involved in Kellanved's purge of a rival cult. It is enough to say the relationship is old and complicated, involving at least one occasion Cotillion still holds against him, without pinning down a fuller personal history the text itself leaves cloudy.

The clearest illustration of how he actually handles that kind of power comes in *Gardens of the Moon*, when Quick Ben deliberately walks into the Warren of Shadow, greets Shadowthrone's Hounds by name, and lets them herd him straight to an audience with the god. There, he identifies himself as a former acolyte to prove he knows Shadow's secrets — a claim that, if false, would likely have gotten him killed on the spot — and uses that leverage to strike a bargain: he'll hand over the location of a dangerous rogue sorcerer in exchange for Shadowthrone lifting the standing death sentence placed on anyone who leaves the Path of Shadow. Shadowthrone agrees, gets what he wants, and only afterward realizes exactly which "former acolyte" he's been dealing with. It's a small, near-perfect model of the character: walk into the den of something that could unmake you, arrive already holding the one card that makes killing you a worse option than talking, and leave having gotten exactly what you came for.

None of this should read as fearlessness. Quick Ben is frequently, visibly terrified underneath the composure, and that fear is part of what makes the negotiating trick work rather than undercutting it — he understands exactly how outmatched he is in raw terms, which is precisely why he never tries to win on raw terms. Gods in this series find him useful when his unpredictability serves their purposes, irritating when it doesn't, and consistently too costly, or too entertaining, to simply remove. That's a narrower, more survivable position than "feared by gods," and it's the one the text actually supports.

## Does Quick Ben Actually Have a Plan?

The series seems to enjoy this question more than it enjoys answering it, and the honest answer distinguishes between several things the report has, until now, been treating as one:

- **Long-term preparation** — resources, allies, and knowledge laid down years in advance of a specific crisis, sometimes traceable all the way back to whatever he built out of Raraku.
- **Tactical improvisation** — adjustment on the fly when the long-term preparation, inevitably, does not cover the actual situation in front of him.
- **Bluffing** — persuading an opponent, sometimes a genuinely cosmic one, that he holds more cards than he does, or that the cards he holds are more dangerous than they are.
- **Genuine foresight** — the rarer cases where he appears to have correctly anticipated a specific outcome well ahead of time, rather than simply having prepared broadly for trouble.
- **Surviving the collapse of an earlier plan** — which is, across the series, at least as common as any of the above, and is often mistaken by other characters, understandably, for the plan having worked as intended.

Whiskeyjack's death at Coral is the clearest evidence that this list has a limit. It isn't that Quick Ben tried some climactic rescue spell and failed — he wasn't there for the moment itself. The failure is quieter and, in a way, worse: none of his preparation, his contingencies, or his gift for improvising past disaster put him where he needed to be, and nothing available to him afterward could undo what had already happened. The series does not let its most resourceful character win every hand, and that failure is doing real work: it's the counterweight that keeps his competence from curdling into invincibility, and it's part of why the "does he have a plan" question stays interesting instead of becoming a running gag with no teeth. Most of the time, the honest answer is that Quick Ben has several partial plans, a genuinely startling amount of prepared leverage, and an unusually fast capacity to improvise new arrangements out of whatever's still standing when the original approach fails — which, in his line of work, it regularly does. Occasionally, the honest answer is that none of it was enough, and there was nothing left to improvise with.

## Power Without Submission

Quick Ben is enormously capable and shows almost no interest in a throne, a title, a cult, or any form of visible dominion — a genuinely unusual restraint in a setting that includes plenty of ascendant and near-ascendant figures who want exactly those things. What he seems to want instead is options: independence from being owned by an empire's chain of command, leverage he can spend when it matters, the ability to protect the small number of people he's actually loyal to, freedom from being conscripted into a god's longer game, and the standing ability to remain unpredictable, because predictability is the thing that gets people like him used up.

This deserves the less flattering half of the picture too. Quick Ben can be genuinely arrogant, confident to a fault that his read on a situation is the correct one. He is manipulative not only toward enemies and gods but sometimes toward his own allies, withholding information when honesty would plainly serve them better, because withholding is simply his default setting rather than a choice weighed fresh each time. And he is willing, more than once, to make significant decisions on other people's behalf without their consent — deciding what Kalam, or Whiskeyjack, or an entire squad needs to know and when, as though their consent were a detail he's entitled to manage along with everything else. The report's argument about secrecy as freedom is stronger, not weaker, for admitting this: the same instinct that protects him from being owned by gods and empires is also, sometimes, a way of quietly claiming ownership over other people's right to their own information. That's not a contradiction the character resolves. It's one he lives inside.

## The Intelligence of Quick Ben

Quick Ben is not formidable because he knows everything — he is formidable because he is unusually good at operating without knowing everything, which is a different and more interesting skill. His intelligence, as the novels actually display it, is made of specific, learnable habits rather than raw brilliance alone: he assumes his information is incomplete and plans around the gap rather than pretending it isn't there; he prepares multiple contingencies rather than committing early to one; he studies the people and beings around him for motive and leverage rather than simply their strength; he recognizes, quickly, when raw power won't solve a given problem and looks instead for the constraint, rivalry, or appetite he can work with; and he is willing to retreat, bluff, bargain, or completely discard a plan the instant the situation changes underneath it.

This reads as more compelling, not less, for how often the series shows him wrong. He is surprised, outmatched, and frightened at multiple points across the ten books — Coral is the sharpest example, but later in the series, defending the First Throne alongside Trull Sengar's company against an enraged Icarium, Quick Ben is simply overwhelmed, beaten back and badly hurt, saved only because something else intervened before Icarium finished the job. A character whose plans never failed would be a power fantasy. A character whose plans regularly fail, who is regularly afraid, and who is regularly still standing at the end anyway because he adapts faster than the failure can catch up with him, is a much better argument for what intelligence actually looks like under pressure.

## Identity and the Souls Within Him

This is the report's most speculative territory, and it's worth stating plainly that the novels leave it that way on purpose.

Is "Quick Ben" a single personality carrying passengers, a coalition of several, something closer to integration, or a condition the text deliberately declines to classify at all? The novels never say. Do the eleven other souls bound to him in Raraku remain distinct, with anything like continuing awareness or agency, or have they been absorbed into something that only functions as Quick Ben now? Also unstated — and worth remembering that even the "eleven souls" framing comes from Whiskeyjack's own inference rather than confirmed narration, which leaves room for the truth to be stranger still. Does his unusual range of warren-access come directly from what those souls knew, or from something more fundamentally changed in him by the event itself? The text supports either reading without confirming one. Does he experience himself as singular — as one continuous "I" — or is some of his notorious evasiveness less strategy than simple difficulty describing a condition ordinary language was never built to hold? This is speculation, clearly labeled as such, but it's a reasonable speculation: a man who cannot fully explain what happened to him in the desert is not necessarily lying when he changes the subject.

Whether this insight comes from the souls he carries, from his own experience, or from both, Quick Ben has an unusually wide, practical understanding of other people's fear, desperation, and self-interest — useful in negotiation, useful in reading a room full of Ascendants — and that same difficulty being fully known makes him, by any reasonable measure, harder to read than almost anyone else in the series, including possibly to himself.

## Loyalty Beneath the Deception

For a character built out of misdirection, Quick Ben's loyalty is remarkably durable, and it survives contact with everything else the report has said about him. He lies constantly, but the lies point, with real consistency, toward protecting specific people rather than serving himself: Whiskeyjack, Kalam, the surviving Bridgeburners, and later the officers and soldiers of the Bonehunters he comes to respect. He absorbs risk that isn't his to take. He shows up — at Coral, in the defense of the First Throne, into the war's final convergence — at serious personal cost, for people he would have a much easier time simply leaving behind.

This is the character's central, load-bearing contradiction, and it's worth stating without trying to resolve it: a manipulator capable of real loyalty; a habitual liar who lies most often to shield others rather than himself; a man who repeatedly risks a survival instinct this finely tuned for the sake of people he could, by any coldly rational accounting, abandon; a figure of enormous power who treats power itself, including his own, with open and consistent suspicion. None of these traits cancel out the others. They sit next to each other, uncomfortably, which is a large part of why he reads as a person rather than a type.

That loyalty carries all the way to the series' final movement. By the time Tavore's Bonehunters march on the Crippled God's chains in the campaign that closes the saga, Quick Ben has stopped pretending to be a modest squad mage in any way that fools anyone still paying attention — he is, by then, openly one of the few mortals in the story capable of standing in the path of forces like the Forkrul Assail and doing more than dying quickly, and he spends that capability in service of Tavore's cause and Kalam's safety rather than any agenda of his own. True to form, the books never fully cash out what he actually is by the end; the scale of what he does gets confirmed even as the mechanism behind it stays, characteristically, his business alone.

## The Cost of Never Being Fully Known

It's worth naming plainly what a life built this thoroughly around concealment might cost, since the text gestures at it more than it states it outright. There's the possible fragmentation that comes with carrying more than one history, whatever its exact nature — the uncomfortable possibility that "Quick Ben" is a working arrangement rather than a simple, unified self. There's the plain loneliness of never being fully known, even by Kalam, even after decades. There's the exhausting habit of treating nearly every relationship as at least partly a strategic problem, difficult to fully switch off even with people who've earned better — visible in the way he decides, unilaterally, what others need to know. There's the likelihood that constant anticipation of betrayal, however professionally useful, makes trust itself harder to access even when it would be safe to extend it. And there's the quieter possibility, never stated outright by the text but hard to avoid noticing: that survival has become less a means to an end for Quick Ben than an identity of its own, and that "the man who gets out alive" may have crowded out gentler versions of who he could have been.

Whether all of this amounts to freedom or its own kind of prison is the question this report keeps circling rather than answering, because the novels themselves decline to answer it. Both are true, probably, depending on the day and the god currently trying to recruit him.

## Why He Is Not a Conventional Fantasy Wizard

Fantasy has no shortage of wise, distant sages, dispensing counsel from towers and standing outside the grubby realities of ordinary military life. Quick Ben is not that. He belongs to a squad, eats the same bad food, gets exhausted on the same long marches, gets genuinely frightened when the danger is real, gets irritated with his friends and commanders, gets injured, gets cornered, and has to talk, fight, or improvise his way out of trouble like everyone else around him, just with a far stranger toolkit. His magic doesn't lift him above the story's human stakes — if anything it drags more of those stakes toward him, because being the most capable person in a desperate situation tends to mean everyone else is depending on you, and that dependence is its own weight. He is not a wizard who occasionally visits the war. He is a soldier who happens to be terrifyingly good at magic, and the series never lets that ordering reverse.

## Why Readers Love Quick Ben

Part of the appeal is structural: Quick Ben starts as, ostensibly, a minor squad mage, and the slow accumulation of evidence that he's anything but is one of the series' most satisfying long games, earned scene by scene rather than delivered in a single reveal. But that can't be the whole explanation, because he stays compelling long after the surprise has worn off — after readers already know he's dangerous, that he's hiding something, and that he'll likely survive whatever's coming.

What holds up after the twist stops being a twist is the person underneath it: the humor of watching someone this capable still get visibly rattled by something ancient and irritable; a friendship with Kalam that only deepens on reread, once you know what it costs both men to keep it; the tension between loyalty and habitual concealment that never fully resolves; the failure at Coral that keeps the whole character honest; and the persistent, deliberately unanswered question of what Raraku, the warrens, and decades of instinctive secrecy have actually cost him. Quick Ben rewards attention less as a mystery box to be solved and more, once you stop trying to solve him, as a genuinely well-drawn person: funny, guarded, loyal, exhausted, and permanently doing more than anyone around him realizes.

## Quick Ben in One Completely Unreliable Summary

*(Playful, not canon.)* Quick Ben is a perfectly average Bridgeburner squad mage who has never once concealed anything of real importance from his closest friends, definitely did not absorb the souls of eleven fellow mages who died fleeing across a desert with him, maintains an excellent, entirely-not-tenuous working relationship with a couple of former emperors currently operating as gods of shadow, and would very much appreciate it if everyone stopped bringing up that business with the Rope, since it was a one-time misunderstanding and he had a plan for it the entire time. Probably.

## Conclusion

Quick Ben works because Erikson refuses to let his two most obvious traits — vast, strange power and constant concealment — settle into anything simple. He isn't powerful because he's a Chosen One; he's powerful because of something unresolved and genuinely costly that happened to him in a desert, something the text itself declines to fully explain. He isn't secretive for flavor; he's secretive because he understands, earlier and more thoroughly than almost anyone else in the series, what happens to people once their limits become known and their power becomes something other people feel entitled to draw on. He is loyal in a world that has given him excellent reasons not to be, and he fails, at Coral, in the one moment loyalty demanded more than even he could produce. He is funny in a way that never once undercuts what's actually at stake. And after ten books that tell you an enormous amount about what he's done, he remains someone you don't fully know — which, for a man built the way he was, in the desert, out of pieces of other people, may be less an authorial withholding than the truest ending he could possibly have gotten.
