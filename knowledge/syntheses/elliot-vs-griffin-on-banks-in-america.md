# The First and Second Banks of the United States

## Why This Page Exists

This episode appears in three other documents in this knowledge base — *[[The Federal Reserve System]]*, *[[The Bank of England]]*, and *[[Usury]]* (Elliott) — but has never had a dedicated page of its own. It deserves one: it is the **missing link** in the chain of institutions this knowledge base traces from the Bank of Venice through the Bank of England to the Federal Reserve, and it is the episode where two authors writing 96 years apart, from completely different frameworks — Griffin's secular fiat-money critique and Elliott's religious/moral usury critique — converge most closely, even quoting some of the same historical figures.

The First and Second Banks were, in Griffin's numbering, America's **second and third central banks** (the Bank of North America, 1781–1783, being the first; the Federal Reserve being the fourth). Both were chartered for twenty years, both provoked essentially the same fight (sound money vs. debt-financed government, popular control vs. concentrated private financial power), and both ultimately lost their charters — the only two central banks in American history to actually be shut down rather than merely reformed.

## The First Bank of the United States (1791–1811)

### Background: the Bank of North America

Before either "Bank of the United States" existed, there was the **Bank of North America**, chartered by the Continental Congress in 1781 — while the Revolutionary War was still being fought, before the Constitution existed. It was organized by **Robert Morris**, a wealthy Philadelphia merchant and member of Congress, and modeled closely on the Bank of England (see *[[The Bank of England]]*): a fractional-reserve institution granted a note-issuing monopoly, made the federal government's depository, and used almost immediately to lend the fledgling government $1.2 million, "much of which was created out of nothing for that purpose" (Griffin). Morris could not raise the required private subscription capital, so — in what Griffin calls "legalized embezzlement" — he used gold France had loaned the United States as the Bank's reserve base and then created money against it to lend to himself and his associates. The Bank lost public confidence, Morris's political power waned, and by the end of 1783 it had been converted into an ordinary Pennsylvania state-chartered commercial bank. **[Note:]** Griffin treats this as America's *first* central bank; because it predates the Constitution and was short-lived, this document treats it as essential background rather than one of the "two banks" in its title, following the more common usage (including Elliott's) that counts the "First" and "Second" Banks as the 1791 and 1816 institutions specifically.

### Founding and the Hamilton–Jefferson Fight

With the Constitution now in force and explicitly denying Congress the power to print money, **Alexander Hamilton** — Treasury Secretary, and a former aide to Robert Morris — proposed in 1790 what Griffin calls "an end run around the Constitution": Congress couldn't print money directly, but nothing stopped it from chartering a bank that could create money and lend it to the government. The bank's notes, not the government's, would circulate as currency.

This produced one of the founding era's defining ideological clashes, and the birth of America's first political parties (Federalists around Hamilton; the Jeffersonian Republicans, forerunners of the modern Democratic Party, around Jefferson):

> **JEFFERSON:** "A private central bank issuing the public currency is a greater menace to the liberties of the people than a standing army." · "We must not let our rulers load us with perpetual debt."

> **HAMILTON:** "No society could succeed which did not unite the interest and credit of rich individuals with those of the state." · "A national debt, if it is not excessive, will be to us a national blessing."

Jefferson's constitutional objection was straightforward: nothing in the enumerated powers authorized Congress to charter a bank, so the power was reserved to the states or the people. Hamilton countered that the power was "implied," necessary to carry out other explicitly granted powers — a doctrine of implied powers that would become one of the most consequential precedents in American constitutional law. Hamilton won; in 1791, Congress granted a twenty-year charter.

### Structure and the Rothschild Question

The new bank was, per Griffin, "modelled closely after the Bank of England" and "almost an exact replica" of the old Bank of North America — it even shared a president, **Thomas Willing**, with its defunct predecessor. As with the earlier bank, the government's supposed 20% "investment" was, per Jefferson, really just "an outright gift" — a bookkeeping sleight-of-hand in which the Bank immediately loaned the government back exactly the sum it had "contributed," and the Bank opened its doors having actually collected under 9% of its required private capital ($675,000 in hard cash against a $10 million charter).

Griffin makes a striking claim about the identity of the largest private investors: citing biographer Derek Wilson and historian Gustavus Myers, he states that the **Rothschild banking dynasty** was "the power behind the Bank of the United States," with Nathan Rothschild serving for a time as the official European banker for the U.S. government. **[Note:]** This claim originates entirely in Griffin's sourcing and is presented here as *his* documented assertion, not as independently established consensus history; readers building on this page should treat it as a claim requiring its own verification if used elsewhere.

### Consequences: Inflation and the "Wildcat" Coalition

The Bank's stated maximum lending rate (6%) made it impractical for anyone but the federal government and a handful of prime borrowers to use — its primary function, from the start, was creating money for Washington to spend. In its first five years, the government converted a $2 million "investment" into $8.2 million in borrowing, and wholesale prices rose 72% — meaning, per Griffin's calculation, that **42% of everything people had saved in money form was silently confiscated through inflation.**

By the time the charter came up for renewal, opposition had formed from two groups with opposite motives, in what Griffin calls "strange bedfellows": **Jeffersonian sound-money advocates**, who wanted a bank-free, gold-and-silver-only monetary system, and **frontier "wildcat" bankers and land speculators**, who wanted *no* central restraint at all on their own note-issuing. On **January 24, 1811**, recharter was defeated — by one vote in the House, and one vote (Vice President George Clinton's tie-breaker) in the Senate. The First Bank of the United States closed its doors.

### Aftermath: The War of 1812

With no central bank to moderate them, state-chartered "wildcat" banks proliferated. The unpopular War of 1812 — which Griffin argues pro-banking interests actively wanted for the profits it would generate, since New England (home to the impressed sailors cited as the war's cause) opposed the war while western/southern states (home to the wildcat banks) clamored for it — could not be funded through taxation alone. The government instead encouraged wildcat banks to buy war bonds and convert them into circulating notes. By August 1814, most of the nation's banks outside New England were unable to redeem their notes in specie; rather than let them fail, state and federal governments simply let them **suspend specie payment** — the same expedient the Bank of England had used in 1696 (see *[[The Bank of England]]*) — setting what Griffin calls "a precedent for all financial crises from then on." The national debt roughly tripled (from $45 million to $127 million) and the dollar lost roughly two-thirds of its purchasing power — on top of the 42% already lost to the First Bank's operations.

Jefferson, by then retired, never stopped writing against what he called "juggling tricks and banking dreams" that money "can be made out of nothing." Among his most quoted lines from this period:

> "The toleration of banks of paper discount costs the United States one-half of their war taxes; or, in other words, doubles the expenses of every war."
>
> "It is a wise rule never to borrow a dollar without laying a tax at the same instant for paying the interest annually and the principal within a given term... The earth belongs to the living, not the dead."
>
> "The modern theory of the perpetuation of debt has drenched the earth with blood, and crushed its inhabitants under burdens ever accumulating."

Congress did not listen.

## The Second Bank of the United States (1816–1836)

### Founding

In 1816, rather than let the free market clean up the wildcat-bank mess, Congress chartered a **Second Bank of the United States** — again for twenty years, again "a carbon copy of the old" (Griffin), with one addition: private investors paid Congress a $1.5 million up-front fee for the charter itself. Once again, roughly a third of the Bank's stock was foreign-held, leading Griffin to note it "was rooted as deeply in Britain as it was in America." Elliott, writing independently in 1902, records the same basic facts more tersely: "The first bank was incorporated in 1791... a charter granted for twenty years. When that charter expired by limitation in 1811, there was a struggle by the usurers to secure its renewal, but they were defeated... In 1816 they secured the charter of the second bank of the United States."

### America's First Boom-Bust Cycle

The Second Bank fueled an immediate postwar land speculation boom, then — fearing for its own solvency amid the fraud and over-expansion it had encouraged — abruptly reversed course starting in 1818, contracting credit and calling in loans nationwide. The result, per economist Murray Rothbard (as quoted by Griffin), was America's **first genuinely nationwide financial depression**: "a rash of defaults, bankruptcies of business and manufacturers, and a liquidation of unsound investments." Historian William Gouge's summary verdict: **"The Bank was saved, and the people were ruined."** Griffin frames this as the template for every subsequent "boom-bust cycle" attributed to central banking — expansion, encouraged over-extension, deliberate contraction, and a "panic" whose true cause (the central bank's own policy reversal) is rarely acknowledged at the time.

### McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

When Maryland tried to tax the Bank's local branch out of existence, the case reached the Supreme Court. Chief Justice **John Marshall** — a Federalist and strong-central-government advocate — ruled the Bank constitutional as a "necessary and proper" instrument of federal power, and struck down the state tax with the famous line: **"The power to tax is the power to destroy."** Griffin calls this "another end run around the Constitution, executed this time by the very men who were assumed to be its most loyal defenders" — noting the Court sidestepped the harder question of whether the Bank's fractional-reserve note creation was itself an unconstitutional "emission of bills of credit."

### Nicholas Biddle and the Road to War with Jackson

By the late 1820s the Bank was run by **Nicholas Biddle** — Griffin describes him as "wealthy, arrogant, ruthless, and brilliant," a University of Pennsylvania graduate at 13 who had fully mastered "the secret science of money." Asked by a Senate committee whether the Bank ever abused its power over state banks, Biddle answered: **"Never. There are very few banks which might not have been destroyed by an exertion of the powers of the Bank. None has ever been injured"** — an admission Jackson later seized on as proof that state banks survived only at Biddle's personal pleasure.

**Andrew Jackson** was elected President in 1828 on an explicitly anti-Bank platform, as part of the newly formed Democratic coalition (with Martin Van Buren) that had reorganized around Jeffersonian sound-money principles after the original Republican party abandoned them. In 1832, four years before the charter's natural expiration, Biddle asked Congress for early recharter — betting that Jackson, facing re-election, would flinch from a fight. He miscalculated.

### The Veto (1832)

Jackson's veto message — described by biographer Robert Remini as hitting "the nation like a tornado" — went far beyond the constitutional objection and made three arguments:

1. **Injustice**: Bank stock was concentrated among the wealthy and politically connected, effectively taxing ordinary Americans to subsidize an elite few — including foreigners. Jackson: "More than eight millions of the stock of this bank are held by foreigners... By this act the American Republic proposes virtually to make them a present of some millions of dollars."
2. **Unconstitutionality**: Jackson argued the President and Congress had equal authority to independently judge a law's constitutionality — he was not bound by *McCulloch v. Maryland*.
3. **National security**: Heavy foreign ownership made the Bank a potential instrument of foreign influence: "Controlling our currency, receiving our public monies, and holding thousands of our citizens in dependence, it would be more formidable and dangerous than a naval and military power of the enemy."

His closing, addressed past Congress to the voting public directly, became one of the most quoted passages in American political rhetoric:

> "It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes... when the laws undertake to add to these natural and just advantages artificial distinctions, to grant titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, to make the rich richer and the potent more powerful, the humble members of society — the farmers, mechanics, and laborers — who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves, have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government."

### Biddle's Congressional Leverage

Biddle's advantage, per Griffin, was that "Congress was in his pocket" — he routinely advanced funds to members facing pay delays and kept prominent figures on retainer. Even **Senator Daniel Webster**, a sound-money advocate, was simultaneously a Bank director and paid legal counsel, writing to Biddle: *"I believe my retainer has not been renewed or refreshed as usual... it may be well to send me the usual retainers."* Webster went on to argue the Bank's case before the Supreme Court in *McCulloch* and later delivered pro-recharter speeches the Bank itself printed and distributed by the hundreds of thousands. Congressman **John Randolph** summarized the mood of the House: "Every man you meet in this House or out of it, with some rare exceptions... is either a stockholder, president, cashier, clerk or doorkeeper, runner, engraver, paper-maker, or mechanic in some other way to a bank."

### The 1832 Election and Removal of Deposits

Jackson took his fight directly to voters — a genuine political innovation at the time — declaring war on "a moneyed aristocracy" and famously campaigning on the slogan **"Bank and no Jackson, or no bank and Jackson!"** He paid his travel expenses in gold coin as a visual rebuke of paper money. He won re-election with 55% of the popular vote and 80% of the electoral college.

Jackson moved immediately, ordering federal deposits withdrawn from the Bank and placed in state ("pet") banks. When Treasury Secretary William Duane refused the order on policy grounds, Jackson simply fired him — a constitutionally untested move, since the Constitution required Senate consent to *appoint* a cabinet officer but was silent on dismissal. "I have it chained," Jackson said of the Bank.

### Biddle's Retaliation and the "Den of Vipers"

Biddle counterattacked by deliberately contracting credit nationwide, calculating that the resulting economic pain would be blamed on Jackson and force Congress to override his veto. His own private correspondence is damning:

> "Nothing but widespread suffering will produce any effect on Congress... My own course is decided. All other banks and all the merchants may break, but the Bank of the United States shall not break."

The strategy worked at first: on **March 28, 1834**, the Senate formally **censured** Jackson by a vote of 26–20 — the first censure of a sitting President in American history. Jackson's fury produced the line that gives Griffin's chapter its title:

> **"You are a den of vipers,"** he told a delegation of the Bank's supporters. **"I intend to rout you out and by the Eternal God I will rout you out."**

### The Tide Turns

Biddle's undoing was largely his own ego — he could not resist publicly boasting about the deliberate contraction, and word spread. When Pennsylvania's own governor, George Wolf, publicly denounced both Biddle and the Bank, support collapsed almost overnight. On **April 4, 1834**, the House passed three resolutions: the Bank "ought not to be rechartered" (134–82), deposits "ought not to be restored" (118–103), and a special committee should investigate whether the Bank had deliberately engineered the crisis (175–42). The Senate's censure of Jackson was later formally rescinded. When Congress subpoenaed the Bank's books, Biddle flatly refused to comply and was never held in contempt — but the defiance finished him in public opinion.

### The End

The charter expired on schedule in 1836; Pennsylvania rechartered the institution as a state bank, which — after a speculative cotton binge, self-dealing loans to its own officers, and another suspension of specie payments — collapsed for good within five years. Biddle himself was arrested and charged with fraud; he was never convicted but was still fighting the civil litigation when he died. **On January 30, 1835**, in an unrelated but striking footnote, Jackson survived the first assassination attempt against a sitting U.S. President when both of assailant Richard Lawrence's pistols misfired.

### Griffin's Balanced Coda

Griffin does not let Jackson leave the story as an unqualified hero. He notes Jackson's anti-Bank coalition included frontier and business interests who wanted "no restraints at all" rather than sound money, and that they — not the hard-money Jeffersonians — ultimately got what they wanted once the federal bank was gone. More significantly, Griffin argues Jackson's *methods* — his direct appeal to voters ("The President is the direct representative of the people"), his willingness to defy the Supreme Court's constitutional judgment, and his precedent of a more forceful, personally popular presidency — permanently shifted the constitutional balance the framers had deliberately engineered (the electoral college was designed specifically to insulate presidential selection from direct popular passion). **[Note:]** This is presented as Griffin's own interpretive judgment about Jackson's legacy, not a settled historical consensus.

## Elliott's Independent Account (1902) — Points of Overlap and Difference

Writing 96 years before Griffin, from an entirely different (religious/moral rather than monetary-institutional) framework, Calvin Elliott's Chapter XXX ("Mammon Dominates the Nations") narrates the same core sequence of events far more briefly, but quotes several of the same founding-era figures Griffin would later cite independently:

> **J. C. Calhoun**: "Never was an engine invented better calculated to place the destines of the many in the hands of the few, or less favorable to that equality and independence which lies at the bottom of our free institutions."
>
> **Thomas H. Benton**: "I object to the continuance of this bank because its tendencies are dangerous and pernicious to the government and the people. It tends to aggravate the inequality of fortunes; to make the rich richer, and the poor poorer; to multiply nabobs and paupers, and to deepen and widen the gulf that separates Dives from Lazarus."
>
> **Thomas Jefferson**: "I sincerely believe that banking establishments are more dangerous than standing armies... to preserve their independence, we must not let our rulers load us with perpetual debts."
>
> **Andrew Jackson**: "Events have satisfied my mind, and I think the minds of the American people, that the mischief and dangers which flow from a national bank far overbalance all its advantages."

Elliott frames this entire episode as one installment in a repeating historical pattern he traces through the Bank of Venice and the Bank of England (see *[[The Bank of England]]* for the full parallel) — financiers exploiting a nation's moment of acute need (for Venice, the Crusades; for England, William III's war against France; for the young United States, wartime and postwar reconstruction financing) to secure permanent, state-guaranteed interest income, with a resulting concentration of political power that erodes republican self-governance.

**[Note — where the two accounts diverge:]** Griffin's telling is far more granular on institutional mechanics (fractional reserve ratios, specific vote counts, Biddle's private correspondence) and personalities (Hamilton, Willing, Biddle, Duane, Webster). Elliott's is more compressed but extends the story further chronologically — into the **Civil War National Banking Act fight** (see below), which Griffin covers in a different chapter of his own book. Read together, the two accounts corroborate each other on every point where they overlap, despite being written for entirely different purposes nearly a century apart.

## The Aftermath: Thirty Years Without a Central Bank, Then the Civil War

Both authors agree on what followed the Second Bank's demise: roughly **thirty years (1836–1861/63)** during which the United States had no central bank and no national public debt policy resembling the earlier pattern — a period both treat as a genuine, if imperfect, victory for the sound-money position. Elliott specifically notes "for thirty years there was no hint of a purpose to overthrow what was regarded as the settled policy of the nation."

That changed with the **Civil War**. Elliott's account (Ch. XXX) picks up the thread here: financiers offered to fund the Union war effort in exchange for concessions echoing the earlier banks' privileges — a perpetual, tax-exempt loan, interest paid in coin rather than paper legal tender, the right to issue their own currency, and government suppression of competing note-issuers. The measure passed the Senate by a narrow **23–21** vote, framed explicitly as a temporary wartime measure. Treasury Secretary Hugh McCulloch charged that financial resistance to the war-finance measures was itself "as decidedly manifested... as has been by the enemy." Congressman **Thaddeus Stevens**, reluctantly voting for the measure, captured the mood: *"I approach the subject with more depression of spirits than I ever before approached any question... I have a melancholy foreboding that we are about to consummate a cunningly devised scheme, which will carry great injury and great loss to all classes of people throughout the Union, except one."* This produced the **National Banking Act** system that structured American banking until the creation of the Federal Reserve in 1913 — see *[[The Federal Reserve System]]* for the continuation of this story.
