# Wesley Clark's "Seven Countries in Five Years" Account

> **Entry type:** Primary-testimony analysis / historical-political reference
> **Core subject:** Retired General Wesley Clark's repeated public account of a Pentagon conversation, weeks after 9/11, in which a staff officer showed him a memo describing a plan to "take out seven countries in five years" — and how that account fits into, corroborates, and is corroborated by the documented paper trail already covered in this knowledge base (the 1992 Defense Planning Guidance, *A Clean Break*, and PNAC's *Rebuilding America's Defenses*).
> **Related entries:** `clean-break-1996.md`, `pnac.md`, `greater-israel-project.md`, `zionism.md`. This entry sits downstream of the first two in particular — it is, in effect, the point where their documentary paper trail meets a single, repeatedly told, first-person eyewitness account from inside the Pentagon.
> **A note on method:** This is a slightly different kind of entry than its companions. Clean Break and *Rebuilding America's Defenses* are texts you can read. The "seven countries" memo is not a text anyone outside the Pentagon has ever produced, read in full, or independently confirmed to exist in the specific form Clark describes. What we have instead is a first-person account, told by a single credible, named, on-the-record source, repeated with substantial consistency (and some genuine variation) across at least five occasions between 2003 and 2007, situated against a documented sequence of actual American military action in the following two decades that maps onto it with real but imperfect precision. This entry treats Clark's account as serious eyewitness testimony worth taking seriously — his career, security clearances, and position make him a source most journalists would treat as credible on a matter like this — while being precise about exactly what is and is not independently verified, and about where Clark's own retellings differ from each other.

## 2. Clark's Account, Assembled From His Own Retellings

Because no single interview contains every detail Clark has given across different tellings, this section assembles the fullest composite account from his multiple retellings, flagging where they diverge (see §5 for the divergences themselves).

**The first Pentagon visit — approximately September 20–21, 2001.** Clark, by then retired from active duty and working as a CNN military analyst, describes visiting the Pentagon roughly ten days after the September 11 attacks. In the *Democracy Now!* telling, he states he saw Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld and Deputy Secretary Paul Wolfowitz, then went downstairs to see former colleagues on the Joint Staff. An officer called him in and told him, in Clark's account, "We've made the decision we're going to war with Iraq." Clark says he asked why, and the officer replied "I don't know" — adding, when Clark pressed further on whether new intelligence tied Saddam Hussein to al-Qaeda, "No, no... there's nothing new that way. They just made the decision to go to war with Iraq." The officer, per Clark, characterized the decision as arising from a felt need to demonstrate strength and capability against an amorphous terrorist threat that conventional military power could not directly address — the line Clark quotes as "I guess if the only tool you have is a hammer, every problem has to look like a nail."

**The second Pentagon visit — approximately six weeks later, during the Afghanistan campaign.** Clark returned to the Pentagon and asked the same officer whether the Iraq plan was still active. The officer's reply, in Clark's most widely quoted rendering: "Oh, it's worse than that." The officer then picked up a document from his desk and told Clark, "I just got this down from upstairs" — meaning, per Clark's gloss, the Secretary of Defense's office — "today." Clark quotes the officer describing the document as "a memo that describes how we're going to take out seven countries in five years, starting with Iraq, and then Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Somalia, Sudan and, finishing off, Iran." Clark says he asked whether it was classified; the officer said yes; Clark says he told the officer not to show it to him, and did not personally read the document.

**The later confirmation exchange.** Clark states that roughly "a year or so" before the *Democracy Now!* interview (i.e., circa 2005–06), he saw the same officer again and referenced the memo — at which point, per Clark, the officer denied ever having shown it to him: "Sir, I didn't show you that memo! I didn't show it to you!" Clark has consistently declined, across every telling reviewed for this entry, to name the officer.

## 3. The Documented Timeline of Clark's Public Retellings

This is worth laying out chronologically and precisely, because the memo's public life — how, when, and in what form Clark told this story — is itself a significant part of the historical record, and popular accounts frequently compress or garble the sequence.

**September 2003 — first public surfacing.** *Newsweek* published an excerpt of Clark's forthcoming book roughly a month before its official release; Al Jazeera's September 22, 2003 report on that excerpt appears to be among the earliest press coverage of the account, describing Clark's claim that a senior officer told him in November 2001 that the administration planned to move against Iraq first, then Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Iran, Somalia, and Sudan — notably, in this earliest published account, Iran appears fifth in the list rather than last, a genuine and rarely noted variation from the sequence in Clark's later, more famous tellings (see §5).

**October 2003 — *Winning Modern Wars*.** Clark's book, reviewed at the time by Slate's Fred Kaplan (a critical but not dismissive review — Kaplan called the book's first ninety pages "dull" but its later foreign-policy critique "as searing an indictment of George W. Bush's foreign policy as any tome out there"), gives the fullest early published version: "This was being discussed as part of a five-year campaign plan, he said, and there were a total of seven countries, beginning with Iraq, then Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Iran, Somalia, and Sudan... He said it with reproach — with disbelief, almost — at the breadth of the vision." Notably, this 2003 book version frames the officer's tone as one of alarm or disapproval at the plan's scope, a detail present in this telling but considerably less emphasized in Clark's later, more polished retellings.

**October 2006 — University of Alabama speech.** Referenced by Salon's 2007 coverage as an occasion on which Clark had "described this same conversation," though this entry has not independently located a full transcript of this specific speech and flags that gap.

**February 27, 2007 (broadcast March 2) — *Democracy Now!* with Amy Goodman.** The fullest, most consistently quoted version, reproduced in full in §2 above and cited by the great majority of secondary sources that reference Clark's account at all. This is also the version in which Clark most explicitly names the specific countries in the specific order — Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Somalia, Sudan, Iran — that has become the standard, most-repeated form of the claim across nearly all subsequent secondary discussion.

**October 2007 — CNN, *The Situation Room* with Wolf Blitzer.** Discussed in detail in §5 below; this is the interview in which Clark's own characterization of the document's evidentiary status shifts most noticeably.

**October 3, 2007 — Commonwealth Club of California, "A Time to Lead."** Delivered to promote Clark's second book of the same title, this speech gives the account its most explicit institutional and political framing, with Clark directly naming PNAC and specific individuals: "This country was taken over by a group of people with a 'policy coup'! Wolfowitz and Cheney and Rumsfeld, and... you can name a... dozen other collaborators from 'Project for a New American Century'— they wanted us to destabilize the Middle East, turn it upside down, make it under our control." In this same speech, Clark connects the 2001 memo backward to an account of his own 1991 conversation with Paul Wolfowitz (then the Pentagon's third-ranking civilian official) shortly after the Gulf War, in which — per Clark's account — Wolfowitz expressed regret that the U.S. had left Saddam Hussein in power after expelling Iraqi forces from Kuwait. This 1991 anecdote, worth flagging as analytically distinct from the 2001 memo claim (it is a separate recollection, from a different decade, involving a different specific individual), is frequently cited alongside the memo story as further evidence of long-standing, pre-9/11 neoconservative intent regarding Iraq — a claim that connects directly to this knowledge base's PNAC entry (§4, on the 1992 Defense Planning Guidance, which Wolfowitz co-authored, and §4 on the 1998 PNAC Iraq letters Wolfowitz signed).

## 4. Clark's Own Analytical Framework

It's worth documenting, separately from the memo anecdote itself, how Clark has explicitly and repeatedly connected it to the broader neoconservative policy network this knowledge base's companion entries document in detail. In the Commonwealth Club speech, Clark's own explanatory framework runs: a small, identifiable group of officials and allied intellectuals (he names Wolfowitz, Cheney, and Rumsfeld specifically, and gestures at "a dozen other collaborators from Project for a New American Century") had, in Clark's reading, effectively captured post-9/11 decision-making through what he calls a "policy coup" — a strong claim about the *process* by which the decision was made (bypassing normal interagency deliberation, congressional debate, and public discussion) rather than merely a claim about the decision's *content*. Clark explicitly ties this back to "those comments in 1991" (the Wolfowitz anecdote above), suggesting in his own framing that the 2001 memo represented the operational activation of intentions and planning that, in his account, had been present among specific individuals for a full decade before 9/11 provided the political opportunity to act on them — a claim that lines up closely, though Clark does not himself cite the specific documents by name in most tellings reviewed for this entry, with the documented trajectory this knowledge base's PNAC entry traces from the 1992 Defense Planning Guidance through the 1998 Iraq Liberation Act campaign to *Rebuilding America's Defenses* in 2000 (see PNAC entry, §3–§5).

## 5. Where Clark's Own Retellings Diverge — And What That Does and Doesn't Mean

A serious knowledge-base treatment of this material has to engage directly with the fact that Clark's own account is not perfectly stable across its various tellings, rather than smoothing this over in service of a cleaner narrative.

**The country order and Iran's position.** As noted in §3, the September 2003 Al Jazeera report on the Newsweek excerpt lists Iran fifth (Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Iran, Somalia, Sudan); the 2003 book version quoted by Slate's Kaplan gives the same order (Iran before Somalia and Sudan); but the 2007 *Democracy Now!* version and the version that has become by far the most widely quoted — "starting with Iraq, and then Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Somalia, Sudan and, finishing off, Iran" — moves Iran to the final position. This is a real, documentable variation, not an invention of skeptics: readers can verify it by comparing the Slate excerpt of the 2003 book text against the 2007 Democracy Now transcript directly. Whether this reflects ordinary memory drift across retellings of a conversation from years earlier, deliberate rhetorical restructuring (ending on Iran, the country Clark was most actively campaigning against in 2007 via his website stopiranwar.com, gives the 2007 tellings a sharper contemporary political point than the 2003 version had), or something else, this entry does not resolve — but flags clearly rather than silently reproducing only the more famous, "finishing off, Iran" version as though it were the sole or original form of the claim.

**The document's evidentiary status.** This is the more consequential divergence. In the *Democracy Now!* and Commonwealth Club tellings, Clark's language treats the document straightforwardly as "a memo" from "the Secretary of Defense's office," describing an actual plan. In the CNN *Situation Room* interview with Wolf Blitzer, however — per the Salon account of that interview, which this entry was not able to independently verify against a full transcript and flags accordingly as a secondary characterization pending direct verification — Clark reportedly "backed off slightly," describing the document in more hedged terms: it "wasn't [necessarily] a plan," Clark is quoted as saying. "Maybe it was a think piece. Maybe it was a sort of notional concept, but what it was, was the kind of indication of dialogue around this town in official circles... that has poisoned the atmosphere and made it very difficult for this administration to achieve any success in the region." This is a genuinely significant qualification, offered by Clark himself, and it deserves to be weighted accordingly: the same person who, in his most quoted tellings, describes a specific "memo... from the Secretary of Defense's office" outlining a five-year, seven-country plan has, on at least one occasion, characterized that same document in considerably softer terms — a "think piece" or "notional concept" reflecting Beltway "dialogue" rather than a formal, adopted operational plan. Any treatment of this material that cites only the harder, more dramatic formulation without acknowledging Clark's own softer alternative characterization is incomplete.

**What was and wasn't independently corroborated.** No other named, on-the-record source — military, civilian, or journalistic — has come forward to confirm having seen the specific document Clark describes, and no copy of it (or an official acknowledgment of its prior existence, declassified or otherwise) has surfaced through FOIA requests, congressional investigation, leaked-document releases (including the large WikiLeaks and other document troves of the following decade), or journalistic investigation that this entry's research was able to identify. This absence of independent documentary corroboration is a real and important limitation on the claim's evidentiary weight, and should be stated plainly rather than minimized by proponents of the strong version of the story — while also being weighed against the fact that Clark is a named, credentialed, on-the-record source repeating a consistent core account across multiple occasions over several years, which is a meaningfully different evidentiary situation than an anonymous or pseudonymous claim, or a single one-off assertion never repeated or elaborated.

**Fred Kaplan's contemporaneous questions.** Slate's Kaplan, reviewing the 2003 book at the time of its release — writing skeptically but not dismissively, and clearly sympathetic to much of the book's broader critique of Bush administration Iraq policy — raised a set of pointed questions about the anecdote specifically that remain, per this entry's research, essentially unanswered in any of Clark's later retellings: who, specifically, was this senior staff officer, and if his identity can't be revealed, how senior was he actually — a full-bird colonel, a one-star, a much more senior figure? Why did Clark, by his own account, actively "move the conversation away" rather than pursue the matter further in the moment? And why, if Clark found the substance of what he'd been told this alarming, did he not raise it in any of his extensive wartime CNN commentary during the actual run-up to and prosecution of the Iraq War in 2002–03, waiting instead until his book's publication after the invasion had already occurred? These are fair, serious questions from a critic broadly sympathetic to Clark's overall political position on the war, not a hostile partisan dismissal, and this entry flags that no fully satisfying public answer to them has been identified in the sourced material reviewed here.

## 6. The Documented Sequence of Actual U.S. and Allied Military Action, 2001–2026

Whatever one concludes about the specific memo's existence, form, and evidentiary weight, it's worth laying out plainly and separately what actually happened, in documented historical fact, to each of the seven named countries in the decades following Clark's account — distinguishing throughout between direct U.S. military action, U.S.-supported or U.S.-adjacent action by other parties, and developments with no clear U.S. hand at all, since conflating these categories is one of the most common moves made by both over-claiming and dismissive treatments of Clark's account.

- **Iraq (invaded 2003):** Direct, large-scale, sustained U.S. invasion and multi-year occupation — the clearest and least disputed match to the account, covered extensively in this knowledge base's Clean Break and PNAC entries.
- **Syria:** No direct U.S. ground invasion or formal declared war occurred, but the U.S. provided arms, training, and support to select Syrian opposition factions during the 2011–24 civil war (a conflict whose origins, as the Greater Israel entry notes at §10, were not caused by any single external plan but whose *trajectory* was shaped by extensive external intervention from multiple parties including the U.S., Turkey, Iran, Russia, and Israel), conducted direct airstrikes against ISIS and other targets inside Syrian territory from 2014 onward, and maintained a small but sustained U.S. troop presence in northeastern Syria for years. The Assad regime's eventual December 2024 collapse — detailed in the Clean Break entry, §10 — involved no direct U.S. military action in the collapse itself, complicating any simple "the U.S. took out Syria" reading, even as the broader post-2011 trajectory involved substantial and sustained U.S. involvement.
- **Lebanon:** No direct U.S. invasion or bombing campaign against the Lebanese state occurred in this period. U.S. involvement has been indirect — sanctions and financial pressure targeting Hezbollah, intelligence and special-operations cooperation with the Lebanese state security apparatus, and (as the Clean Break entry notes) covert Saudi-U.S. coordination reported by Seymour Hersh in 2007 aimed at weakening Iranian-aligned Shia influence in Lebanon — alongside extensive direct Israeli military action against Hezbollah and Lebanese territory across multiple periods, most intensively in 2006 and again during the 2023–24 regional war following October 7. This is among the weakest direct matches to a claim of American "taking out" the Lebanese government specifically, though the broader pattern of sustained external pressure on Lebanon's Iranian-aligned political-military faction is real and documented.
- **Libya:** The clearest post-Iraq match: a 2011 NATO-led air campaign, with the United States as a leading participant (though the Obama administration notably sought to characterize U.S. involvement as supporting a broader coalition rather than leading it — the much-discussed "leading from behind" framing), directly contributed to the overthrow and killing of Muammar Gaddafi, followed by years of subsequent state collapse and civil conflict — a genuinely close match to a "take out" outcome, achieved through a different specific instrument (air power and coalition support for local rebels, rather than ground invasion) than the Iraq precedent.
- **Somalia:** No formal U.S. invasion or declared regime-change campaign, but a sustained multi-decade pattern of U.S. drone strikes, special-operations raids, and support for African Union and Somali government forces against al-Shabaab, dating back to (and predating, in the case of the 1990s Black Hawk Down-era U.S. involvement) the period Clark's account covers, and continuing with strikes documented into the mid-2020s. Somalia's chronic state weakness and internal conflict long predate 2001 and have many causes with no connection to any American plan; U.S. involvement here is real, sustained, and documented, but the country's instability is much more clearly overdetermined by pre-existing internal factors than in the Iraq or Libya cases.
- **Sudan:** No direct U.S. invasion occurred. The U.S. did conduct a single cruise-missile strike on a Khartoum pharmaceutical factory in 1998 (the al-Shifa strike, under the Clinton administration, predating and analytically separate from Clark's 2001 account) following the U.S. embassy bombings in Kenya and Tanzania, and has applied sustained sanctions, diplomatic pressure, and (particularly regarding the Darfur conflict of the 2000s and the 2023–ongoing civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces) diplomatic and humanitarian engagement without direct military intervention. Sudan's post-2001 turmoil (Darfur, South Sudan's 2011 secession, the 2019 revolution against Omar al-Bashir, and the ongoing 2023 civil war) has multiple deep internal and regional causes considerably predating and independent of any American plan, making this among the weaker direct matches to Clark's account.
- **Iran:** No direct U.S. ground invasion occurred through the period covered by this entry, but — as documented extensively in the Clean Break entry's §10 — direct Israeli and, at two distinct points, joint U.S.-Israeli air campaigns did occur: the June 2025 Twelve-Day War (Israeli-led, with direct U.S. strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities) and the February–May 2026 war (a joint U.S.-Israeli operation that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and pursued explicitly stated regime-change objectives, per President Trump's own public statements at the time). Whatever one concludes about causal continuity with a 2001 memo specifically, Iran has, as of this entry's writing, been the target of exactly the kind of large-scale, regime-change-oriented U.S. and allied military action Clark's account describes as the plan's intended final stage — a genuinely striking, if temporally very distant, correspondence between the 2001 account and 2025–26 events that this entry flags without asserting a proven causal chain between the specific memo and the specific later wars, consistent with the causal-weight cautions this knowledge base has applied throughout its companion entries to similar claims about Clean Break and PNAC.

**A necessary caution about this section.** Assembling this list risks creating a false impression of tidy, complete confirmation. It is entirely possible — and, per the causal-weight cautions applied throughout this knowledge base's companion entries, arguably the more historically responsible reading — that a wide range of independent U.S. strategic interests (oil and energy security, counterterrorism operations against genuinely transnational threats like al-Shabaab and ISIS, longstanding and separately motivated hostility to the Iranian regime dating to 1979, alliance commitments to Israel and Gulf partners, and simple bureaucratic momentum within a permanent national-security establishment) would have produced substantial U.S. military involvement with most or all of these seven countries across a twenty-five-year period *regardless* of whether any specific 2001 Pentagon memo existed in the form Clark describes. The documented outcomes are real; whether they confirm a single coordinated plan conceived in 2001, or simply reflect the ordinary operation of pre-existing, separately motivated American strategic interests that a hypothetical memo-writer in 2001 might have been synthesizing rather than originating, is precisely the kind of causal-weight question this knowledge base has flagged as genuinely unresolved in its treatment of Clean Break and PNAC, and the same caution applies here with at least equal force, given that this entry's central evidentiary anchor is a single, not-fully-corroborated eyewitness account rather than a documented primary text.

## 7. How Other Writers in This Knowledge Base's Tradition Have Used Clark's Account

**Michel Chossudovsky and Global Research.** As flagged in the Greater Israel entry (§9) regarding this same source cluster, Chossudovsky's Global Research network has been among the most prominent and earliest popularizers of Clark's account in written form, reproducing the full Democracy Now transcript repeatedly since 2007 and explicitly integrating it into the network's broader "Greater Israel Project" framework — the January 2019 Global Research piece cited in this entry's research explicitly glosses Clark's account as confirming that "these seven countries have directly or indirectly been the object of US aggression" as part of a design serving what the piece calls Israel's "diabolical objective" of controlling the region. Consistent with this knowledge base's standing practice (see Clean Break entry §6.5 and Greater Israel entry §9), this entry flags that this specific framing — folding Clark's account directly into the maximalist "Greater Israel Project" thesis — goes well beyond what Clark's own testimony, taken on its own terms, actually claims; Clark's own account and analytical framework (§4 above) attribute the plan to a specific American neoconservative policy network via PNAC, not to an Israeli-directed project, and conflating the two is an interpretive move the Global Research material makes that Clark himself has not.

**Scott Horton.** Consistent with the treatment of Horton's work throughout this knowledge base's companion entries, Horton's writing and broadcasting on the Iraq War's origins (see the Clean Break and PNAC entries' §8 sections) integrates Clark's account as one data point among the fuller, more textually disciplined documentary record he assembles from PNAC's own letters, the Office of Special Plans, and named personnel — treating it as corroborating and consistent with, rather than as the primary evidentiary foundation for, his broader argument about a specific, identifiable neoconservative network's sustained pursuit of regional regime change. This entry was not able to locate an instance of Horton citing the Clark memo account as a standalone centerpiece of his own reporting in the sourced material reviewed, suggesting he treats it, appropriately, as one corroborating anecdote within a much larger documented paper trail rather than as load-bearing evidence in its own right.

**Gareth Porter and Seymour Hersh.** This entry was not able to locate a specific instance of investigative journalist Gareth Porter directly addressing Clark's memo account in the sourced material reviewed, and flags that as a gap for future research (see §8) rather than asserting a position on his behalf. Seymour Hersh's own reporting is directly relevant to this entry's subject matter in a different, more specific way: Hersh's 2007 *New Yorker* reporting — discussed directly in the same *Democracy Now!* interview in which Clark gives his most-quoted memo account, where Amy Goodman asks Clark to respond to it — documented a Pentagon "special planning group" within the Joint Chiefs of Staff office specifically tasked with preparing options for a bombing campaign against Iran, and separately documented reported covert U.S.-Saudi coordination aimed at weakening Iranian-aligned Shia influence in Lebanon and Syria. Clark's own response to being asked about the Hersh reporting in that same interview was notably measured rather than confirmatory: "I don't have any direct information to confirm it or deny it. It's certainly plausible" — worth noting as an instance of Clark himself declining to overstate his own certainty when discussing a related but distinct claim he did not have direct personal knowledge of, a useful data point for calibrating how much evidentiary weight Clark himself seems to place on secondhand or unconfirmed reporting generally.

**Skeptical and mainstream treatments.** Beyond Kaplan's 2003 Slate review (§5), this entry's research did not identify a rigorous, dedicated fact-checking treatment of the memo claim specifically from a major fact-checking organization (PolitiFact's archive of Clark-related fact checks, reviewed for this entry, did not appear to include a dedicated assessment of this specific claim). This represents a genuine gap in the documented record — a claim this widely repeated across nearly two decades of political commentary has received surprisingly little sustained, methodical, independent journalistic verification effort specifically targeted at establishing or disproving the memo's existence, as opposed to the considerably more extensive journalism (including Hersh's own separate reporting) documenting the broader pattern of neoconservative regime-change advocacy the memo, if real, would have reflected.

## 8. Open Questions for Further Research

1. **A full transcript or recording of the October 2006 University of Alabama speech** would help establish whether Clark's account had stabilized into something close to its now-standard form by that point, or whether it was still evolving between the 2003 book version and the 2007 Democracy Now version; this entry was unable to locate one.
2. **A full, verified transcript of the CNN Wolf Blitzer interview** (rather than the Salon article's characterization of it, relied on in §5) would allow direct quotation and more precise analysis of exactly how far Clark walked back the document's evidentiary status in that specific venue, and in what words.
3. **Whether any subsequent journalist, historian, or congressional investigation ever attempted to identify the "senior officer"** Clark describes, through Freedom of Information Act requests, interviews with plausible Joint Staff personnel from the relevant period, or other investigative means, is a genuine and significant gap in this entry's research; this would be the single most valuable line of further inquiry for anyone seeking to move this account from "credible, repeated, uncorroborated testimony" to "independently verified historical fact."
4. **A systematic comparison of Clark's account against the actual documented content of Rebuilding America's Defenses and the 1992 Defense Planning Guidance** — checking whether either document's actual text supports a literal "seven countries, five years" formulation, as opposed to the broader primacy and Gulf-presence framework this knowledge base's PNAC entry documents — would help establish more precisely whether Clark's memo, if real, represented a novel synthesis specific to the Rumsfeld Pentagon's post-9/11 planning, or a more direct textual descendant of the earlier PNAC corpus.
5. **Gareth Porter's specific published views on this claim**, if they exist, were not located in this entry's research and would be a valuable addition given his standing as an investigative journalist with a substantial record of Pentagon and intelligence-community sourcing on adjacent Iraq War-origins questions.

Where this account has been folded into larger interpretive frameworks — particularly the Global Research network's "Greater Israel Project" thesis — this entry flags that the larger framework's claims exceed what Clark's own testimony, taken on its own terms, actually asserts.*
